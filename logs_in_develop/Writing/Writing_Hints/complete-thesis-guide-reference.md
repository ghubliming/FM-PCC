## General foundations

### Why write a thesis?

A thesis is formally an assessed requirement for obtaining a degree, but the guide argues that it should not be viewed as merely another exam. Conventional exams are designed to make assessment manageable; a thesis is better suited to sustained learning through independent work on a substantial problem.

Use the project to practise abilities that matter beyond the degree: framing a problem, locating and judging evidence, building or applying a method, making defensible decisions, communicating results, and responding to feedback. The final document is important, but the learning process and research judgement developed along the way are central outcomes.

### What makes a thesis good?

There is no single recipe such as “implement a system,” “prove a theorem,” “write a literature review,” or “run an experiment.” A good thesis uses the type and amount of work needed to produce a credible answer to its problem. The necessary evidence depends on the discipline and research question.

Before committing to methods or chapters, clarify the following with the supervisor:

- What is the concrete problem or knowledge gap?
- What claim, answer, artefact, or insight should the thesis deliver?
- What counts as convincing evidence: theory, implementation, empirical data, comparison, user study, literature analysis, or a combination?
- What are the most important quality criteria: novelty, rigor, correctness, usefulness, reproducibility, clarity, or completeness?
- What is deliberately outside the scope?

A practical quality test is whether a knowledgeable reader can identify the problem, understand the approach, inspect the evidence, and decide whether the conclusion follows. Length, complexity, or the number of features are not reliable substitutes for this logic.

### Thesis project phases

The guide describes the thesis as a project with distinct but connected phases:

1. **General orientation:** Understand the purpose, quality expectations, and overall process.
2. **Initiation:** Find a topic, identify supervisors, set expectations, and establish a viable start.
3. **Execution:** Research, design, implement or investigate, analyze, write, revise, and manage time.
4. **Presentation:** Create and rehearse the final explanation and defense of the work.
5. **Follow-up:** Consider publication or further use of valuable results.

These phases overlap. You should develop an outline and record writing material during execution rather than waiting until research appears “finished,” and you should consider the presentation narrative while deciding what result the thesis needs to make clear.

### Typical pitfalls

The site includes a collection of thesis “anti-patterns”: common problems paired with possible remedies. The key lesson is to look for predictable failure modes at the appropriate stage—unclear expectations at the beginning, loss of focus or schedule control during the work, and insufficient revision or rehearsal at the end.

Regularly review scope, supervisor alignment, progress toward a central result, and remaining time. A problem is usually less costly to fix when discovered early.

## Initiating the thesis

### Find topics beyond listings

The guide calls advertised openings on faculty websites the **visible thesis-topic market**. It also argues that a **hidden market** exists: possible projects that are not currently posted but can emerge through conversations with researchers and potential supervisors.

Do not limit yourself to topic lists. Contact doctoral researchers, postdoctoral researchers, lecturers, professors, and research groups relevant to your interests. Ask about ongoing work, unexplored questions, available data or infrastructure, potential collaborations, and whether a thesis project could be shaped around a shared area of interest.

A person who teaches a subject is not necessarily the ideal supervisor, but may know who conducts research in that area. Networking therefore improves both topic discovery and the chance of finding a supervisor with relevant capacity.

### Avoid “topic roulette”

Choosing the title that sounds best from an online list is risky because project descriptions rarely communicate everything that determines thesis quality and fit. They may omit the real scope, prerequisite knowledge, expected independence, available support, data access, uncertainty, technical constraints, and the supervisor’s working style.

Before selecting a topic, have a conversation that lets you assess the work behind the title. Useful questions include:

- What research question or practical problem motivates this topic?
- What would a successful thesis deliver?
- Which methods, tools, datasets, or skills are required?
- Which parts are well understood and which are genuinely uncertain?
- What prior knowledge is expected and what can be learned during the project?
- Who will provide day-to-day feedback?
- What risks could make the original plan infeasible?

Choose on informed fit, not on the attractiveness of a short description.

### Secure effective supervision

The guide identifies supervisor time and dedication as the dominant influence on how useful supervision feels. Expertise and reputation matter, but an expert who has little time to engage cannot provide timely direction or feedback.

Discuss supervision early and concretely:

- Meeting frequency and how meetings are arranged
- Expected preparation before meetings
- Feedback channels and typical turnaround time
- What drafts or deliverables will be reviewed
- How decisions about scope or method are made
- What to do when the project becomes blocked or delayed
- Who is the primary contact if multiple people are involved

For an external or company thesis, align the university supervisor and company supervisor together. Make responsibilities, confidentiality limits, intellectual-property constraints, desired outcomes, assessment expectations, and possible conflicts explicit. The student should not become the sole messenger between two unaligned supervisors.

### Clarify responsibilities

The **IAuthor and ISupervisor** framing treats the student and supervisor as two roles whose interface needs definition. The author is responsible for carrying out the work, making progress, preparing material, and producing the thesis. The supervisor is responsible for guidance, feedback, and helping keep the work viable—but not for doing the thesis for the student.

Misunderstandings arise when either side assumes unspoken obligations. Turn implicit expectations into explicit agreements at the start and revisit them if the project changes.

### Learn from disappointment patterns

The “Recipe for Disappointment” post reverses the advice for good supervision. Its underlying warning is that poor outcomes become likely when students choose without investigating the topic or supervisor, fail to establish communication, wait passively for direction, or defer difficult conversations until deadlines make change hard.

Act early: investigate, agree on a working relationship, communicate obstacles, and actively seek feedback. These behaviours improve the probability that supervision capacity turns into useful support.

## Executing the thesis

### Allocate writing resources

A thesis contains different types of chapters—such as introduction, foundations, related work, method or solution, evaluation, discussion, and conclusion. The guide frames time and page space as scarce writing resources that must be allocated according to what is central to the contribution.

Spend the most care on material that establishes the thesis’s original result: the research method, design or solution, empirical evidence, analysis, interpretation, and limitations. Do not consume disproportionate time expanding definitions, background, or technical details that do not help the reader judge the main claim.

For each chapter or section, ask:

- Does it help answer the research question?
- Does it supply evidence for the core contribution?
- Does it enable a reader to understand or evaluate the main result?
- Is this information better placed in an appendix, cited source, or omitted altogether?

### Track writing progress

The guide presents the writing schedule as the primary protection against discovering too late that progress is insufficient. A schedule should show whether the project is moving at an adequate rate while there is still time to change scope, priorities, or working habits.

Plan deliverables, not vague intentions. Possible milestones include:

| Milestone | Evidence of completion |
|---|---|
| Topic and objective agreed | A brief written problem statement, scope, and expected outcome |
| Initial literature map | Key sources grouped by role and relevance |
| Outline validated | Supervisor feedback on a chapter-level architecture |
| Method or solution defined | A concrete plan that can be executed and evaluated |
| Data, implementation, or study completed | Materials and records sufficient for analysis |
| Results drafted | Findings presented with necessary context and evidence |
| Full draft completed | A coherent document ready for substantive review |
| Revision complete | Feedback addressed and formal requirements checked |
| Presentation rehearsed | A timed, end-to-end practice run and revisions |

Update the plan using actual progress. If a milestone slips, decide deliberately whether to reduce scope, change approach, reallocate time, or seek help; do not merely shift every deadline forward.

### Write at the right time

Your knowledge of individual chapters increases as you read, develop, and experiment, but may fade after you move to another task. Writing a polished chapter too early can create major rework as the project changes. Writing it too late may require rediscovering information and decisions you no longer remember well.

Use staged writing:

- Write durable factual material early, such as research notes, source annotations, setup details, decision rationales, protocol descriptions, and intermediate observations.
- Maintain a provisional outline and short chapter notes while the structure evolves.
- Draft interpretation and synthesis when the evidence is sufficiently stable.
- Reserve a final pass for coherence, wording, transitions, and consistency across the whole document.

This approach preserves information without pretending that early conclusions are final.

### Design the thesis architecture

The guide compares the outline to software architecture. Chapters are components and references are dependencies; a weak architecture causes costly restructuring later because text is difficult to refactor.

Reduce that risk by starting with a standard architecture and validating it early with the supervisor. A typical adaptable flow is:

1. **Introduction:** Context, problem, objective or question, contribution, and document roadmap.
2. **Background / related work:** Concepts and prior work needed to understand the gap and approach.
3. **Method / solution:** What was done, why it was done, and enough detail to assess or reproduce it.
4. **Evaluation / results:** Evidence gathered and results obtained.
5. **Discussion:** Meaning of the results, limitations, validity concerns, and implications.
6. **Conclusion:** Direct answer to the research question, contribution, limitations, and possible future work.

Use the outline as a prototype. Test whether the narrative leads naturally from problem to answer, whether every promised contribution is supported by evidence, and whether chapters depend on information introduced at the right point.

### Choose tools to reduce waste

The guide argues that writing-tool selection is less critical than it can appear: the limiting factor in thesis work is often thinking and research progress, not software capability. The goal is therefore to avoid waste, not to find a perfect tool.

Choose a stable workflow for writing, references, file organization, backups, and—when appropriate—version control. Prefer tools that meet local formatting requirements and that you can use consistently. Avoid switching systems late or spending excessive time on configuration, typography, or automation that does not improve the research or explanation.

### Write a case study

The case-study guidance is aimed especially at empirical software-engineering work, while its broader message applies to empirical research generally: readers expect a recognizable structure that makes the study easy to assess.

A strong case-study section should make explicit:

1. **Purpose and research questions:** What is being investigated and why?
2. **Context:** What system, participants, organisation, dataset, environment, or setting is involved?
3. **Study design:** What was measured, compared, observed, or manipulated?
4. **Procedure:** How were materials collected and how was the work conducted?
5. **Analysis:** How were observations turned into results?
6. **Results:** What was found, with relevant quantitative or qualitative evidence?
7. **Interpretation:** What do the findings mean for the research question?
8. **Threats to validity and limitations:** What could weaken, bias, constrain, or limit generalisation of the result?

Do not hide mixed or negative outcomes. A study is credible when its design, evidence, and limitations allow readers to judge what can and cannot be concluded.

## Presenting the thesis

### Draft the presentation

The presentation should deliver the big picture before technical detail. An audience does not yet care about algorithmic details, visual effects, or performance figures until it understands the problem, purpose, and the role those details play in the story.

Build the narrative first. A useful order is:

1. Problem and context
2. Why the problem matters
3. Research question or objective
4. Approach or method
5. Key evidence or evaluation
6. Main result
7. Interpretation, contribution, and takeaway

Only then decide which technical details, examples, diagrams, or demonstrations make the narrative clearer. A visually polished slide deck cannot repair a weak structure.

### Rehearse the presentation

The guide compares new slides to a program that has never been executed: they likely contain bugs. Rehearsal is testing that reveals unclear transitions, timing problems, unreadable visuals, missing explanations, and confusing detail before the audience sees them.

Rehearse repeatedly and treat each run as a source of fixes. Include at least one full timed rehearsal under conditions similar to the actual presentation. Ask someone unfamiliar with the work to identify where the story becomes unclear, and revise slides or wording based on that feedback.

The author’s observation across many attended presentations is that rehearsal and preparation are stronger indicators of quality than a presenter’s previous speaking experience. The practical consequence is simple: schedule rehearsal as a required project task rather than an optional final-hour activity.

## After submission

### Convert a thesis into a paper

The Follow-up content describes an example of extracting a conference paper from a bachelor’s thesis and presenting it at Software Quality Days 2016. The general lesson is that a thesis can be a basis for publication, but it needs reframing for a new venue and audience.

A paper is not usually just the thesis with pages removed. Select the strongest contribution, clarify the audience and venue requirements, compress supporting material, sharpen the claim, and revise the narrative around the paper’s limited space and purpose. Publication also brings new tasks, such as preparing a conference presentation and learning venue-specific expectations.

## FAQ and local rules

The site FAQ provides recurring practical advice. Several answers are contextualised for TUM, so treat them as examples rather than universal rules.

| Question | Practical guidance |
|---|---|
| I do not have a topic yet | Search both listed and unlisted opportunities, talk to potential supervisors, and assess real scope before committing |
| Who can supervise me? | Eligibility rules are institution-specific; check your programme and chair requirements |
| How long should the thesis be? | No universal page count is reliable; follow local norms and supervisor guidance, focusing on relevant content |
| My approach did not work | An unsuccessful approach can still yield a valid contribution if it was investigated rigorously and its limits are evidenced |
| I am running out of time | Discuss this early; narrowing the scope may be more practical than seeking an extension, which is governed by local procedures |
| How early should I begin? | Start topic and supervision conversations well before the intended start; external or exchange arrangements often require more lead time |

Always verify submission deadlines, extensions, formatting, examination regulations, authorship expectations, ethics approval, confidentiality, data protection, and plagiarism rules through official departmental and university sources.

## Working templates

### One-page project agreement

```text
Working title:
Student:
University supervisor:
Additional / company supervisor:

Problem or knowledge gap:
Research question / objective:
Expected contribution:

In scope:
Out of scope:

Main method or approach:
Expected evidence / evaluation:

Milestones:
Feedback schedule:
File, data, and backup workflow:
Known risks and contingency plan:
```

### Weekly progress review

```text
This week’s completed deliverable:
Evidence / output produced:
What changed in the problem, scope, or assumptions:
Current blocker or key risk:
Next week’s concrete deliverable:
Question for supervisor:
```

### Thesis quality review

```text
[ ] The research question is specific and consistently answered
[ ] The contribution is visible in the introduction and conclusion
[ ] The method is sufficiently described and justified
[ ] The evidence supports the stated claims
[ ] Results and interpretation are clearly separated where needed
[ ] Limitations and validity threats are reported honestly
[ ] Background and related work serve the central argument
[ ] The chapter structure leads logically from problem to conclusion
[ ] References, figures, terminology, and formatting are consistent
[ ] Local university requirements have been checked
```
