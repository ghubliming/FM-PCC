% ABSTRACT -- v1 sketch. Rewrite last, once Results is settled.

\ifstandalone
Predictive control with a generative planner has to pay for its samples. Diffusion
Predictive Control couples a denoising planner to a per-step projection that enforces
dynamics and safety constraints, and inherits diffusion's step budget: the number of
network evaluations is fixed at training time and cannot be reduced afterwards. This
thesis replaces that engine with deterministic ODE transport --- flow matching and its
few-step variants --- while holding the backbone, the projector, the data and the
evaluation harness fixed, so that the step budget becomes an inference-time choice.

Across a state-based manipulator benchmark, a vision-conditioned variant of it, and an
aerial embodiment built for this work, the resulting system is evaluated against the
diffusion baseline under a strict definition of improvement: Pareto dominance at equal
success and equal constraint satisfaction. \hole{Headline numbers -- write last, after
Results is settled and the powered runs land.}

The thesis makes three further contributions beyond the engine swap. First, a
correctness condition for evaluating in-loop guided samplers: a step budget below a
computable threshold reduces the guided sampler to sample-then-project, so that a
substantial class of published-style comparisons measures nothing. Second, a measured
regime split between the two constraint-enforcement arms, with a mechanism that explains
it: projecting a predicted clean endpoint is cheaper per solve than projecting an
off-manifold iterate, and the advantage appears exactly where the guided sampler becomes
well-defined. Third, negative results are retained rather than discarded --- an
additional engine that does not improve on its predecessor, and a benchmark defect
diagnosed by this work's own instrumentation.
\fi

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:intro:motivation}

A robot that plans with a generative model buys expressiveness with computation. Diffusion
planners represent multi-modal demonstration data well and, when paired with a constrained
projection step, can respect dynamics and safety at deployment. The price is the sampling
loop: producing one plan costs tens to hundreds of network evaluations, and in a
receding-horizon controller that cost is paid again at every timestep.

The section argues that this cost is not incidental but \emph{structural}, and that the
structure is what makes it addressable. In a diffusion model the number of steps is a
property of the trained noise schedule; reducing it after training degrades the sample.
In a deterministic transport model the same quantity is a property of the ODE solver, and
is chosen at inference. The motivation of this thesis is therefore not ``make the planner
faster'' but ``move the step budget from training time to inference time, and then find out
what it costs.''

A second motivation follows from the first. Once the budget is cheap, the constraint
machinery --- not the generative model --- becomes the dominant cost, and the question of
\emph{how} constraints are enforced stops being a footnote.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:intro:problem}

The object of study is a closed-loop controller in which, at each timestep, a conditional
generative model proposes a trajectory over a fixed horizon, a constraint mechanism makes
that trajectory admissible, and the first action is executed. The thesis holds this
architecture fixed and varies exactly two things: the generative engine, and the
constraint mechanism.

Stating it this way exposes the methodological demand the rest of the thesis has to meet.
A claim that one engine beats another is only meaningful if the backbone, parameter count,
data, normalisation, horizon, seeds, projector and harness are all held constant. A claim
that one constraint arm beats another is only meaningful if both arms see the same feasible
set, the same solver, the same activation threshold and the same candidate budget. Most of
the engineering behind this thesis exists to make those two sentences true, and the section
says so plainly rather than leaving it to the reader to infer.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:intro:rqs}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 --- Engine.] Under an identical backbone, projector, dataset and harness, does
    replacing the diffusion engine with deterministic ODE transport improve the
    quality--cost frontier of constrained predictive control, and does the improvement
    order the engine family?
  \item[RQ2 --- Budget.] How far can the number of function evaluations be reduced before
    plan quality degrades, and what mechanism explains why the flow family can follow that
    ladder down while diffusion cannot?
  \item[RQ3 --- Constraints.] Given one fixed feasible set, which constraint-enforcement
    arm --- per-step projection of the iterate, or in-loop trajectory optimisation on a
    predicted endpoint --- should be used, and under what condition is the comparison even
    well-posed?
  \item[RQ4 --- Transfer.] Do the answers to RQ1--RQ3 survive a change of observation
    modality (state $\rightarrow$ vision) and a change of embodiment (manipulator
    $\rightarrow$ aerial), or are they properties of one benchmark?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro:contributions}

The section lists six contributions and is explicit that two of them are negative or
methodological rather than performance claims.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An architecture-matched engine substitution.} Deterministic transport
    engines are placed inside the DPCC control stack with the backbone, parameter count and
    constraint machinery held fixed, so that the engine is the only moving part. The
    resulting comparison is reported under Pareto dominance rather than a single metric.
  \item \textbf{A correctness condition for evaluating in-loop guided samplers.} A guided
    sampler that is only active near the end of its own integration performs no guidance at
    all when the step budget is small: the sole active step is terminal, and what executes is
    sample-then-project. The condition is a computable function of the step budget and the
    activation threshold, and it disqualifies a class of comparisons that otherwise look
    valid. This thesis derives it, instruments it, and enforces it in every table.
  \item \textbf{A measured regime split between constraint arms, with a mechanism.} The two
    arms are shown to swap places as a function of the same quantity, and the swap is
    explained by where in the transport each arm poses its optimisation problem, not by
    solver differences.
  \item \textbf{Removal of the candidate-selection machinery.} The projector's selection
    rules are shown not to earn their computation, and the system ships with the redundancy
    removed rather than retained ``just in case''.
  \item \textbf{Two constructed benchmark environments} --- a vision-conditioned
    manipulation task and an aerial task with four scenes, expert data, constraint geometry
    and a low-level controller --- together with their construction documented as
    methodology.
  \item \textbf{Retained negative results.} An additional engine variant that does not
    improve on its predecessor is reported as a clean negative rather than omitted, and a
    defect in this work's own benchmark geometry, found by its own instrumentation, is
    reported with the correction and its limits.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Terminology}\label{sec:intro:scope}

A short section, placed before the background because the rest of the thesis is unreadable
without it. It fixes the vocabulary that recurs in every chapter:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Step budget $K$ / NFE.} The number of generative network evaluations spent
    producing one plan. For the transport family this is a solver setting; for diffusion it
    is fixed by training. \emph{$K$ is never the planner's candidate fan} --- a distinct
    quantity, also swept, and a persistent source of confusion.
  \item \textbf{Arms.} Arm~A is the unguided plan (no constraint enforcement). Arm~B is
    per-step projection of the iterate. Arm~C is in-loop trajectory optimisation on a
    predicted endpoint.
  \item \textbf{Genuine steps.} The number of sampler steps at which arm~C performs
    non-terminal guided work; formalised in Chapter~\ref{ch:method}. Zero genuine steps
    means arm~C is not arm~C.
  \item \textbf{Tightened geometry.} A stricter constraint margin applied to an otherwise
    identical feasible set; an experimental factor, not a formatting suffix.
  \item \textbf{``Better''.} Pareto dominance --- equal success \emph{and} equal constraint
    satisfaction, with strictly fewer evaluations \emph{and} lower wall-clock. Anything else
    is named a trade-off.
\end{itemize}

% =============================================================================
\chapter{Background}\label{ch:background}
% =============================================================================

\emph{Chapter summary.} This chapter builds the argument of RQ2 before any experiment is
described, as the target requires. It moves from behaviour cloning of trajectories, through
the two generative families, to the mechanism that separates them on step count, and closes
on the control-theoretic side that the constraint arms instantiate.

\section{Learning Trajectory Policies from Demonstration}\label{sec:bg:il}

Establishes the setting: a dataset of expert trajectories, a conditional model over
horizon-length trajectory segments rather than single actions, and receding-horizon
execution. The section makes two points that later chapters lean on. First, planning over a
horizon rather than emitting one action is what makes constraint enforcement possible at
all --- a per-action policy has nothing to project. Second, multi-modality in the
demonstrations is the reason a generative model is used instead of a regressor, and it is
also why a mean-seeking failure mode is a real risk when the sampler is truncated.

\section{Diffusion Generative Models}\label{sec:bg:diffusion}

Forward noising process, learned reverse denoiser, and the sampling loop, presented only to
the depth the thesis needs. The section's purpose is to isolate one property: the reverse
process is defined \emph{relative to a training-time noise schedule}, so the number of
reverse steps is not free. This is stated here and used in \ref{sec:bg:fewstep}; it is the
reason the baseline cannot follow the step ladder downward.

\section{Flow Matching}\label{sec:bg:fm}

Continuous-time transport between a simple prior and the data distribution, learned by
regressing a velocity field along a prescribed interpolation path, and sampled by
integrating an ODE. The section derives the training objective, notes that the conditional
formulation makes the target available in closed form without simulation, and observes that
sampling is now an ODE integration --- so the step count is a solver parameter and the
sample is deterministic given the initial noise.

Determinism is worth its own remark: it removes a source of run-to-run variance from the
plan, which matters for a controller that replans every timestep, and it changes what
``the same plan'' means when comparing arms.

\section{Few-Step Generative Models}\label{sec:bg:fewstep}

\textbf{The load-bearing section of the background.} It answers RQ2's mechanism half before
any number appears.

Naive flow matching still needs enough integration steps for the ODE solution to be
accurate. The few-step family removes that requirement by changing \emph{what the network
predicts}: instead of the instantaneous velocity at a point, the network is trained to
predict the \emph{average} velocity over an interval, so that a single evaluation can
traverse the whole interval. The section presents this target, its identity with the
instantaneous field in the limit, and the practical consequence that the training objective
now requires a directional derivative of the network --- a Jacobian-vector product ---
which constrains what may appear inside the differentiated closure.

A second variant is presented as a \emph{curriculum} on the same objective: rather than
regressing the analytic average-velocity target directly, it bootstraps a target from the
network's own output and anneals a scalar that interpolates between the two regimes. The
section presents it neutrally here; Chapter~\ref{ch:method} explains why the distinction is
a training-path difference and nothing else, and the results chapter reports that it does
not pay off.

The section closes with the comparison that the whole thesis turns on: \emph{for the
transport family the step budget is a solver argument, for diffusion it is a trained
property.} Everything in the results chapter about cheap plans is a consequence of that one
asymmetry, and it should be argued, not measured.

\section{Model Predictive Control and Constrained Sampling}\label{sec:bg:mpc}

Two threads. The first is standard receding-horizon control: an optimisation over a finite
horizon, executed one step at a time, re-solved each timestep. The second is the family of
methods that make a \emph{learned} plan admissible --- soft guidance by gradient, hard
projection onto a feasible set, and in-loop trajectory optimisation interleaved with the
sampler. The section sets up the vocabulary for the three arms and states the constrained
projection problem in the form the method chapter instantiates: a nonlinear program over the
horizon, with dynamics rows, box bounds, and geometric inequalities.

It also makes explicit that arms~B and~C solve \emph{the same feasible set with the same
solver}. Only the point at which the problem is posed differs. That framing is what makes
the later mechanism claim a claim about algorithms rather than about implementations.

\chapter{Related Work}\label{ch:related}
% =============================================================================

\emph{Chapter summary.} Four bodies of work converge on this thesis; the chapter treats each
as a line the thesis extends or corrects, and closes by stating precisely which gap is left.

\section{Diffusion-Based Planners and Policies}\label{sec:rel:diffusion}

Trajectory-level diffusion planning and its policy-level descendants, up to the
constrained-projection variant that this work starts from. The section is careful about a
dual role that recurs throughout: the immediate predecessor is simultaneously the
\emph{codebase} inherited and the \emph{baseline} measured against. Both roles are declared
here so no later table looks like a straw man.

\section{Flow Matching for Robot Control}\label{sec:rel:fm}

Transport-based generative models applied to control and manipulation, including
few-step variants and their reported step budgets. The section positions this thesis
against them on one axis: most report the engine improvement \emph{without} a hard
constraint mechanism, so the interaction between a cheap sampler and an expensive projector
--- the interaction this thesis finds to be decisive --- is not visible in them.

\section{Safety Filters and Hard-Constrained Sampling}\label{sec:rel:safety}

Projection-based safety filters, control-barrier approaches, and samplers that interleave
optimisation with generation. This is where arm~C's ancestry is set out, and where the
thesis's degeneracy contribution is positioned: the activation-threshold convention these
methods use has a low-budget regime in which the method reduces to its own baseline, and
that regime is not, to the author's knowledge, characterised in the literature.

\section{Accelerating Generative Planners}\label{sec:rel:accel}

Distillation, consistency-style objectives, solver improvements, and caching. The section
distinguishes these from the present approach: they accelerate a model \emph{post hoc},
whereas the few-step objective changes what is learned, and --- more importantly for a
controller --- leaves the budget adjustable at deployment rather than baking in a new one.

\section{Positioning of This Thesis}\label{sec:rel:positioning}

One page. The gap in one sentence: \emph{the engine question and the constraint question have
been studied separately, and the interaction between them --- which is where the cost
actually lives in a receding-horizon controller --- has not been measured under matched
conditions.} The section then states what this thesis adds that is not a combination of the
above: the genuine-step condition, the regime split with its mechanism, and the removal of
the selection machinery.

\chapter{Method}\label{ch:method}
% =============================================================================

\emph{Chapter summary.} The chapter is written as a sequence of deltas from the inherited
system. It states what was taken unchanged, what was replaced, and what was added, and it
keeps those three categories visibly separate --- both because it is honest and because the
validity of every comparison depends on the first category being large.

\section{Starting Point: Diffusion Predictive Control}\label{sec:method:dpcc}

A compact but complete description of the inherited system: temporal U-Net backbone,
diffusion engine, per-step projection onto a feasible set defined by dynamics and geometry,
a candidate fan with selection rules, and receding-horizon execution. Presented at the level
of detail needed to see exactly where the substitution is made.

The section ends with a table of provenance --- inherited, modified, new --- at file
granularity, which is the honest form of the ``we build on X'' sentence and feeds
\ref{app:repro}. \srcnote{TARGET \S5.3}

\section{Problem Formalisation}\label{sec:method:formalisation}

Notation, once, for the whole thesis: state and observation, the horizon-length trajectory
variable, the conditional generative model, the feasible set as the intersection of dynamics
rows, box bounds and geometric inequalities, and the closed-loop map. The constrained
sampling problem is stated in the form both arms attack, so that the difference between the
arms is visible as a difference in \emph{where} the problem is posed rather than in what is
being solved.

\section{System Overview}\label{sec:method:overview}

One figure and one page. The pipeline observation $\rightarrow$ conditional plan
$\rightarrow$ constraint mechanism $\rightarrow$ executed action, with the two swappable
blocks marked and everything else marked as held fixed. The figure is referenced throughout
the results chapter; it should be drawn once and drawn well.

\section{Generative Engine}\label{sec:method:engine}

The substitution itself. Three engines are instantiated behind one interface: naive flow
matching, the average-velocity few-step objective, and the bootstrapped-curriculum variant.
The section gives each one's training target, states what changes in the sampler for each,
and --- the point of the section --- shows that \emph{nothing outside the target and the
integration loop changes}.

Two sub-points carry real weight:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The curriculum variant is a training-path difference, not a new objective.}
    Its scalar interpolation parameter appears only in training and at one setting the two
    targets coincide branch for branch. This has a consequence the thesis must state, because
    it invalidated a set of earlier runs: a checkpoint trained at that setting \emph{is} the
    predecessor engine, whatever the directory is called.
  \item \textbf{The Jacobian-vector product constrains the architecture.} The few-step target
    differentiates through the network, so every component inside the differentiated closure
    must be forward-mode friendly. This is not a performance detail --- it determines how the
    visual encoder may be attached (\ref{sec:method:backbone}).
\end{itemize}

\hole{Per TARGET \S8 kill criterion 1, the curriculum variant is now presented as an
\emph{ablation and a negative result}, not as the top rung of an engine ladder. Keep it in
this section --- it is a legitimate arm of the mechanism story --- but do not promise a win.
The engine claim the thesis defends is the average-velocity objective.}

\section{Backbone and Conditioning}\label{sec:method:backbone}

Where the matched-architecture discipline is spelled out. One temporal U-Net at one
parameter count is used by every engine, including the baseline's. For the vision-conditioned
task, perception is a vendored encoder producing a fixed-width latent, and the generative
backbone never sees an image --- so the latent is a clean interface boundary and the
``same backbone'' claim is verifiable rather than asserted.

The section then explains the one non-obvious construction this forces: because the few-step
target needs a Jacobian-vector product, the encoder is evaluated \emph{once, outside} the
differentiated closure and its output captured as a constant inside it. Without this the
objective and the visual observation are incompatible. \srcnote{Methodology\_Sources/AUX\_visual\_aligning\_env.md \S3.1}

Transformer-style backbones are declared out of scope with a reason rather than by omission:
using them properly requires spatial features and cross-attention rather than a pooled
latent, which is a different architecture and would break the parameter match.

\section{Constraint Enforcement I: Per-Step Projection}\label{sec:method:proj}

Arm~B, inherited. The nonlinear program, the solver, the activation threshold that decides
at which points of the transport the projection is applied, and the candidate fan. The
section notes what is projected --- the current iterate --- and flags that this point is
off-manifold at early transport times, which is the fact the mechanism argument later uses.

\section{Constraint Enforcement II: In-Loop Trajectory Optimisation}\label{sec:method:hardflow}

Arm~C. The same feasible set and the same solver, but the optimisation is posed on the
\emph{predicted clean endpoint} implied by the current iterate and velocity, and the result
is pulled back into the transport before the next step. The section makes the structural
comparison to arm~B explicit --- identical constraints, identical solver, different
linearisation point --- because that is what licenses attributing any cost difference to the
algorithm.

The section also accounts honestly for what arm~C spends: it evaluates the network an extra
time per guided step to form the endpoint estimate, so the two arms are \emph{not}
evaluation-matched even when their step budgets are equal. This is stated here so that no
results table has to carry the caveat.

\section{When the Guided Sampler Is Not Guided}\label{sec:method:degenerate}

\textbf{A short section carrying one of the thesis's contributions.} The activation
convention that gates arm~C admits a regime in which the only active step is the terminal
one. At the terminal time the lookahead vanishes, the pull-back becomes a full snap rather
than a damped correction, and no subsequent step exists for the network to react to. What
executes is projection applied to a finished sample --- arm~B's algorithm with a different
solver.

The section defines the genuine-step count as a function of the budget and the threshold,
derives the boundary, and states the reporting rule the thesis enforces without exception:
rows below the boundary are tagged and excluded from every claim; rows with a single genuine
step are marked as carrying no attributable signal. The rule is applied to this work's own
earlier measurements, several of which it invalidates --- which the thesis says so, in this
section, rather than quietly dropping them.

\section{Candidate Selection}\label{sec:method:selection}

The projector's fan-and-select machinery, described and then examined. The section gives the
three selection rules, and establishes analytically what the ablation later confirms
empirically: at a fan of one the three rules are the \emph{same computation}, so evaluating
all three is pure waste. This is stated as a proof obligation discharged here, so that the
results chapter only has to handle the fan-greater-than-one case.

\section{Closed-Loop Deployment}\label{sec:method:deployment}

How a plan becomes a command, for both embodiments, in one section --- because the parallel
is the substance of RQ4.

For the manipulator, the model emits a Cartesian delta, the eval adds it to the current
observation, and inverse kinematics closes the loop inside every timestep. For the aerial
platform, the model emits a position delta which is accumulated into a running setpoint, and
a cascaded PID converts the setpoint into rotor commands. In both cases the generative model
replans at every timestep and never emits an actuator command.

Two properties deserve to be named rather than left implicit. The setpoint recursion is a
free-running integrator driven by the model's own past outputs, so plan error accumulates ---
which is the structural reason the aerial task is the fragile one. And the observation fed
back to the model contains that commanded setpoint alongside the measured state, so the
model conditions on its own history.
