\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

The results follow the order of the study: D3IL-avoiding, the benchmark of \ac{DPCC}, first, then
the two environments built for this thesis, D3IL-aligning and the UAV scenes. \autoref{sec:res:summary} brings the three together and
answers, per environment, how the generative models and the projection methods compare. Each
environment is reported in those two parts and closes with the supported model--projection combination.
Each table states its backbone and its evaluation protocol.

% =============================================================================
\section{Obstacle Avoidance}\label{sec:res:state}
% =============================================================================

Every comparison in this section is made at the protocol of \ac{DPCC} --- five training seeds and two
episodes per seed and geometry, ten episodes per geometry (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:avoiding}).
Results are averaged per geometry and then across the three geometries. A longer evaluation of the same
configurations at twenty episodes per seed exists, but at the baseline's budget it is short of seeds and
geometries, so no claim in this section rests on it; it is kept as supplementary material in
\autoref{app:avoiding-twenty-episode}. At every control step the planner generates
$B=4$ candidate plans and executes one of them, and every model is shown under each of the three rules
that make that choice (\autoref{sec:method:selection}): random selection ($r$), cumulative projection
cost ($c$) and temporal consistency ($t$). The models are then compared at their best rule.

The generative-model comparison first reads the raw outputs with projection switched off. All results
with a projector in the loop are then reported together under projection methods, including the
comparison of the four models through the fixed per-step projector of \ac{DPCC} and the comparison
between per-step and endpoint projection. The section closes with the supported model--projection
combinations.

\subsection{Generative Models}\label{sec:res:avoiding:models}

The four models of \autoref{sec:setup:baselines} are compared, on the shared temporal U-Net: FM,
MeanFM, CI-MeanFM and the diffusion model of \ac{DPCC}. One property of the step budget decides how the
results below are read. $\nfe$ counts network evaluations in both families but is not the same quantity
in them (\autoref{tab:eval}): for the flow-based models it is the number of ODE solver steps, chosen at
inference, and for the baseline the number of denoising steps, fixed when its noise schedule is
discretised during training --- so every diffusion budget is a separately trained checkpoint and every
flow-based budget is the same checkpoint read again. This subsection reads the plans executed without
projection; projected results begin in \autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projection}.

The demonstrations teach the route to the goal. With projection switched off, each of the three
flow-based models reaches it in all thirty episodes at both reported budgets; the diffusion model
reaches it in 28 of thirty at one denoising step and 29 of thirty at twenty
(\autoref{tab:avoiding-raw-models}). Reaching the goal is therefore distinct from satisfying the
test-time constraints: no unprojected row exceeds $0.100$ success with constraint satisfaction.

Read down the success column first. Every flow-based model reaches the goal in every one of its thirty
episodes at every budget in the table, and the baseline in $0.933$ of them at one denoising step and
$0.967$ at twenty. Producing a plan that arrives is not what separates these models on this benchmark.

Read the next column and none of them arrives legally. The highest success with constraint satisfaction
anywhere in the table is $0.133$, instantaneous-velocity matching at twenty evaluations; the two
average-velocity models read $0.033$ at the budgets they are operated at, instantaneous-velocity
matching $0.100$, the baseline $0.100$ at its own budget and $0.000$ at one denoising step. Between
$15.5$ and $19.0$ control steps of an average episode violate a constraint, and raising the budget does
not work that number down: the intermediate budgets in \autoref{tab:app:avoiding-raw-full} hold it
between $14.2$ and $19.1$, and the best and the worst of those counts are both a ten-evaluation row.
The demonstrations all four models learn from cross the
same constraint sets (\autoref{tab:avoiding-geometries}), so a plan that respects them is not something
any of them could have imitated. On this benchmark constraint satisfaction is the projector's work, for
the flow-based objectives exactly as for the diffusion baseline.

The last column prices generation alone. One network evaluation of a flow-based model costs $9.0$ to
$10.3$\,ms per control step and twenty denoising steps of the baseline $179.3$\,ms. Against the projected
table the remainder is the projector: about $8$ of the $18.3$\,ms analytic average-velocity matching
takes at one network evaluation, and about $374$ of the baseline's $553.4$\,ms at its own twenty
denoising steps. The projector runs on
$\lceil \eta\nfe \rceil$ sampling steps (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:guiding}), which is one at the
flow-based operating budget and ten at the baseline's, so the budget is paid twice --- once in the
network and again in the solver.

\FloatBarrier

Those three columns score what the models produce. \autoref{fig:raw-plans} shows it. The one thing to be
clear about before reading it is that it is not the executed path --- the executed path is not drawn in it
at all. At every control step the policy returns a plan tensor of shape $(N,8,6)$
(\autoref{tab:train}): $N=4$ candidate plans, each $H=8$ waypoints long, each waypoint carrying a planar
position increment together with the commanded and the measured planar position. What the figure draws,
per candidate, is the \emph{measured} planar position channels of those eight waypoints --- one
eight-point curve --- and the green dot marks the first of the eight, the state the plan is anchored at.
Drawing the fan at every control step would fill the panel with a solid block, so it is drawn at every
fourth; the stored tensor keeps all of them.

Of the eight waypoints exactly one is ever carried out. The selection rule picks one of the four
candidates (\autoref{sec:method:selection}), its first action is applied as a Cartesian increment, and the
other seven waypoints are thrown away and regenerated at the next control step, because the planner
replans every step (\autoref{tab:eval}). So the eight-step plan is never executed as drawn --- and that is
what makes it worth drawing. It is the network's output before the projector repairs it and before the
controller smooths it, the quantity that for an image generative model would be the fidelity of a sample.
Every other figure and table in this section reads the models through something downstream; this one reads
them directly.

Read that way, the models separate (\autoref{fig:raw-plans}). At one network evaluation the successive
plans of analytic average-velocity matching form a narrow ribbon: consecutive fans overlap, and each of
the eight-point curves is a bounded arc towards the goal. The diffusion baseline at the same budget emits
plans that jump across the workspace from one control step to the next.

That difference does not appear in the motion, and it is worth saying why before the panels are used for
anything. Two things sit between a plan and the arm (\autoref{sec:setup:platforms}): only the
\emph{first} action of the plan is executed, and that action is a Cartesian increment reaching the joints
through inverse kinematics and a joint-space PD controller. A path assembled one first-action at a time,
through an integrator, is smooth whether or not the plans behind it were. The panels are therefore the
only place the incoherence is visible at all. The analytic average-velocity matching panel at two
evaluations shows the ribbon surviving a second evaluation, without reducing the violation count in this
episode.

The two remaining objectives, in the middle two columns, fall between those extremes and in the order
the projected results below give. Instantaneous-velocity matching throws a wide, jagged fan at both budgets, and much of its length
lies inside the forbidden halfspace on the left of the panel rather than in the free corridor.
Consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching is noisy at one evaluation and markedly tighter at two,
where its plans thread the obstacle field in a band comparable to the analytic objective's. Neither is as
tight as analytic average-velocity matching at the same budget, and all three are far from the near-scribble
of the baseline at one evaluation.

The baseline's small-budget panels are two models, one trained at each budget, and the second denoising step is
what turns the scribble into a plan: at $\nfe=2$ its plans form a single coherent bundle from start to
goal. That bundle runs along the wrong side of the constraint. The same evaluation scores the
unprojected arm at $0.00$ success with constraint satisfaction with $20.5$ violating control steps per
episode, so what the second step buys is coherence and not feasibility --- which is the argument for the
projector rather than against the model. At its own twenty steps, in the last row, the baseline draws a
single tight bundle that runs from the start state to the goal line: the budget buys coherence, and the
upper half of that bundle still lies inside the left halfspace. On this panel's own cell the baseline
violates a constraint on $18.5$ control steps of the episode at twenty denoising steps, against $28.0$ at
one --- coherent and infeasible, where the small budget was incoherent and infeasible.

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:avoiding:projection}

The remaining results all include projection. The first comparison holds the per-step projection of
\ac{DPCC} fixed and varies the generative model. The later comparison holds the generative model fixed
and varies the projection method.

\subsubsection{At the Protocol of DPCC}\label{sec:res:avoiding:dpccprotocol}

This pass puts the projector back and holds it fixed at the per-step projection of \ac{DPCC}, so that
the generative model is the only thing that varies; the two projection methods are compared against
each other in \autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projectors}. Every number from here to the end of the
subsection is an executed-episode result of that pipeline.

Two marks recur in this table and in the ones after it. The \textbf{operating point}, \selectedmark, is
the configuration this thesis carries forward on the benchmark --- the cheapest one that holds its
success, not the highest number in a column. The \textbf{baseline of record}, \baselinemark, is the
diffusion model of \ac{DPCC} at its own budget of twenty under its own best selection rule, and it is
what every claim of this chapter is measured against. Every row of the table carries all five training
seeds and all three geometries: the budgets are $\nfe\in\{1,2,5,10\}$ for analytic average-velocity
matching, $\{1,2\}$ for the consistency-interpolated model, $\{1,2,20\}$ for instantaneous-velocity
matching and $\{1,10,20\}$ for the diffusion baseline, whose step count is fixed at training. Diffusion at
$\nfe=2$ has a single-seed result and no five-seed one, and stays pending rather than being pooled with
rows that have five.

The baseline reproduces its published result (\autoref{tab:avoiding-dpcc-protocol},
\autoref{tab:target}): under cumulative projection cost it reaches a success rate with constraint
satisfaction of $1.000$ in $70.1$ control steps, at $553.4$\,ms per control step. Temporal consistency
reaches the same rate in more steps and random selection falls short of it.

The baseline's own budget is not its own best configuration. At ten denoising steps under temporal
consistency it also reaches $1.000$, in $68.7$ control steps at $321.7$\,ms --- the same constraint
satisfaction, fewer control steps and $58\,\%$ of the time per action, on five seeds and three
geometries (\autoref{tab:app:avoiding-dpcc-full}, where that row is held to the same standard as the
others). It does not change what the baseline of record is, because the baseline of record is the
configuration \ac{DPCC} reports and the one every comparison here is stated against, and the halved cost
is still an order of magnitude above every flow-based row. It does mean that the $30\times$ figure quoted
below is against the published configuration and not against the cheapest diffusion configuration
measured here, which is $18\times$.

Two flow-based models clear that row at a single network evaluation, on all three of its axes at once.
Instantaneous-velocity matching reaches $1.000$ in $67.0$ control steps and consistency-interpolated
average-velocity matching $1.000$ in $59.2$, the fewest in the table, each at about $18$\,ms per control
step --- a thirtieth of the baseline's, on the same temporal U-Net of $4.0$\,M parameters. Neither gives
up success with constraint satisfaction, and both finish in fewer control steps. This is the one place
in the thesis where a flow-based model is ahead of the baseline on every reported axis at once rather
than trading one against another.

Analytic average-velocity matching, the model this benchmark is operated with, reaches $0.967$ in $58.6$
control steps at $18.3$\,ms --- against the baseline's best rule, one episode of thirty fewer reaching
the goal within the constraints, $11.5$ fewer control steps, and a thirtieth of the time. What moves that
result is the selection rule: under cumulative projection cost the same model drops to $0.933$ and stalls
at $72.4$ or $97.2$ control steps, the
behaviour described in \autoref{sec:res:ablations}. The three models differ in how exposed they are to
that choice. Instantaneous-velocity matching is the only one that holds $1.000$ under all three rules at
both budgets; consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching holds it under temporal consistency at
both, but falls to $0.900$ under random selection at two evaluations and stalls under cumulative
projection cost in the same way.

\subsubsection{Candidate Plans and Selection Rules}\label{sec:res:ablations}

The rule column of \autoref{tab:avoiding-dpcc-protocol} moves some rows more than the objective does, so
it is worth saying what that column is and what it costs before the table is used further.

With a single candidate plan the three selection rules return the same plan: success and control steps
are identical in all fifteen paired seed--geometry combinations, for the diffusion baseline, analytic average-velocity matching,
instantaneous-velocity matching and consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching. With four candidates, generation time changes by at most six per
cent, because the four plans are one batched network evaluation, while projection time grows by a factor
of $3.2$ to $4.9$, because the programs are solved one after another on the CPU. Solving them in parallel
would bring analytic average-velocity matching at $\nfe=1$ from $18.1$ to about $11.7$\,ms per control step with all four
candidates kept; this is a prediction from two measured points.
\dataref{DA\_20260827 \S2, \S6, \S10.5}

Cumulative projection cost slows analytic average-velocity matching down on this task. In
\autoref{tab:avoiding-dpcc-protocol} it takes $72.4$ control steps at $\nfe=1$ and $97.2$ at $\nfe=2$,
against $58.6$ and $59.4$ under temporal consistency, and reaches $0.933$ success with constraint
satisfaction against $0.967$: the rule occasionally selects a plan that stalls, and a stalled plan costs
steps without buying constraint satisfaction. The same happens to consistency-interpolated
average-velocity matching, which goes from $60.1$ steps under temporal consistency to $89.7$ under
cumulative projection cost at $\nfe=2$, and it does not happen to the diffusion baseline, for which
cumulative projection cost is the best of the three. Temporal consistency is therefore the selection rule
the flow-based models are operated with on this task.

\subsubsection{The Cost Frontier}\label{sec:res:avoiding:pareto}

At equal success, two costs remain: how many control steps an episode takes, and how long one action takes
to compute. They are not exchangeable --- a plan that arrives in fewer steps but costs half a second per
step is not obviously better than the reverse --- so this section does not add them into one score. It
places every configuration on both axes at once and asks which ones nothing else beats on both
(\autoref{fig:avoiding-tradeoff}).

A configuration is \emph{non-dominated} when no other configuration in the comparison is at the same time
faster per action and shorter in control steps. Only configurations that keep the task solved take part:
a point enters the comparison when its success with constraint satisfaction is within $0.05$ of the best
value in its panel, so that a fast configuration that reaches the goal less reliably cannot appear as a
favourable trade. Reading the figure, colour identifies the model, marker shape the selection rule, the
number beside a point its step budget, and a ring marks each non-dominated point; down and to the left is
better on both axes, which the boxed arrow in the legend repeats. The step axis is broken at $80$: every
configuration that solves the task lies below that line, and the few above it --- the hollow $\nfe=2$
points and one diffusion cell --- are drawn on a compressed scale so that the solved band is not squeezed
into a third of the panel. The figure and
\autoref{tab:avoiding-dpcc-protocol} draw on the same cells: every budget plotted here is a row of that
table, on five training seeds and three geometries.

\subsubsection{Step Budget}\label{sec:res:fewstep}

The flow-based models keep their success with constraint satisfaction as the step budget falls to a
single network evaluation, while the diffusion baseline does not reach it at a small budget at all
(\autoref{fig:k-ladder}). The baseline reaches $0.667$ at $\nfe=1$ and $1.000$ at $\nfe=10$ and
$\nfe=20$. Over the same range the flow-based models never leave the top of the axis: both
average-velocity models read $1.000$ or $0.967$ at one and two evaluations, analytic average-velocity
matching holds $0.933$ out to ten, and instantaneous-velocity matching reads $1.000$ at one, two and
twenty.

The two curves are not read the same way, and the difference is the point. Each diffusion point is its own
model: the number of denoising steps is fixed when the noise schedule is discretised during training, so
the budgets of that curve are three separately trained checkpoints, one per step count, and moving along
it costs a training run. The flow-based curve is one checkpoint per model, read at every budget, because
there the step count is the number of steps the ODE solver takes at inference. For a flow-based model the
budget is therefore a dial that can be turned after training, and for the diffusion baseline it is a
property of the checkpoint --- the same asymmetry \autoref{fig:raw-plans} shows, where the two diffusion
panels are two separately trained models and each flow-based row is one model read twice. The cost of
that asymmetry is a training run: the $\nfe=2$ panel of that figure required one, and the single-seed
evaluation it produced puts the baseline at about $225$\,ms per control step at two denoising steps,
against instantaneous-velocity matching's $17.3$\,ms at one. Cutting the budget from twenty to two
leaves the baseline at thirteen times the cost.

\subsection{Generative Models}\label{sec:res:aligning:models}

One model moves the box to its target on this task and the other three barely move it at all
(\autoref{tab:va-models}). The median is the column to read there: the spread over the ten contexts is
of the order of the mean itself, because a context in which the box is not touched contributes the whole
starting distance and one in which it is delivered contributes almost nothing. Analytic average-velocity matching ends a median $0.0741$\,m from the target,
from a starting distance of $0.4530$\,m --- it closes $84\,\%$ of the distance --- and it is the only model
that moves the box in every context. The other three end between $0.39$ and $0.46$\,m. The two

\subsubsection{Step Budget and the Cost Frontier}\label{sec:res:aligning:budget}

Analytic average-velocity matching improves with the budget: its median final distance falls from
$0.2779$\,m at $\nfe=2$ to $0.1194$\,m at $\nfe=10$, $0.0741$\,m at $\nfe=20$, and
$0.0673$\,m at $\nfe=100$ (\autoref{tab:va-models}). The last fivefold increase in evaluations reduces the median distance by
$0.0068$\,m while raising the time per control step from $190.5$ to $892.4$\,ms. Unlike D3IL-avoiding,
where one network evaluation already saturates the task (\autoref{sec:res:state}), alignment rewards
additional evaluations.

The three flow-based models are measured at $\nfe=100$ as well, and the ordering among them is the same as
at twenty: analytic average-velocity matching first ($0.0741$ and $0.0673$\,m), then the
consistency-interpolated model ($0.3897$ and $0.1791$\,m), with instantaneous-velocity matching last and
barely moving ($0.4085$ and $0.4247$\,m). A hundred evaluations buy the consistency-interpolated model a
great deal --- it more than halves its median --- and buy instantaneous-velocity matching nothing at all.
The diffusion baseline is compared at the budget it was trained with, as everywhere in this chapter. Below twenty the ladder is
incomplete: at $\nfe=10$ only analytic average-velocity matching has a recorded cell, at $\nfe=2$ the
two average-velocity models have one, and at $\nfe=1$ no model has been evaluated; those cells are
printed \emph{pending} in \autoref{tab:va-models} rather than left blank.
\dataref{va\_results.py; CLOSURE\_20260907 \S1, \S5.1}

Putting the outcome and its price on the same axes gives this task its cost frontier
(\autoref{fig:aligning-tradeoff}). It is not the frontier of \autoref{fig:avoiding-tradeoff} and cannot
be: on D3IL-avoiding every eligible configuration solves the task and only the two costs differ, whereas
here nothing is solved or failed --- what a model delivers is a distance, and the control-step count is
set by the episode limit rather than by arrival (\autoref{tab:eval}). The frontier is therefore the
outcome itself against the time one action costs, and a configuration is on it when nothing else both
ends closer to the target and computes faster.

Three marks of the drawing carry a reading rule. The outcome axis is the share of the starting distance
the box was moved towards its target, the bracketed number of \autoref{tab:va-models}, so the dashed
line at $0\,\%$ is a box that was not moved and $100\,\%$ at the top is a box delivered; two points
that would print on top of each other are displaced by a few pixels, which moves the drawing and not the
data; and the boxed arrow is the direction in which both axes improve.

Four of the ten configurations are on it, and all four are analytic average-velocity matching, at
$\nfe=2$, $10$, $20$ and $100$. Every other model at every budget is dominated, and the way they are dominated
is the result: at $\nfe=20$ the consistency-interpolated objective, instantaneous-velocity matching and
the diffusion baseline all sit close to the line at which the box was not moved at all, at between $189$
and $298$\,ms per control step, while the analytic objective at $\nfe=2$ --- a seventh to a tenth of
that cost --- has already closed two fifths of the distance. MeanFM's own $\nfe=100$ point reduces its median distance
by only $0.0068$\,m at $4.7$ times its $\nfe=20$ cost.

\subsubsection{Consistency Interpolation across the Step Budget}\label{sec:res:aligning:consistency}

The consistency-interpolated objective behaves differently from the analytic one on this task: it does
not improve when it is given a larger budget. At two network evaluations the two are level --- each ends
closer in five of the ten contexts. At twenty, where the analytic objective has moved the box most of the
way to its target, the consistency-interpolated one has not moved at all from where it was at two
evaluations, and it ends further away in seven of ten contexts. Raising the budget spends time and buys
nothing, and the same holds for the more aggressive setting $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=0.05$.

The numbers behind that are flat where the analytic objective's fall. With
$\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=0.2$ the median final distance moves from $0.3738$ to $0.3897$\,m between two and
twenty evaluations, and with $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=0.05$ from $0.3978$ to $0.4488$\,m, against the analytic
objective's $0.2779$ to $0.0741$\,m.

The mechanism is in the training target. It is built from a stop-gradient copy of the network itself, so
part of the network's own error enters the field it learns, and that part does not shrink when the solver
takes smaller steps (\autoref{sec:method:alphaflow}). A task that rewards more evaluations therefore
exposes it, as this one does; a task already saturated at one evaluation, as D3IL-avoiding is, does not.
\dataref{va\_results.py; Gen14 DA\_20260907 Gate 1; CLOSURE\_20260907 \S3, R.4}
\guard{The level result at $\nfe=2$ is in distance only. Counting the contexts in which any
configuration reaches the target, analytic average-velocity matching succeeds in seven and
consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching in two (CLOSURE\_20260907 R.4).}

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:aligning:projection}

D3IL-aligning is the task on which the generative model needs many network evaluations: analytic
average-velocity matching comes closest to the target at $\nfe=20$ (\autoref{sec:res:aligning:budget}),
so that is the model and the budget the projection is put on. The projected results below are all read
on it.

\paragraph{The activation threshold is compressed, because the budget is high.}
Everything about the threshold on this task follows from one fact: D3IL-aligning is the task that needs a
large step budget, and the default of \ac{DPCC}, $\eta=0.5$, was set for small ones. The projector runs
on the last $\lceil \eta\nfe \rceil$ sampling steps, so at a fixed share $\eta$ its cost grows with the
budget: every extra sampling step in the active window is another program solved, for each of the four
candidate plans, at each of up to four hundred control steps. On D3IL-avoiding at one or two evaluations
the default costs one solve; here, at $\nfe=20$, it would be ten solves per candidate per control step,
and at $\nfe=100$ fifty. At those budgets the default threshold is not a projector one can run, and on
this task that was measured rather than assumed. At $\nfe=100$ the same model, constraint set and
selection rule cost
$\mathbf{15{,}218 \pm 3{,}885}$\,ms per control step at $\eta=0.5$ and
$\mathbf{1{,}195 \pm 180}$\,ms at $\eta=0.1$ --- a factor of $12.7$ --- while the median final distance
was $0.1816$\,m in the first case and $0.1876$\,m in the second, a difference of $6$\,mm on a box that
starts $0.4530$\,m from its target.

The threshold is therefore compressed as the budget grows, so that the projector keeps a roughly constant
\emph{number} of guiding steps instead of a constant share: $\eta=0.2$ at $\nfe=20$ and $\eta=0.4$ at
$\nfe=10$, which is three guiding steps at both (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:guiding}), where on
D3IL-avoiding at one evaluation there were none. Every projected alignment result of this chapter is under
that compressed threshold, and \autoref{tab:va-threshold} is the ladder it was chosen on. What a lower threshold gives up is the early part of sampling, where the sample is still
far from its final shape and the projection has least to hold on to; what it buys is a projected
configuration whose time per action is of the same order as the generation it is attached to.

\subsubsection{Endpoint Projection across the Models}\label{sec:res:aligning:projection:models}

The comparison above is one generative model. \autoref{tab:va-projection-models} runs it across the
models, at every budget this section reports and under every selection rule, and it holds in the same
direction.

That table is as complete as this evaluation allows, and it is worth saying exactly where it stops. All
three flow-based models carry \emph{both} projectors at $\nfe=20$ on the tightened set, so for them the
pre-projection comparison of \autoref{tab:va-models} and the post-projection comparison here rest on the
same cells. The diffusion baseline carries \emph{neither}: it has no tightened projected cell at all on
this task, under either projector. A four-model comparison before and after projection is therefore not
something this data can be reduced to, and the missing cell is the one pending run that would change
that.
\guard{The diffusion baseline has no projected cell on the tightened alignment constraint set under
either projection method, so nothing in this subsection or in \autoref{tab:va-projection} compares a
projector against the baseline on this task --- the comparison is between the two projectors, on the
flow-based models. Recorded as R2 in
\texttt{data\_status/PENDING\_20260922\_all\_lacking\_runs.md}; verified against the corpus 2026-09-21.} Twelve comparisons run the two methods on the same model, the same budget and the same rule.
Endpoint projection keeps more contexts free of violations in nine of them, per-step projection in one,
and two are level. The single exception is instantaneous-velocity matching at $\nfe=20$ under temporal
consistency, by one context.

That is the reverse of D3IL-avoiding, and the reason is the budget rather than the task. This task is
run at ten and twenty network evaluations because the generative model needs them
(\autoref{sec:res:aligning:budget}), so the endpoint solve has several guiding steps to act on, where on
D3IL-avoiding at one evaluation it had none (\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projection}). Given steps to act
on, the method does what it is for: it removes violations from the plan before the plan is finished,
rather than correcting each step after the fact.

What it does not do is dominate. Its advantage is on the constraint and not on the task: across the same
twelve comparisons it moves the box in more contexts than per-step projection in two, in as many in
seven, and in fewer in three, and its median final distances are level. Nor is it uniformly cheaper. At
$\nfe=10$ and $\nfe=20$ it costs between $0.6$ and $0.9$ of per-step projection's time per control step,
but at $\nfe=2$ it costs about three times as much, because a single interior-point solve over the whole
trajectory does not become cheap merely because the trajectory was generated in two steps. The honest
summary is that endpoint projection is the better projector on this task wherever it has a guiding step,
by a margin that is consistent rather than large, and at a comparable price.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:aligning:conclusion}

D3IL-aligning selects analytic average-velocity matching as the generative model: at twenty evaluations
it ends closer to the target than instantaneous-velocity matching and the diffusion baseline, whereas
consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching does not improve with the larger budget. It selects
endpoint projection as the projection method: at the large budget this task needs, endpoint projection
with random selection keeps all ten contexts free of violations and still moves the box in eight, at the
time per control step of per-step projection at $\nfe=20$ and at $37\,\%$ of it at $\nfe=10$. The
conclusion is limited to training-set contexts.

The section is built like the two manipulation benchmarks: the generative models are read first on the plans
they generate, executed without projection (\autoref{sec:res:uav:models}); the same cells are then read under
the projector of \ac{DPCC} (\autoref{sec:res:uav:projected}), where the cost frontier and the flown paths
are; and the two projection methods are compared against each other last (\autoref{sec:res:uav:projection}).
UAV-corridor is the scene with a test-time constraint the demonstrations do not satisfy, UAV-pillars the scene
on which the plans are feasible before projection, so that its projected rows measure what a method preserves
rather than what it repairs, and UAV-s-curve records the tracking-controller failure examined in
\autoref{sec:res:uav:controller}; it is kept in one piece in \autoref{sec:res:uav:scurve}.
\flawed{Every UAV-pillars block of this section is temporarily disabled (author, 2026-09-22, v3.65): the
evaluation on that scene is under review. The blocks stay in place, printed in grey, and no claim of this
chapter may rest on them until they are updated.}

All results use training seed 6
(\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:uav}). Every generative model is a temporal U-Net with $3.96$--$3.97$\,M
parameters, trained with the same optimiser settings (\autoref{tab:train}), and every flight is tracked by
the cascaded geometric controller with brake-to-rest setpoints unless stated otherwise. The diffusion model
is trained at $\nfe=20$ and is compared at the budget it was trained with.

\emph{Brake-to-rest} is the third of three velocity-setpoint policies the tracker accepts, and it is the
only one of them that a learned planner can supply honestly. The tracker wants a position setpoint, a
velocity feedforward and an acceleration feedforward; the demonstrations were flown with all three, taken
as analytic derivatives of the reference curve. A planner emits none of them --- it emits one position
increment per control step --- so the velocity feedforward has to be manufactured, and the two ways of
manufacturing it each inject an error the planner cannot control. Dividing the increment by the planning
interval makes the commanded speed a function of how long inference happened to take, so latency jitter
is fed straight into the tracker. Fixing the speed at a constant magnitude in the direction of the
increment removes the jitter but discards the magnitude the planner asked for, commanding the same speed
whether the plan moved $2$\,cm or $20$. Brake-to-rest sets the velocity feedforward to zero, which makes
the tracker a pure position regulator: the only quantity that reaches the vehicle is the setpoint the
planner produced. It is the conservative choice, and it is the one under which every number in this
section was measured. \autoref{sec:res:uav:controller} is where that choice is tested rather than assumed.
\srcnote{\texttt{mix\_uav\_test/eval\_mix\_uav.py:1591--1594} lists the four tracker options:
\texttt{pid} ($v\sidx{des} = a/\Delta t\sidx{fm}$, E7 legacy, timing-sensitive), \texttt{pid\_stopgo}
($v\sidx{des} = 0$, U2, used here), \texttt{pid\_const\_v} ($v\sidx{des} = \mathrm{unit}(a)\cdot 0.4$, U3)
and \texttt{mjpc}. The comparison of the three and the expert-versus-evaluation feedforward mismatch is
\texttt{logs\_in\_develop/Gen11/Epoch8\_UAV\_Mjpc\_thrust\_control/DESIGN\_obs\_action\_loop.md} \S3.4--3.5,
whose own table marks \texttt{pid} \enquote{No --- \texttt{dt\_fm} jitter} and \texttt{pid\_const\_v}
\enquote{No --- magnitude is wrong}. The $0.4$\,m/s default is the expert dataset's average speed.}

\subsubsection{UAV-corridor}\label{sec:res:uav:corridor}

With per-step projection the models separate, and they separate on the constraint rather than on the goal
(\autoref{tab:uav-corridor}; the same cells before projection are \autoref{tab:uav-corridor-raw}). Analytic average-velocity matching and consistency-interpolated
average-velocity matching succeed collision-free on all twelve flights at $\nfe=3$ and at $\nfe=5$.
Instantaneous-velocity matching succeeds on every flight too, but not collision-free: it keeps $1.5$
to $5.2$ shallow violating steps per flight and is collision-free on at most two of twelve. The diffusion
baseline is collision-free on every flight and succeeds on none.

That last row is the one to read carefully, because the flown paths do not look like a failure
(\autoref{fig:uav-corridor-paths}). The finish line is not a vertical plane at the end of the corridor: it
is the plane through the end of the demonstrated route, perpendicular to that route's final approach
heading, and the demonstrated routes turn downward as they leave the corridor. Projected, the diffusion
baseline holds the lane it learned and never makes that turn. It therefore travels as far along $x$ as
the models that succeed --- its flights end between $x=2.66$ and $x=2.79$\,m, inside the range of the
successful flights --- while staying on the near side of the line, and it runs the full $396$-step episode limit on
every flight rather than stopping early on arrival, ending $0.51$\,m from the goal point. Collision-free
and $0/12$ is not a contradiction here: the projector kept it out of the halfspace, and it did so by
keeping it on a lane that does not reach the goal.
\srcnote{\texttt{success\_relaxed = crossed\_line and safe}
(\texttt{mix\_uav\_test/eval\_mix\_uav.py:1872}); the line is built at \texttt{:1580--1588} through the

one does not reach, so the runs were not spent. Analytic average-velocity matching at $\nfe=3$ is therefore ahead of instantaneous-velocity
matching on the constraint, $12/12$ against $0/12$, and ahead of the diffusion baseline on the goal,
$12/12$ against $0/12$, at $0.16$ of its time per control step.

The budgets are $\nfe\in\{1,3,5\}$ rather than $\{1,2,5\}$ because of what the second one has to support.
At the activation threshold of $0.5$ this scene runs, the projector acts on the last
$\lceil \eta\nfe \rceil$ sampling steps, and at $\nfe=1$ and $\nfe=2$ that is the terminal step alone ---
the degenerate case of \autoref{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}, in which endpoint projection is
projection after sampling and has nothing to guide. $\nfe=3$ is the smallest budget at which it has a
real guiding step, so it is the smallest budget at which the two projectors of
\autoref{sec:res:uav:projection} can be compared at all. Choosing it for the model table as well keeps
one budget ladder across the whole scene instead of two.

The same numbers against their price give this scene its cost frontier
(\autoref{fig:uav-corridor-tradeoff}). As on D3IL-aligning, and unlike D3IL-avoiding, the control-step
count is not the cost to plot: a flight that succeeds keeps flying to the episode limit, so its
step count measures the limit and not the arrival. What is plotted is therefore success with constraint
satisfaction against the time one action costs.

All three selection rules of every cell are tabled in \autoref{tab:uav-corridor-projection}, and points
that would print on top of each other are displaced by a few pixels, which moves the drawing and not the
data. The success axis is logarithmic in the number of successful flights, so that the difference between
two and six of twelve is as visible as the difference between six and twelve; a configuration that never
succeeds cannot be drawn on it and sits on a floor below the axis break. Two rules follow from that
drawing: a configuration with no successful flight is not a trade-off and is not eligible for the
frontier, and two costs within one per cent of each other are treated as one cost, so that a tenth of a
millisecond cannot put a point on the frontier.

Four configurations are on the frontier and they are the two average-velocity models at $\nfe=3$ and at
$\nfe=5$, all four at $1.00$. The cheapest of them costs $97.7$\,ms per control step, and the fifth point
on the staircase is analytic average-velocity matching at $\nfe=1$, which buys $0.50$ for $29.2$\,ms ---
so a third of the cost carries half the flights and no more. Nothing else is on it: instantaneous-velocity
matching reaches at most $0.17$ at any budget, and the diffusion baseline is the rightmost point in the
figure at $625$\,ms and $0.00$, because within the episode it never reaches the finish line. The shape of
this frontier is a step rather than a slope, which is the difference between this scene and
D3IL-aligning: here the budget either buys the constraint or does not.

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:uav:projection}

On UAV-corridor, per-step projection is the method, and the comparison is now complete: both methods have
been run at $\nfe=3$ and $\nfe=5$ under every selection rule, for all three flow-based models
(\autoref{tab:uav-corridor-projection}). Per-step projection succeeds collision-free on all twelve
flights for both average-velocity models under all three rules at both budgets. Endpoint projection reaches
$0.58$ at best --- analytic average-velocity matching at $\nfe=3$ with cumulative projection cost --- and
is below half on every other cell of those two models. It is the cheaper arm, $74$\,ms against $99$\,ms per
control step at $\nfe=3$ and $47$\,ms in its single-candidate form, so what it offers on this scene is
about half the projection cost for about half the constraint satisfaction. The bar this thesis set for it
--- overtaking the per-step projector of \ac{DPCC} at a lower projection threshold --- is not cleared here.

Endpoint projection also gets worse as the budget grows, where per-step projection does not move: analytic
average-velocity matching falls from $0.58$ to $0.33$ and consistency-interpolated from $0.42$ to $0.17$
between $\nfe=3$ and $\nfe=5$, while per-step projection stays at $1.00$ throughout. Raising the budget
lengthens the ODE whose endpoint the single solve has to anticipate, so the prediction it corrects is
further from what the sampler finally produces.

\subsection{Caveat: The Tracking Controller}\label{sec:res:uav:controller}

On the manipulator, inverse kinematics and the joint-space controller lie between a generated plan and
the executed motion. The quadrotor is underactuated and
open-loop unstable (\autoref{sec:bg:quadrotor}), and a plan reaches the rotors only through the cascaded
geometric controller that tracks it. That controller needs a velocity setpoint the plan does not supply,
and every result in this chapter uses the brake-to-rest policy of \autoref{sec:method:geometric}: the setpoint
is zero, so the vehicle accelerates towards each new position and brakes to rest at it. The result of a flight is therefore a property of the planner and the controller
together, and UAV-s-curve, with its two sharp turns, is where the controller becomes the limit.

This is shown by keeping the plan and changing the controller (\autoref{tab:uav-controller}). Analytic
average-velocity matching at $\nfe=10$, executed without projection, succeeds on none of ten flights
with the brake-to-rest controller and ends on average $2.81$\,m from the goal point of its route. With
MuJoCo MPC \parencite{howell2022predictive} as the tracking controller, the same plans end $0.299$, $0.298$
and $0.294$\,m from that point, and two of the three flights cross the finish line. The brake-to-rest
failures are not wall collisions: on all ten flights the vehicle stays at altitude until it loses attitude
control and inverts, between control steps $395$ and $421$, about $12$\,s into the flight. MuJoCo MPC flies
the same turn. The unprojected plan is flyable; the brake-to-rest controller cannot fly it.

What separates the two is the velocity setpoint and not the gains. Both controllers act on the same
vehicle with the same nominal gains, which the evaluation fixes and never varies, and the brake-to-rest
policy is the one that tracks the reference most closely: its mean tracking error, $0.29$ to $0.34$\,m,
is the smallest of any configuration on this scene, and it is also the only one that never advances along
the route. A controller that follows a reference tightly while making no progress along it is not failing
to track; it is failing to move. On a scene whose reference demands sustained forward speed, a setpoint
of zero velocity at every plan step is what produces both that standstill and the loss of attitude that
ends the flight.

Changing the controller is not sufficient once projection changes the plan. With per-step projection the
plan succeeds on none of three flights under MuJoCo MPC and on none of ten under the brake-to-rest
controller, and no flight succeeds within the constraints. The unprojected experiment isolates a limit of
the controller; the projected experiment remains a failure of the planner, the projection and the
controller together. UAV-s-curve therefore supports no ordering of the generative models and no preferred
projection method, and the ordering on the two other scenes is to be read for the controller with which it
was measured.

The other reason MuJoCo MPC replaces the controller here and nowhere else is what it costs to run. The
time reported in every other table of this chapter is measured around the planner and stops there
(\autoref{sec:setup:metrics:avoiding}), so it prices no controller at all; pricing one means reading the
elapsed time of the flights themselves and dividing it by the control steps that executed
(\autoref{tab:uav-controller-cost}). On the unprojected arm, where nothing lies between the plan and the
controller, a control step takes $94.9$\,ms under the brake-to-rest controller and $215.6$\,ms under
MuJoCo MPC. The planner's share of that is the same in both, between $88$ and $90$\,ms at $\nfe=10$, so
the difference belongs to the controller: about $6$\,ms per step for the cascaded geometric law against
about $126$\,ms for MuJoCo MPC, which solves an optimisation problem at every control step where the
other evaluates an algebraic law. The controller that flies this scene is about twenty times the price
of the one that does not, and by itself it costs more per control step than generating the plan --- some
seven times what the operating point of D3IL-avoiding spends on an entire action
(\autoref{tab:avoiding-conclusion}).

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:uav:conclusion}

UAV-corridor shows that the flow-based planners fly under constraints: analytic and
consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching with per-step projection at $\nfe=3$ succeed
collision-free on every flight, at a sixth of the time of the diffusion baseline, which stays collision-free
but never reaches the finish line. \flawed{The UAV-pillars sentence that follows is disabled, v3.65.}
UAV-pillars, on which the plans are feasible before projection, ranks
nothing among the three flow-based models: instantaneous-velocity matching with endpoint projection succeeds
collision-free on all ten flights at $78$\,ms per control step, analytic average-velocity matching on nine
of ten in a shorter path, and at $\nfe=2$ the order is reversed; the one ordering that holds in every cell
is against the diffusion baseline, $0.30$ at best at $4.4$\,s per control step. UAV-s-curve has no
supported combination; its limit is the tracking controller, and the scene's flights end with the vehicle
inverted rather than off course. What the
quadrotor benchmark supports is therefore UAV-corridor's result, and it is a strong one: a test-time constraint the demonstrations do not satisfy,
under which both average-velocity models at $\nfe=3$ succeed collision-free on every flight, and the
diffusion baseline on none.
