\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

Results are reported entry by entry, and each entry opens by stating its regime
(\autoref{sec:setup:tasks}), because the regime determines which metric is a ranking instrument
there. The three entries do not carry equal weight and the chapter does not pretend they do: the
state-based benchmark is multi-seed and carries the principal claim; the vision-conditioned entry is
single-seed but paired, and carries a decisive engine result and one citable projector result; the
aerial entry carries the embodiment argument and a new safety and cost result, and its \emph{engine
ranking} rests on one seed of one scene and says so in every sentence.

\section{State-Based Manipulation}\label{sec:res:state}

This is the foundation. It is the benchmark the baseline reports on, the only entry with multi-seed
data, and the entry on which the thesis's principal claim rests. Its geometries are
\textbf{discriminating}: the unprojected arm violates the constraint set, so success and constraint
satisfaction ranks arms here rather than collapsing to plain success.

\subsection*{The principal result}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{At a step budget of one, the MeanFlow engine Pareto-dominates the pinned baseline:
higher success and constraint satisfaction, fewer control steps, and thirty-one times less compute
per step, on the same backbone at the same parameter count.}
\end{quote}

\autoref{tab:state-headline} gives it. The comparison is architecture-matched --- a 4.0\,M temporal
U-Net against the baseline's 4.0\,M temporal U-Net --- so it is not confounded by capacity, and it
satisfies \autoref{def:improvement} in its strong form: equal-or-better on \emph{every} axis, with
no trade-off to negotiate.

The margins are of two different kinds and should be read differently. The compute margin ---
$31\times$ per step at $\nfe = 1$, $21\times$ at $\nfe = 2$, and $35\times$ end-to-end per episode
--- is enormous relative to any plausible measurement noise, and its mechanism is not in doubt: the
baseline must evaluate its network twenty times per plan because its step budget was fixed when it
was trained, and the MeanFlow engine evaluates it once. The quality margin, 0.993 against 0.983, is
\emph{within} the across-seed standard error of roughly 0.07 and is therefore not a claim that
MeanFlow is safer. \guard{What \autoref{def:improvement} requires on that axis is equal-or-better,
not better, and 0.993 against 0.983 satisfies it; the claim is dominance, and the quality axis is
its gate rather than its margin.}

\guard{The coverage asymmetry stated in \autoref{sec:setup:baselines} applies to this table.} The
MeanFlow rows are complete at five seeds on all three geometries, 300 episodes; the baseline
completed five seeds on the two single-halfspace geometries and only seed~6 on \emph{both-hard}, 220
episodes. The geometry-first aggregation is what makes the two rows comparable at all, and the
deficit is on the baseline's hardest geometry, which is the one where a single seed is least
representative.

that this is a claim about cost and must be written as one.

On both fully measured geometries this is dominance on all three axes, at $21\times$ lower wall

own sake: \autoref{sec:res:visual} finds the two engines \emph{tied} on plan quality, and an
unqualified quality claim here would contradict it.

\subsection*{Decomposing the twenty-one-fold factor}

The obvious objection to \autoref{tab:state-fm} is that the comparison is not budget-matched: flow
matching is given a tenfold smaller budget and still wins on wall clock, so how much of the factor is
the engine and how much is simply fewer network evaluations? Running the same flow-matching
checkpoints at the baseline's own budget of twenty answers it.

At matched budget, \textbf{the two engines are a wash on success}. Paired by seed across three
projection rules on both complete geometries, thirty pairs, the pooled difference is $-0.012$ with a
standard deviation of $0.113$ --- indistinguishable from zero. Flow matching wins
\emph{top-right-hard} on all three rules by $+0.04$ to $+0.07$ and loses \emph{top-left-hard} by
$-0.03$ to $-0.11$, and the entire top-left deficit is one seed. On compute at matched budget it is
only $1.24$--$1.33\times$ cheaper, and that comes almost entirely from cheaper constraint solves
rather than from cheaper generation: per-evaluation generation cost differs by nine per cent
($0.0087$\,s against $0.0095$\,s), as it must for two networks of the same size.
\dataref{DA\_20260819 \S1b, job 25006}

The factor therefore decomposes into a modest model advantage of about $1.4\times$ at equal budget,
and about $15\times$ from the fact that flow matching \emph{can} run at a budget of two and the
baseline cannot. \textbf{The second factor is the result}, and it is a statement about the budget
ladder rather than about the engine --- which is what \autoref{sec:res:fewstep} is about.

\subsection*{An engine-independent finding about the selection rules}

One row of \autoref{tab:state-headline} deserves separate mention because it is a defect, not a
result. On the MeanFlow flagship the cumulative-projection-cost rule stalls: 98.0 control steps at a
budget of two and 72.0 at a budget of one, against roughly 61 for every other tightened arm, and at
a lower success and constraint satisfaction as well (0.927 and 0.943 against 0.993). The same
signature appears on the bootstrapped target and does \emph{not} appear on the baseline, despite all
three sharing a backbone. The sign of the effect therefore tracks the projection-cost landscape
rather than the architecture. This is why \autoref{tab:state-headline} names its selection rule
rather than reporting a best-over-rules number, and it is picked up in
\autoref{sec:res:ablations}.

\section{Few-Step Engines}\label{sec:res:fewstep}

\subsection*{The step budget is inference-time for one family and training-time for the other}

\autoref{fig:k-ladder} is the figure this chapter turns on. It plots success and constraint
satisfaction against the step budget, and the shape of the two families is categorically different:
the transport engines are \emph{flat} in the budget down to a single network evaluation, while the
diffusion baseline collapses below the budget it was trained at.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_avoiding_k_ladder}
  \caption[Quality against step budget]{Success and constraint satisfaction against the step budget
  on the state-based benchmark, geometry-mean over all three geometries, 4.0\,M temporal U-Net
  throughout. Solid: this work's protocol, 5 seeds $\times$ 20 trials. Dashed with hollow markers:
  the baseline at its own published protocol, 5 seeds $\times$ 2 trials --- shown because it is the
  only measurement of what the diffusion engine does below its training budget, and drawn
  differently because it is a different protocol. The baseline is plotted on the
  cumulative-projection-cost rule, which is also the rule its pinned configuration uses; the
  transport engines are on temporal consistency.}
  \label{fig:k-ladder}
\end{figure}

The asymmetry is structural rather than empirical. For the diffusion engine the number of network
evaluations is a property of the trained model: the noise schedule is discretised at training time,
and sampling with fewer steps than were trained is sampling from a different chain. For the
transport engines the budget is a property of the \emph{integrator}, chosen at inference; the
learned object is a velocity field, and the average-velocity objective is what makes a single Euler
step of that field a usable plan rather than a coarse one.

Measured, at the published protocol, the baseline scores $0.667$ at a budget of one and $1.000$ at
ten and at twenty; the transport engines hold $0.99$--$1.00$ from one to twenty.
\guard{The low-budget baseline cells are ten episodes per cell, a resolution of $\pm 0.10$. The
\emph{direction} is far outside that resolution --- $0.667$ against $1.000$ is not a
ten-episode artefact --- but the exact value is not, and the sentence this licenses is a mechanism
sentence, not a powered comparison.} A clean budget sweep of the baseline at this work's protocol is
the single highest-value outstanding run on this entry (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol}).

\subsection*{What this does and does not order within the transport family}

Within the transport family the budget ladder is flat enough that it does \emph{not} order the
engines on this entry: at every budget from one to twenty, all three transport rows sit between
$0.97$ and $1.00$, inside seed noise of each other. The separation between them is a
\emph{cost} separation, and it is reported as such in \autoref{tab:state-lowk}. The entry on which
the few-step objective separates the engines on \emph{quality} is the vision-conditioned one, where
the task is hard enough to have the range, and that result is in \autoref{sec:res:visual}.

One negative observation belongs here. Raising the budget does not monotonically improve plain flow
matching: its worst-geometry score goes $0.99$, $1.00$, $1.00$, $0.91$ across budgets of one, two,
five and twenty. The drop is not spread over seeds --- four of five stay between $0.90$ and $1.00$
and one falls to $0.65$ on one geometry, having scored a clean $1.00$ there at every lower budget.
So it is one checkpoint misbehaving once the trajectory is integrated finely, not a property of the
engine. \dataref{DA\_20260819, bonus finding}

\section{Constraint Arms}\label{sec:res:constraints}

The two constraint arms differ in exactly one thing --- the point handed to an identical projection
operator. Per-step \emph{iterate} projection applies it to the sampler's current iterate, which at
early transport times is not yet a plan; in-ODE \emph{endpoint} projection applies it to the
predicted clean endpoint and then damps the correction back
(\autoref{sec:method:hardflow}). \textbf{The citable comparison differs per entry and the three are
never pooled}, because they occupy different regimes and different budgets.

\subsection*{On the state-based benchmark: a cost crossover, at a low budget}

Here the arm comparison is a \emph{side topic} rather than the entry's headline, and it is the least
powered result in it. At matched budget, in the same job, on the same checkpoint, the endpoint arm
performs \emph{more} constraint solves than the iterate arm and costs about half as much per step:
$0.0745$\,s against $0.1478$\,s at a budget of three, $0.1304$ against $0.2268$ at five.
\autoref{fig:projector-cost} shows the crossover.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_avoiding_projector_cost}
  \caption[Cost of the two constraint arms]{Per-step wall clock of the two constraint arms against
  the step budget, MeanFlow on the 4.0\,M temporal U-Net, activation threshold fully open,
  temporal-consistency tightened geometry, one job. The shaded band is the regime in which the
  endpoint arm executes a single genuine step and is therefore not running the mechanism it names
  (\autoref{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}); its markers there are hollow.}
  \label{fig:projector-cost}
\end{figure}

At a budget of three --- the lowest citable one --- the endpoint arm reaches perfect success and
constraint satisfaction with zero executed violations at $58.88$ control steps and $0.0745$\,s per
step, against the pinned baseline's $1.000$, $70.13$ and $0.5534$\,s. That is sixteen per cent fewer
control steps at $7.4\times$ less compute, architecture-matched, and it satisfies
\autoref{def:improvement}.
\dataref{temp/0609/I/batch\_avoiding\_combined\_20260906\_125724, job 25444; DA\_20260906 \S3}

\provisional{This row is underpowered and must not be quoted as the entry's headline. It rests on
24 rollouts --- 4 seeds $\times$ 3 geometries $\times$ 2 trials --- and \textbf{seed 6 is absent},
while the pinned baseline pools five seeds, so the comparison is not seed-matched. The
\emph{timing} differences survive that: they are factors of two and seven against run-to-run
variation of a few per cent. The \emph{safety} difference does not: $1.000$ against $0.917$ is two
rollouts out of 24 and is not significant. The powered version is a budget sweep at this work's
protocol across all five seeds.}

The mechanism follows from the name and is worth stating because it predicts the sign. The endpoint
is near-feasible at every transport time, so the solver starts close to its answer and converges in
few iterations; the iterate is off-manifold early, so it does not. \guard{This mechanism is
currently \emph{inferred from timing}: per-solve time and iteration counts are not exported for the
iterate arm, so the inference is from total cost divided by solve count rather than from measured
solver work.}

\subsection{Degenerate and Thin Configurations}\label{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}

\autoref{sec:method:degenerate} derives a condition under which the in-loop guided sampler is not
guided at all: the number of \emph{genuine} steps --- active steps that are not the terminal one ---
is
\begin{equation*}
  n_{\text{gen}} \;=\; \max\!\big(\nfe - \lfloor (1-\eta)\,\nfe \rfloor,\, 1\big) - 1 ,
\end{equation*}
and where it is zero, the endpoint arm collapses to sample-then-project: at the terminal step the
predicted endpoint \emph{is} the iterate, so the two arms compute the same thing.

\textbf{This is a correctness condition for evaluation, and it was confirmed by the shipped code at
run time.} The evaluation printed its own thin-configuration banner at a budget of two with the
threshold fully open, reporting one active step and zero genuine ones, and ran with two and four
genuine steps at budgets of three and five. The count of constraint solves scaled $1 : 1.51 : 2.52$
across those three budgets against a prediction of $1 : 1.5 : 2.5$.
\dataref{job 25444 evaluation log, 2026-09-06}

Three consequences bind the rest of this chapter. \emph{First}, the budget floor is a pair --- the
budget and the activation threshold together --- and not a budget: a budget of one is structurally
impossible for the endpoint arm at \emph{every} threshold. \emph{Second}, every degenerate row in
this thesis is tagged and never averaged into a mean with non-degenerate rows. \emph{Third}, and
most consequentially outside this work, a published-style comparison of a guided sampler run below
this threshold measures sample-then-project while reporting it as guidance. The condition is cheap
to check and this thesis reports it for every configuration it evaluates; that this is \emph{our}
condition and not the source method's is stated in \autoref{sec:rel:safety}.

\section{Visual Observations}\label{sec:res:visual}

This entry adds two increments to the foundation at once --- three-dimensional control and
perception in place of state --- and \autoref{sec:disc:limitations} says plainly what that costs the
transfer argument. Its regime is \textbf{all-fail}: no arm satisfies the constraint set, success and
constraint satisfaction is floored on every arm including the baseline, and ranking therefore uses
the funnel of \autoref{sec:setup:metrics}. Its protocol is ten enumerated contexts on one seed, which
makes every comparison paired and every test below an exact one.

\subsection*{The engine result, which is decisive}

On plan quality --- funnel stage one, evaluated on the unguided arm, from an initial distance of
$0.4530$\,m:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MeanFlow beats the diffusion baseline decisively.} Median final distance $0.0741$\,m
    against $0.4140$\,m; paired difference $-0.3744$\,m with the baseline better in \emph{none} of
    the ten matched contexts, $p = 0.0020$ --- the smallest attainable two-sided value at ten pairs
    --- and at $0.64\times$ the per-step cost. \dataref{Gen14 CLOSURE\_20260907 R.1}
  \item \textbf{MeanFlow beats plain flow matching.} Paired difference $+0.216$\,m, nine of ten
    contexts, $p = 0.0039$; flow matching freezes in five of ten contexts against MeanFlow's one.
  \item \textbf{Flow matching and the baseline are tied.} $0.0055$\,m apart inside a band of roughly
    $0.4$\,m, $p = 1.0000$. \guard{This is why \autoref{sec:res:state} states the flow-matching
    result as a cost claim: the quality version of it is refuted here, and the two entries would
    otherwise contradict each other.}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The few-step objective, not transport, is what separates.} That is the cleanest statement of
the engine result in this thesis, and this is the entry that makes it, because it is the entry with
the dynamic range to separate anything at all.

\subsection*{Flat in the budget, and why}

MeanFlow improves as the budget rises, from $0.2354$\,m at two evaluations to $0.0741$\,m at twenty,
while the bootstrapped target is flat or worsening on both of its floors --- $0.3738 \to 0.3918$\,m
at $\alpha = 0.2$ and $0.3978 \to 0.4488$\,m at $\alpha = 0.05$. The derived mechanism is that the
bootstrapped target closes its finite difference with the model's own frozen output, which
contributes a field bias of order $(1-\alpha)$ that does not shrink as the interval does; the bias
is therefore budget-independent, and raising the budget cannot integrate it away.
\dataref{Gen14 CLOSURE\_20260907 \S5.1}

\subsection*{The projector result: one operating point, and it is not the largest budget}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{At a step budget of ten, in-ODE endpoint projection delivers the baseline's safety and the
baseline's distance at about 325\,ms per step less --- a clean sweep of nine matched pairs at
$p = 0.0039$.}
\end{quote}

That is the one citable projector claim on this entry and it is a \emph{latency} claim at equal
safety. It is strong because it is a clean sweep, not because it is large. At a budget of twenty the
same comparison is at parity on latency ($p$ between $0.18$ and $1.00$), and the apparent safety
advantage there --- ten of ten against nine of ten zero-violation rollouts --- is \emph{one
discordant rollout} at $p = 1.0000$ and is not quoted as a result anywhere in this thesis.
\dataref{Gen14 CLOSURE\_20260907 \S8.1; H10 \S10.4}

What \emph{is} separately true at the largest budget is a property of the configuration rather than
a margin: the endpoint arms are the only arms in the corpus that reach a zero-violation rate of
$1.000$ with zero violations \emph{while still moving the box}. The flagship cell is MeanFlow at a
budget of twenty on tightened geometry, at $275.3$\,ms per step, with two of ten contexts untouched;
the baseline reaches comparable safety only at $2157.9$\,ms per step, $7.8\times$ more. One baseline
variant also reaches $1.000$ --- by freezing in ten contexts out of ten, which disqualifies it, and
that has to be said in the same breath as the number.
\dataref{H10 \S10.5}

\provisional{One table is held. The constraint comparison against the pinned baseline cannot be
completed, because the baseline has no tightened cell at a budget of twenty on this entry. What is
published here is the plan-quality ranking and the flagship cell; the constraint comparison against
the baseline waits on one evaluation.}

\guard{Everything in this section is one seed at ten contexts.} The constraint-family ablations
present in the corpus are ablations and are never ranked against legal arms: they win by not
enforcing. Minima are single rollouts against a measured reproducibility floor of about $0.4$\,m,
which is the scale of the quantity being compared, so no minimum here is an estimate.

\section{Aerial Trajectory Planning}\label{sec:res:uav}

\subsection*{What this entry is for}

The aerial entry answers the embodiment half of \textbf{RQ4}, and its contribution is the transfer
itself: a planner and a projector developed against a fully actuated, statically stable,
kinematically controlled manipulator are carried onto an underactuated, open-loop-unstable,
second-order plant, and made to fly through static obstacle fields under a cascaded geometric
controller at 33\,Hz planning against 100\,Hz physics. That transfer is the result, and it holds
independently of which engine ranks highest on it.

\guard{Every ordering sentence in this section rests on seed 6 at ten rollouts on one scene. A
difference of 0.1 in mean success and constraint satisfaction there is one rollout.} The chapter is
written; the ranking is not banked.

\subsection*{The scenes occupy three different regimes, and only one of them ranks}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{corridor} scene is \textbf{all-pass} and \emph{constraint-trivial}: violations
    read zero and collision-free completion reads one on every arm, projected and unprojected alike,
    while the same counters report violations in the hundreds on the pillars scene. Success and
    constraint satisfaction therefore reduces to plain success there, and the engine ordering it
    produces is statistically empty --- a spread of 2.4 control steps against a standard deviation
    of 4.5 to 5.0, with the paired split five--five. It is not used to rank. \emph{That the projector
    spends 155--202\,ms per step removing violations that never existed is a genuine finding, and it
    is taken up in \autoref{sec:disc:threats}.}
  \item The \textbf{pillars} scene at a budget of five is \textbf{discriminating} and carries the
    ranking.
  \item The \textbf{s-curve} scene is \textbf{all-fail} and is retained as the \emph{controller-limit
    case} rather than as a ranking scene, which is a stronger use of it. Its ceiling is $0.100$
    across forty configurations and no arm clears the second stage of the funnel; raising the budget
    beyond five is non-monotone and does not rescue it. An earlier engine ordering on this scene was
    \textbf{tracker-confounded and is retracted} --- which is itself the evidence that the scene
    measures the controller and not the planner. \autoref{sec:disc:limitations} treats it as the
    edge of the control stack.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The engine ranking, on the one scene that discriminates}

Averaged over seventeen matched configurations on the pillars scene at a budget of five, mean
success and constraint satisfaction is $0.635$ for MeanFlow, $0.359$ for plain flow matching and
$0.235$ for the bootstrapped target; ceilings are $1.000$, $0.900$ and $0.700$; configurations at or
above $0.8$ number seven, two and \emph{zero} of seventeen respectively.
\dataref{Gen15 CLOSURE\_20260910 \S2}

\provisional{Seed 6, ten rollouts, one scene. This is the ordering the corpus shows and it is not
banked; two to three additional seeds on this scene are the one required run for this entry.}

Two things about it are worth stating even at that strength. It is the fourth environment in which
the bootstrapped target does not improve on MeanFlow, and the first in which it finishes
\emph{behind plain flow matching} --- on the one aerial scene with dynamic range, and it is the only
engine with no configuration above $0.8$. And the corridor scene's empty ordering reproduces, in a
second environment and independently, the same absence of power that the state-based benchmark shows
at low budget: same nominal order, same failure to separate. Two environments agreeing on a
\emph{non}-result is worth more than either alone.

\subsection*{The projector on the aerial entry: cost, and a safety finding that is new}

Endpoint projection is $8.2$--$21.3\times$ cheaper to project than full-geometry iterate projection,
and $5.6$--$12.4\times$ cheaper end to end, at parity on success and constraint satisfaction, with a
non-dominated tally of eight to nine with one tie.
\guard{Against the geometry-ablated variants of the iterate arm the two cost about the same
(roughly 33.7 against 34.8\,ms), and there the endpoint arm's more expensive generator makes it
about 27 per cent \emph{more} expensive end to end in all nine pairs. The cost advantage is against
the full-geometry projector, and the text says which.}

The finding this entry contributes that no other entry could is about safety:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Every unsafe rollout in the study is an iterate-projection rollout.} Endpoint projection
produced zero unsafe rollouts in 210; iterate projection produced 43 in 270. Seven of the eight
failing configurations use the \emph{random} selection rule, which is simultaneously the most
expensive projection row on every engine.
\end{quote}

\dataref{Gen15 T3 \S6; CLOSURE\_20260910 \S5}

\guard{One scene, one seed.} It feeds the selection-rule argument of
\autoref{sec:res:ablations} in a way the other two entries could not: the random rule is not merely
redundant here, it is the only configuration that put the aircraft in an unsafe state, at the highest
projection cost.

Two qualifications complete the entry. Projection is not universally worth paying for: unprojected
scores $0.000$ for the bootstrapped target, where projection is essential, $0.900$ for MeanFlow,
where it is marginal, and $0.900$ for plain flow matching, whose best configuration is unprojected
--- there, projection \emph{hurts}. And the single configuration reaching $1.000$ in the entire
family is MeanFlow on a \textbf{geometry-ablated} projector: that ablation removes the geometric rows
from the constraint program, violations are still measured against true geometry so the pass is
real, but the cell demonstrates \emph{the generator}, not the constrained projector, and is labelled
as such wherever it appears.

untestable on this entry; that half of the claim rests on the state-based benchmark. Reference rows
for the baseline are in flight and, when they land, will be a reference for scale at deliberately
unmatched budgets --- not a proof.}

\section{Ablations}\label{sec:res:ablations}

\subsection*{The selection machinery, at a fan of one, is exactly redundant}

When the candidate fan is one, the three selection rules do not merely agree on average --- they are
\textbf{bit-identical in all fifteen paired blocks}, on success and constraint satisfaction and on
control steps alike, for every engine tested: the baseline, MeanFlow on both backbones, plain flow
matching at two activation thresholds, and the bootstrapped target. This is predicted by the
implementation, which selects index zero when there is only one candidate, and confirmed exactly
rather than statistically. Every fan-one evaluation in this project therefore evaluates three
rules that are the same rollout, and dropping to one is a threefold saving on the projection stage
with no information lost.
\dataref{DA\_20260827 \S6}

\subsection*{The fan is free on the generator and linear on the projector}

Decomposing per-step cost into generation and projection shows that the fan does not touch the
generator --- drawing four candidates instead of one is a single batched forward pass, and generation
moves by at most six per cent --- while projection scales by $3.2$--$4.9\times$ against an ideal of
four, the shortfall being fixed per-solve overhead. The reason is in the implementation: the
constraint program is solved in a serial loop, one solver call per candidate, on the CPU.
\srcnote{diffuser/sampling/projection.py:132}

The engineering consequence is that \textbf{the fan should be parallelised rather than shrunk}. On
the flagship, parallelising four candidates would deliver the same latency as a fan of one
($\approx 11.7$\,ms per step at a budget of one, against $18.1$ today) while keeping all four
candidates and the banked success rate, whereas shrinking the fan buys the same latency and risks the
number the principal claim rests on. \guard{The per-candidate cost model behind that figure is
derived from two measured points, and the achievable speed-up depends on available cores, so it is a
prediction to be measured rather than a result.}

\subsection*{Which rule should be the default}

The cumulative-projection-cost rule never earns its cost on the state-based benchmark, where
temporal consistency is the right default and where the cost rule additionally carries the stall
reported in \autoref{sec:res:state}. \guard{The default is task-dependent, not universal:} the
random rule is the flagship rule on the vision-conditioned entry, and on the aerial entry it is the
only rule associated with unsafe rollouts. What generalises is the \emph{strong} form --- at a fan of
one the machinery is exactly redundant --- and not a single recommended rule.

\section{Summary of Findings}\label{sec:res:summary}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The engine substitution pays, and it pays through the budget.} On the state-based
    benchmark the MeanFlow engine Pareto-dominates the pinned baseline at a single network
    evaluation: better on success and constraint satisfaction, sixteen per cent fewer control steps,
    and $31\times$ less compute per step, architecture-matched, at 300 episodes against 220.
    Decomposed, about $1.4\times$ of the compute factor is the engine at equal budget and about
    $15\times$ is the budget the baseline cannot reach.
  \item \textbf{The budget is inference-time for transport and training-time for diffusion.} The
    transport engines hold their quality from twenty evaluations down to one; the baseline scores
    $0.667$ at one against $1.000$ at ten, at its own published protocol.
  \item \textbf{The few-step objective, not transport alone, is what separates the engines on
    quality.} Plain flow matching matches the baseline --- a \emph{cost} result on the state-based
    benchmark and an exact tie on the vision-conditioned one --- while MeanFlow beats the baseline
    decisively where the task has the range to show it ($-0.3744$\,m, none of ten contexts lost,
    $p = 0.0020$).
  \item \textbf{The bootstrapped target does not improve on MeanFlow.} It is a pair with MeanFlow
    where the task cannot separate them and behind it where the task can, in four environments, with
    a derived mechanism. \autoref{sec:disc:negative} gives it as a negative result rather than a gap.
  \item \textbf{The constraint-arm answer is regime-dependent and is stated per entry}: a cost
    crossover at a budget of three on the state-based benchmark, a $325$\,ms-per-step latency win at
    a budget of ten on the vision-conditioned entry, and an $8$--$21\times$ projection-cost advantage
    plus zero unsafe rollouts in 210 on the aerial entry.
  \item \textbf{A correctness condition for evaluating in-loop guided samplers}, derived and then
    confirmed by the instrumentation at run time: below a computable number of genuine steps the
    guided sampler is sample-then-project, and a comparison run there measures nothing.
  \item \textbf{The selection machinery is exactly redundant at a fan of one}, in all fifteen blocks
    and every engine, and the fan should be parallelised rather than removed.
\end{enumerate}

written, and should not be until the aerial seeds and the fifty-context evaluation land. Writing it
now would present three results of very different strengths in one visual register, which is exactly
what \autoref{sec:setup:metrics} exists to prevent.}

On the wall-clock figures throughout this chapter: they are indicative rather than a hardware
benchmark, for the reasons given with the machine in \autoref{sec:setup:protocol}. Every ratio
quoted has both of its sides measured on the same node under the same conditions, and the largest
ratios --- factors of twenty and thirty --- are far outside any plausible contention effect. The
factors near unity, in the matched-budget comparison specifically, are the ones for which that
caveat is load-bearing, and they are the ones this chapter declines to build a claim on.
