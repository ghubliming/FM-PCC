\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

The results follow the order of the study: D3IL-avoiding, the benchmark of \ac{DPCC}, first, then
the two environments built for this thesis, D3IL-aligning and the UAV scenes. \autoref{sec:res:summary} brings the three together and
answers, per environment, how the generative models and the projection methods compare. Each
environment is reported in those two parts and closes with the supported model--projection combination.
Each table states its backbone and its evaluation protocol.

% =============================================================================
\section{Obstacle Avoidance}\label{sec:res:state}
% =============================================================================

Every comparison in this section is made first at the protocol of \ac{DPCC} --- five training seeds and
two episodes per seed and geometry, ten episodes per geometry --- and then repeated with twenty episodes
per seed, one hundred per geometry, as stronger evidence (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:avoiding}). Results are
averaged per geometry and then across the three geometries. At every control step the planner generates
$B=4$ candidate plans and executes one of them, and every model is shown under each of the three rules
that make that choice (\autoref{sec:method:selection}): random selection ($r$), cumulative projection
cost ($c$) and temporal consistency ($t$). The models are then compared at their best rule.

The section has three parts. The generative models come first, each in the full pipeline with the
per-step projection of \ac{DPCC} held fixed, because that is what the benchmark measures; then the same
models with the projector switched off, which is where they differ most; then the two projection methods
against each other. Where the budget at which this benchmark is run leaves endpoint projection no
sampling step to act on, the projection comparison moves to the environments that run at larger budgets
(\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projection}).

\subsection{Generative Models under Per-Step Projection}\label{sec:res:avoiding:models}

Four generative models are compared. Three are flow-based --- instantaneous-velocity matching (FM),
analytic average-velocity matching (MeanFM) and consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching
(CI-MeanFM) --- and of those three the last two are average-velocity models, trained to predict the
average velocity over an interval rather than the velocity at an instant
(\autoref{sec:method:engine}). The fourth is the diffusion model of \ac{DPCC}, the baseline. All four
share the temporal U-Net of $4.0$\,M parameters.

Every number in this subsection is an executed-episode result with the per-step projection of \ac{DPCC}
active, because that is the pipeline the benchmark defines; the generative model is what varies and the
projector is held fixed. The models are compared without any projector in
\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:plans}, and the two projection methods against each other in
\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projection}.

What differs between the families, besides the training target, is what the step budget means. The budget
$\nfe$ counts network evaluations in both, but it is not the same quantity. For the diffusion baseline it
is the number of denoising steps of the reverse process, fixed when the noise schedule is discretised
during training, so each budget is a separately trained checkpoint
(\autoref{sec:method:diffusion}). For the flow-based models it is the number of steps the ODE solver
takes at inference, chosen per result without retraining (\autoref{sec:method:engine}).

\subsubsection{At the Protocol of DPCC}\label{sec:res:avoiding:dpccprotocol}

At its own protocol the diffusion baseline reproduces the published result
(\autoref{tab:avoiding-dpcc-protocol}, \autoref{tab:target}): with cumulative projection cost it reaches a
success rate with constraint satisfaction of $1.000$ in $70.1$ control steps at $553.4$\,ms per control
step. Temporal consistency reaches the same rate in $76.1$ steps, and random selection $0.967$. That row
--- the baseline at the budget it was trained with, under its own best rule --- is what every claim of this
chapter is measured against.

Two flow-based models clear it at one network evaluation, on all three of its axes at once.
Instantaneous-velocity matching with cumulative projection cost reaches $1.000$ in $67.0$ control steps at
$17.3$\,ms, and consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching with temporal consistency reaches
$1.000$ in $59.2$ control steps at $18.1$\,ms --- the fewest steps in the table. Neither gives up success
with constraint satisfaction, both finish in fewer control steps, and both compute an action in about a
thirtieth of the baseline's time, $32.0$ and $30.6$ times less. Both are the same temporal U-Net of
$4.0$\,M parameters as the baseline, so nothing in the comparison rests on a larger network. This is the
one place in the thesis where a flow-based model is ahead of the baseline on every reported axis at once,
rather than trading one against another.

The two differ in how much the selection rule matters to them. Instantaneous-velocity matching holds
$1.000$ under all three rules at one and at two evaluations --- the only model in the table that does ---
while consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching holds it under temporal consistency at both
budgets but falls to $0.900$ under random selection at two evaluations, and its cumulative-projection-cost
cell at that budget takes $89.7$ control steps. That is the stalling behaviour of the
cumulative-projection-cost rule described in \autoref{sec:res:ablations}, and it is the same effect that
costs analytic average-velocity matching $97.2$ steps one block above.

Analytic average-velocity matching is the model this benchmark is run at one network evaluation, and at
DPCC's own protocol it reaches $0.967$ with temporal consistency, in $58.6$ control steps at $18.3$\,ms per
control step. Against the baseline's best rule that is one episode of thirty fewer reaching the goal within
the constraints, $11.5$ fewer control steps, and a thirtieth of the time per control step. A second
evaluation gives the same $0.967$ in $59.4$ steps, and the rule matters more than the budget: with
cumulative projection cost the same model falls to $0.933$ and $72.4$ or $97.2$ control steps. At twenty
network evaluations instantaneous-velocity matching also reaches $1.000$ with both informed rules, in
$63.2$ and $63.7$ control steps against the baseline's $70.1$ and $76.1$, and at $477$ against $553$\,ms
per control step --- but at that budget the time advantage is a sixth, not a thirtieth, which is why the
one- and two-evaluation rows are the ones this section rests on.

\subsubsection{Extended Evaluation}\label{sec:res:avoiding:extended}

Ten times as many episodes change the picture in one respect: they show that on this benchmark the
selection rule matters more than the model (\autoref{tab:state-headline}).

Analytic average-velocity matching is the model most sensitive to it. With temporal consistency it reaches
$0.993$ at one network evaluation, in $61.0$ control steps; with cumulative projection cost, the rule
\ac{DPCC} itself selects, the same model falls to $0.943$ and $72.0$ steps, and at two evaluations that rule
picks plans that stall, $98.0$ steps. Instantaneous-velocity matching is indifferent: every rule puts it
between $0.990$ and $1.000$ at one and two evaluations. The diffusion baseline does best with cumulative
projection cost, $0.983$ in $69.0$ steps.

Two things follow. Temporal consistency is the rule for the flow-based models on this task, and it is used
for them throughout this chapter; and the differences between the models at their own best rule are small,
which is what \autoref{sec:res:avoiding:smoothness} takes up.

\subsubsection{The Cost Frontier}\label{sec:res:avoiding:pareto}

At equal success, two costs remain: how many control steps an episode takes, and how long one action takes
to compute. They are not exchangeable --- a plan that arrives in fewer steps but costs half a second per
step is not obviously better than the reverse --- so this section does not add them into one score. It
places every configuration on both axes at once and asks which ones nothing else beats on both
(\autoref{fig:avoiding-tradeoff}).

A configuration is \emph{non-dominated} when no other configuration in the comparison is at the same time
faster per action and shorter in control steps. Only configurations that keep the task solved take part:
a point enters the comparison when its success with constraint satisfaction is within $0.05$ of the best
value in its panel, so that a fast configuration that reaches the goal less reliably cannot appear as a
favourable trade. Reading the figure, colour identifies the model, marker shape the selection rule, the
number beside a point its step budget, and a ring marks each non-dominated point; down and to the left is
better on both axes.

The frontier is occupied by the flow-based models at their smallest budgets. Analytic average-velocity
matching at one network evaluation sits at the lower left: $61.0$ control steps at $18.1$\,ms, with no
configuration of any model both faster and shorter. Instantaneous-velocity matching at one and two
evaluations lies beside it, a little longer in steps at a similar time. Every diffusion configuration lies
to the right by more than an order of magnitude in time, and the baseline's own operating point, twenty
evaluations with cumulative projection cost, is neither the fastest nor the shortest point in the figure.
What the frontier shows is therefore not a single best configuration but a region --- one or two network
evaluations of a flow-based model --- inside which the remaining choices trade a control step against a
millisecond.

\subsubsection{Step Budget}\label{sec:res:fewstep}

The flow-based models keep their success with constraint satisfaction as the step budget falls to a
single network evaluation, while the diffusion baseline does not reach it at a small budget at all
(\autoref{fig:k-ladder}). At ten episodes per geometry the baseline reaches $0.667$ at $\nfe=1$ and
$1.000$ at $\nfe=10$ and $\nfe=20$; the flow-based models stay between $0.97$ and $1.00$ from $\nfe=1$ to
$\nfe=20$.

The two curves are not read the same way, and the difference is the point. Each diffusion point is its own
model: the number of denoising steps is fixed when the noise schedule is discretised during training, so
the budgets of that curve are three separately trained checkpoints, one per step count, and moving along
it costs a training run. The flow-based curve is one checkpoint per model, read at every budget, because
there the step count is the number of steps the ODE solver takes at inference. For a flow-based model the
budget is therefore a dial that can be turned after training, and for the diffusion baseline it is a
property of the checkpoint --- the same asymmetry \autoref{fig:raw-plans} shows, where the two diffusion
panels are two separately trained models and each flow-based row is one model read twice. The cost of
that asymmetry is a training run: the $\nfe=2$ panel of that figure required one, and the single-seed
evaluation it produced puts the baseline at about $225$\,ms per control step at two denoising steps,
against instantaneous-velocity matching's $17.3$\,ms at one. Cutting the budget from twenty to two does
not recover the gap, because what the budget buys back sits in the projector rather than in the
denoiser.

\subsection{Plans before Projection}\label{sec:res:avoiding:plans}

Everything above is an executed episode with the projector in the loop. This subsection removes it and
compares what the generative models themselves produce, which is where they differ most.

\subsubsection{Smoothness of the Plans}\label{sec:res:avoiding:smoothness}

Success, control steps and violating steps score the executed episode. Between the plan and that episode
sit two stages that both smooth what the generative model produced: the projection pulls a plan towards the
feasible set, and the resulting end-effector positions reach the robot through inverse kinematics and a
joint-space tracking law (\autoref{tab:platforms}, \autoref{sec:method:deployment}). The plans themselves
are the quantity these models differ in, and this section looks at them directly.

The stack between plan and motion has a wide tolerance, which \ac{DPCC} measures. Its authors vary the
sampling time assumed by the dynamics model inside the projection against the true sampling time and still
report success with constraint satisfaction of $0.99$ and $0.95$ at a factor of two in either direction,
and $0.86$ and $0.77$ at a factor of four \parencite[Table~2]{romer2025diffusion}. A model of the dynamics
wrong by a factor of four therefore still yields feasible motion in three quarters of episodes, a margin
wider than the distance between two generative models trained on the same data.

Smoothness is the property that margin hides, and it is one \ac{DPCC} values: replanning at every step from
a multimodal distribution can alternate between behaviour modes, which is what its selection rules are for,
and with them its authors obtain \enquote{a smoother behavior and shorter time to reach the goal}
\parencite[Sec.~5.4, Fig.~3]{romer2025diffusion}. It reaches the executed metrics only through the number of
control steps, and only once it is lost badly enough to lengthen the episode. On a platform whose controller
filters less, such as the quadrotor (\autoref{sec:res:uav:controller}), the executed motion follows the plan
more closely. Comparing the plans before projection therefore measures the models where they differ, and it
is where the flow-based objectives are strongest.

\subsubsection{The Plans Themselves}\label{sec:res:avoiding:raw}

Executed without projection, the models separate (\autoref{fig:raw-plans}). At one network
evaluation analytic average-velocity matching's successive plans form a narrow band around the executed path, each a bounded curve
towards the goal. The diffusion baseline at the same budget produces plans that jump across the
workspace from one control step to the next. Its executed path is nevertheless smooth, because the
tracking controller filters the plan --- which is the point: the smoothness of the executed path is the
controller's, not the model's. The analytic average-velocity matching panel at two evaluations shows that the second evaluation
does not remove this coherence, but it does not reduce the violation count in this episode.

The two objectives added to the figure fall between those extremes and in the order the projected results
give. Instantaneous-velocity matching throws a wide, jagged fan at both budgets, and much of its length
lies inside the forbidden halfspace on the left of the panel rather than in the free corridor.
Consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching is noisy at one evaluation and markedly tighter at two,
where its plans thread the obstacle field in a band comparable to the analytic objective's. Neither is as
tight as analytic average-velocity matching at the same budget, and all three are far from the near-scribble
of the baseline at one evaluation.

The baseline's two panels are two models, one trained at each budget, and the second denoising step is
what turns the scribble into a plan: at $\nfe=2$ its plans form a single coherent bundle from start to
goal. That bundle runs along the wrong side of the constraint. The same evaluation scores the
unprojected arm at $0.00$ success with constraint satisfaction with $20.5$ violating control steps per
episode, so what the second step buys is coherence and not feasibility --- which is the argument for the
projector rather than against the model.

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:avoiding:projection}

Endpoint projection \parencite{li2025hardflow} and per-step projection were run on the same analytic average-velocity matching
model, with the same solver and constraints, in one evaluation. Endpoint projection solves more
programs per control step and still takes about half the time: $0.0745$ against $0.1478$\,s at $\nfe=3$,
and $0.1304$ against $0.2268$\,s at $\nfe=5$ (\autoref{fig:projector-cost}). Its predicted endpoint is
close to the feasible set at every step, so the solver needs few iterations.

\subsubsection{Steps at Which Projection Guides Sampling}\label{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}

The guiding step count is defined in \autoref{sec:method:degenerate} and the convention for reporting a
configuration that has none in \autoref{sec:setup:protocol:guiding}. This section reports what the
evaluations measured against it.

The count holds. With the activation threshold at $1.0$ the evaluation ran with one guiding step at
$\nfe=2$, two at $\nfe=3$ and four at $\nfe=5$, and the number of programs it actually solved grew as
$1:1.51:2.52$ against the predicted $1:1.5:2.5$, so the configurations reported below are the ones the
prediction says they are.

Two settings in this chapter have no guiding step, and neither supports a statement about endpoint
projection. A budget of $\nfe=1$ has none at any threshold, which is why D3IL-avoiding at its operating
budget is reported with per-step projection only and why UAV-corridor has no endpoint row at that budget;
at the default threshold of $0.5$, $\nfe=2$ has none either, which is where the bit-identical rollouts
above come from. The rows of \autoref{tab:hf-ladder} at those two budgets are therefore printed for
completeness and carry no comparison between the methods.

\subsubsection{Candidate Plans and Selection Rules}\label{sec:res:ablations}

With a single candidate plan the three selection rules return the same plan: success and control steps
are identical in all fifteen paired seed--geometry combinations, for the diffusion baseline, analytic average-velocity matching,
instantaneous-velocity matching and consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching. With four candidates, generation time changes by at most six per
cent, because the four plans are one batched network evaluation, while projection time grows by a factor
of $3.2$ to $4.9$, because the programs are solved one after another on the CPU. Solving them in parallel
would bring analytic average-velocity matching at $\nfe=1$ from $18.1$ to about $11.7$\,ms per control step with all four
candidates kept; this is a prediction from two measured points.
\dataref{DA\_20260827 \S2, \S6, \S10.5}

Cumulative projection cost slows analytic average-velocity matching down on this task: $98.0$ control steps at $\nfe=2$ and $72.0$
at $\nfe=1$, against about $61$ with the other rules, at success rates of $0.927$ and $0.943$. The same
happens for consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching and not for the diffusion baseline. Temporal consistency is the
selection rule for the flow-based models on this task.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:avoiding:conclusion}

The supported operating point on D3IL-avoiding is a flow-based model at one or two network
evaluations with the per-step projection of DPCC. Analytic average-velocity matching at one evaluation
provides the five-seed result: $0.993$ success with constraint satisfaction in $61.0$ control steps and
$18.1$\,ms per control step. The single-seed comparison of all four models does show the advantage
remembered for consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching (\autoref{tab:state-models}): at full
success it uses $59.68$ rather than $60.17$ control steps and $0.0286$ rather than $0.2209$\,s per step.
This observed lead is slight on path length and large on time because the compared budgets are two and
five evaluations; it rests on a difference of three episodes and has not been repeated over five
training seeds. The two results therefore support the analytic objective as the multi-seed operating
point and the consistency-interpolated objective as the lowest-cost observed single-seed configuration.
Endpoint projection
is not needed at either operating budget. At one evaluation it has no guiding step, and at two it has
only one; raising the budget so that it can guide sampling gives up the low-step advantage that decides
this benchmark. D3IL-aligning and the UAV scenes below require larger budgets and therefore test the
projection method where it has several guiding steps.

\subsection{Generative Models}\label{sec:res:aligning:models}

(\autoref{tab:va-models}). Analytic average-velocity matching ends a median $0.0741$\,m from the target,
from a starting distance of $0.4530$\,m --- it closes $84\,\%$ of the distance --- and it is the only model
that moves the box in every context. The other three end between $0.39$ and $0.41$\,m, closing between $9$
and $17\,\%$, that is within a few centimetres of where the box started, and each leaves it untouched in
some contexts.

The margins behind that ordering are one-sided. Analytic average-velocity matching ends closer than the
diffusion baseline in all ten contexts, by $0.3744$\,m on average, at $0.64$ of the baseline's time per
control step, and closer than instantaneous-velocity matching in nine of ten, by $0.2333$\,m. Between
instantaneous-velocity matching and the baseline there is no ordering: their medians are $0.4085$ and
$0.4140$\,m, and each ends closer in about half the contexts. Instantaneous-velocity matching leaves the box
unmoved in four of ten contexts and the diffusion baseline in ten of thirty episodes.

\subsubsection{Step Budget}\label{sec:res:aligning:budget}

Analytic average-velocity matching improves with the budget: its median final distance falls from
$0.2354$\,m at $\nfe=2$ to $0.1194$\,m at $\nfe=10$ and $0.0741$\,m at $\nfe=20$. The diffusion model
reaches $0.1849$\,m only when it is trained and run with $100$ steps, at $1526.6$\,ms per control step,
eight times analytic average-velocity matching's time at $\nfe=20$ and still further from the target.
Unlike D3IL-avoiding, where one network evaluation already saturates the task (\autoref{sec:res:state}),
alignment rewards additional evaluations.
\dataref{va\_results.py; CLOSURE\_20260907 \S1, \S5.1}

\subsubsection{Consistency-Interpolated Average-Velocity Matching}\label{sec:res:aligning:consistency}

The consistency-interpolated objective behaves differently from the analytic one on this task: it does
not improve when it is given a larger budget. At two network evaluations the two are level --- each ends
closer in five of the ten contexts. At twenty, where the analytic objective has moved the box most of the
way to its target, the consistency-interpolated one has not moved at all from where it was at two
evaluations, and it ends further away in seven of ten contexts. Raising the budget spends time and buys
nothing, and the same holds for the more aggressive setting $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=0.05$.

The numbers behind that are flat where the analytic objective's fall. With
$\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=0.2$ the median final distance moves from $0.3738$ to $0.3897$\,m between two and
twenty evaluations, and with $\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}=0.05$ from $0.3978$ to $0.4488$\,m, against the analytic
objective's $0.2354$ to $0.0741$\,m.

The mechanism is in the training target. It is built from a stop-gradient copy of the network itself, so
part of the network's own error enters the field it learns, and that part does not shrink when the solver
takes smaller steps (\autoref{sec:method:alphaflow}). A task that rewards more evaluations therefore
exposes it, as this one does; a task already saturated at one evaluation, as D3IL-avoiding is, does not.
\dataref{va\_results.py; Gen14 DA\_20260907 Gate 1; CLOSURE\_20260907 \S3, R.4}
\guard{The level result at $\nfe=2$ is in distance only. Counting the contexts in which any
configuration reaches the target, analytic average-velocity matching succeeds in seven and
consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching in two (CLOSURE\_20260907 R.4).}

\subsubsection{Endpoint Projection across the Models}\label{sec:res:aligning:projection:models}

The comparison above is one generative model. Run across the three flow-based models at the budget each
is evaluated at, under every selection rule, it holds in the same direction
(\autoref{tab:va-projection-models}). Counting every comparison in which the two methods are run on the
same model, the same budget and the same rule --- eighteen in all, six from this table and twelve from
\autoref{tab:va-projection} --- endpoint projection keeps more contexts free of violations in fifteen,
and per-step projection in three. All three exceptions are under temporal consistency, and two of them
are the narrowest possible margin, one context.

That is the reverse of D3IL-avoiding, and the reason is the budget rather than the task. This task is
run at ten and twenty network evaluations because the generative model needs them
(\autoref{sec:res:aligning:budget}), so the endpoint solve has several guiding steps to act on, where on
D3IL-avoiding at one evaluation it had none (\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projection}). Given steps to act
on, the method does what it is for: it removes violations from the plan before the plan is finished,
rather than correcting each step after the fact.

What it does not do is dominate. Its advantage is on the constraint and not on the task: across the six
cells of \autoref{tab:va-projection-models} it moves the box in as many contexts as per-step projection
in four and in fewer in two, and its median final distances are level. Nor is it uniformly cheaper. At
$\nfe=10$ and $\nfe=20$ it costs between $0.6$ and $0.9$ of per-step projection's time per control step,
but at $\nfe=2$ it costs about three times as much, because a single interior-point solve over the whole
trajectory does not become cheap merely because the trajectory was generated in two steps. The honest
summary is that endpoint projection is the better projector on this task wherever it has a guiding step,
by a margin that is consistent rather than large, and at a comparable price.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:aligning:conclusion}

D3IL-aligning selects analytic average-velocity matching as the generative model: at twenty evaluations
it ends closer to the target than instantaneous-velocity matching and the diffusion baseline, whereas
consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching does not improve with the larger budget. It selects
endpoint projection as the projection method: at the large budget this task needs, endpoint projection
with random selection keeps all ten contexts free of violations and still moves the box in eight, at the
time per control step of per-step projection at $\nfe=20$ and at $37\,\%$ of it at $\nfe=10$. The
conclusion is limited to training-set contexts.

Two of the three scenes of \autoref{sec:setup:tasks:uav} are reported here, in increasing difficulty, and
each plays its own part. UAV-corridor shows that the planners fly under constraints at all, and it is the
scene this benchmark rests on; UAV-s-curve exposes a limit that belongs to none of the models, the tracking
controller, which \autoref{sec:res:uav:controller} treats on its own.

UAV-pillars is not reported. Its constraint set is the one its own demonstration generator was built to
satisfy: the demonstrated channels lie at $|y|=1.11$\,m because that is the pillar centre at $0.6$, plus
its radius $0.12$, plus the vehicle's rotor reach and a safety margin, and the projection then enforces the
same clearance the routes were laid out to keep (\autoref{sec:setup:data:uav}). The plan is feasible before
any projection runs, so what a projected configuration measures on that scene is how little of an already
feasible plan a method disturbs, and not whether a method can make an infeasible plan feasible. That is not
the question this chapter asks, so the scene is left out rather than reported against a question it does
not answer. It is being re-evaluated with the obstacle radius enlarged at test time, from the $0.12$\,m pillar the
simulator contains to a keep-out radius of $0.35$\,m --- the same physical field, demonstrations and
checkpoints, under a constraint the demonstrations do not satisfy, which is the construction

projection, scores $0.00$ with about $51$ violating control steps per flight, where the same unprojected
cell scored $0.90$ under the old constraint set.

\subsection{Caveat: The Tracking Controller}\label{sec:res:uav:controller}

On the manipulator, the tracking controller absorbs what the plan leaves rough
(\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:smoothness}). The quadrotor offers no such margin: it is underactuated and
open-loop unstable (\autoref{sec:bg:quadrotor}), and a plan reaches the rotors only through the cascaded
geometric controller that tracks it. That controller needs a velocity setpoint the plan does not supply,
and every result in this chapter uses the brake-to-rest policy of \eqref{eq:method:dep:vdes}: the setpoint
is zero, so the vehicle accelerates towards each new position and brakes to rest at it. The result of a flight is therefore a property of the planner and the controller
together, and UAV-s-curve, with its two sharp turns, is where the controller becomes the limit.

This is shown by keeping the plan and changing the controller (\autoref{tab:uav-controller}). Analytic
average-velocity matching at $\nfe=10$, executed without projection, passes the goal on none of ten flights
with the brake-to-rest controller and ends on average $2.81$\,m from the goal point of its route. With
MuJoCo MPC \parencite{howell2022predictive} as the tracking controller, the same plans end $0.299$, $0.298$
and $0.294$\,m from that point, and two of the three flights cross the finish line. The brake-to-rest
failures are not wall collisions: on all ten flights the vehicle stays at altitude until it loses attitude
control and inverts, between control steps $395$ and $421$, about $12$\,s into the flight. MuJoCo MPC flies
the same turn. The unprojected plan is flyable; the brake-to-rest controller cannot fly it.

What separates the two is the velocity setpoint and not the gains. Both controllers act on the same
vehicle with the same nominal gains, which the evaluation fixes and never varies, and the brake-to-rest
policy is the one that tracks the reference most closely: its mean tracking error, $0.29$ to $0.34$\,m,
is the smallest of any configuration on this scene, and it is also the only one that never advances along
the route. A controller that follows a reference tightly while making no progress along it is not failing
to track; it is failing to move. On a scene whose reference demands sustained forward speed, a setpoint
of zero velocity at every plan step is what produces both that standstill and the loss of attitude that
ends the flight.

Changing the controller is not sufficient once projection changes the plan. With per-step projection the
plan passes the goal on none of three flights under MuJoCo MPC and on none of ten under the brake-to-rest

projection method, and the ordering on the two other scenes is to be read for the controller with which it
was measured.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:uav:conclusion}

UAV-corridor shows that the flow-based planners fly under constraints: analytic and
consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching with per-step projection at $\nfe=3$ pass the goal
collision-free on every flight, at a sixth of the time of the diffusion baseline, which stays collision-free
but never reaches the finish line. UAV-s-curve has no supported combination; its limit is the tracking
controller, and the scene's flights end with the vehicle inverted rather than off course. What the
quadrotor benchmark supports is therefore UAV-corridor's result, and it is a strong one: a test-time constraint the demonstrations do not satisfy,
passed collision-free on every flight by both average-velocity models at $\nfe=3$, and by the diffusion
baseline on none.
