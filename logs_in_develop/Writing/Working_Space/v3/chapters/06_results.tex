\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

The results follow the order of the study: the obstacle-avoidance benchmark of \ac{DPCC} first, then
the two environments built for this thesis. \autoref{sec:res:summary} brings the three together and
answers, per environment, how the generative models and the projection methods compare. Each
environment is reported in those two parts and closes with the supported model--projection combination.
Each table states its backbone and its evaluation protocol.

% =============================================================================
\section{Obstacle Avoidance}\label{sec:res:state}
% =============================================================================

All results in this section use five training seeds with twenty episodes each and are averaged per
geometry, then across the three geometries (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:avoiding}).

The section reaches one operating point and stays there. On this benchmark MeanFlow reaches $0.993$
success with constraint satisfaction at a single network evaluation, which is the smallest budget a
model can be run at, and neither a second evaluation nor a larger one improves it. Two consequences
follow and are shown below: the remaining differences between the generative models cannot be read off
the success rates, because the projection and the tracking controller absorb them
(\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:raw}); and endpoint projection, which needs a step on which to act before the
last one, has none at this budget, so per-step projection is the method used here
(\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projection}).

\subsubsection{Step Budget}\label{sec:res:fewstep}

The flow-based models keep their success with constraint satisfaction as the step budget falls to a
single network evaluation, while the diffusion baseline loses it below the budget it was trained with
(\autoref{fig:k-ladder}). At ten episodes per geometry the baseline reaches $0.667$ at $\nfe=1$ and
$1.000$ at $\nfe=10$ and $\nfe=20$; the flow-based models stay between $0.97$ and $1.00$ from $\nfe=1$ to
$\nfe=20$. For the diffusion model the number of steps is fixed when the noise schedule is discretised
at training time; for the flow-based models it is chosen by the ODE solver at inference.

episodes. Their nearly horizontal shape shows that reducing the inference budget does not materially
change the flow-based models on this task. The dashed diffusion curve answers a narrower mechanism

\subsubsection{Plans before Projection}\label{sec:res:avoiding:raw}

Every number reported so far is measured after two stages that both smooth what the generative model
produced. The projection pulls a plan towards the feasible set, and the tracking controller turns the
remaining setpoints into joint commands through inverse kinematics. Both absorb incoherence, so two
models whose plans differ greatly can post similar success rates, step counts and violation counts.
The comparison in \autoref{tab:state-headline} therefore measures the tolerance of the control stack
as much as the quality of the plan.

How wide that tolerance is can be read off the baseline's own paper. \textcite{romer2025diffusion}
vary the sampling time assumed by the dynamics model inside the projection against the true sampling
time, and report success with constraint satisfaction of $0.99$ and $0.95$ when the assumption is wrong
by a factor of two in either direction, and $0.86$ and $0.77$ when it is wrong by a factor of four.
A dynamics model off by a factor of four still satisfies the constraints in three quarters of episodes.
Whatever passes through this stack is corrected by a margin far larger than the difference between two
generative models, which is why the plans themselves have to be looked at directly.

Executed without projection, the two models separate (\autoref{fig:raw-plans}). At one network
evaluation MeanFlow's successive plans form a narrow band around the executed path, each a bounded curve
towards the goal. The diffusion baseline at the same budget produces plans that jump across the
workspace from one control step to the next. Its executed path is nevertheless smooth, because the
tracking controller filters the plan --- which is the point: the smoothness of the executed path is the
controller's, not the model's. The MeanFlow panel at two evaluations shows that the second evaluation
does not remove this coherence, but it does not reduce the violation count in this episode.

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:avoiding:projection}

Endpoint projection \parencite{li2025hardflow} and per-step projection were run on the same MeanFlow
model, with the same solver and constraints, in one evaluation. Endpoint projection solves more
programs per control step and still takes about half the time: $0.0745$ against $0.1478$\,s at $\nfe=3$,
and $0.1304$ against $0.2268$\,s at $\nfe=5$ (\autoref{fig:projector-cost}). Its predicted endpoint is
close to the feasible set at every step, so the solver needs few iterations.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_avoiding_projector_cost}

  and endpoint projection against the step budget; MeanFlow, temporal U-Net with 4.0\,M parameters,
  activation threshold 1.0, temporal consistency, tightened. The shaded budget has a single guiding step
  (\autoref{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}).}
  \label{fig:projector-cost}
\end{figure}

At $\nfe=3$, endpoint projection reaches a success rate with constraint satisfaction of $1.000$ with no
violations, in $58.88$ control steps at $0.0745$\,s per step. The \ac{DPCC} baseline reaches $1.000$ in
$70.13$ steps at $0.5534$\,s per step.
\dataref{temp/0609/I/batch\_avoiding\_combined\_20260906\_125724, job 25444; DA\_20260906 \S3}
\provisional{This comparison uses four training seeds (7--10) $\times$ three geometries $\times$ two
episodes, 24 episodes per configuration. The difference in success with per-step projection at the same
budget, $1.000$ against $0.917$, is two episodes and not significant. The evaluation at five training
seeds and twenty episodes is pending.}

That result is at $\nfe=3$. The budget at which this task is actually run is lower, and there endpoint
projection does not execute its own arithmetic at all. With the activation threshold at $0.5$ the
guiding step count (\autoref{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}) is zero at $\nfe=1$ and at $\nfe=2$: the
only active step is the terminal one, where the predicted endpoint is the finished sample and the
method reduces to projection after sampling. This is measured, not inferred --- the evaluation records
one program solved per plan at both budgets, against three at $\nfe=5$ and five at $\nfe=10$, matching
the predicted count exactly. A run at $\nfe=2$ with both methods given the same number of candidate
plans produced bit-identical rollouts, at $2.45$ times the cost.

Across the budgets at which it does run, endpoint projection shows no consistent advantage on this task
(\autoref{tab:hf-ladder}). Untightened it reaches higher success with constraint satisfaction than all
three selection rules of per-step projection only at $\nfe=10$; on tightened constraints it does so only
at $\nfe=1$, where it runs no endpoint-projection arithmetic, and at $\nfe=10$ it is ahead of none of
them. Its one systematic advantage is control steps, and the mechanism is not the projection: with four
candidate plans the cumulative-projection-cost rule sometimes selects a stalled plan of 97 control steps,
and endpoint projection, given one candidate, cannot make that selection.

by raising the budget, which is the cost the flow-based models were adopted to avoid.

\subsubsection{Steps at Which Projection Guides Sampling}\label{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}

Endpoint projection needs sampling steps on which it is active and which are not the final step: only
there does a later step exist for the network to react to, so only there does projection steer the
sample being generated rather than correct a finished one. Their number, the \emph{guiding step count},
is
\begin{equation*}
  n\sidx{guide} \;=\; \max\!\big(\nfe - \lfloor (1-\eta)\,\nfe \rfloor,\, 1\big) - 1
\end{equation*}
(\autoref{sec:method:degenerate}). With $n\sidx{guide}=0$ endpoint projection computes the same plan as
per-step projection, because at the final step the predicted endpoint is the current sample. The
evaluation code reports the count at run time: with the activation threshold at $1.0$ it ran with one
guiding step at $\nfe=2$, two at $\nfe=3$ and four at $\nfe=5$, and the number of solved programs grew as
$1:1.51:2.52$ against the predicted $1:1.5:2.5$. A budget of $\nfe=1$ has no guiding step at any
threshold. Configurations with $n\sidx{guide}=0$ are reported separately and not averaged with others.
\dataref{job 25444 evaluation log, 2026-09-06}

\subsubsection{Candidate Plans and Selection Rules}\label{sec:res:ablations}

With a single candidate plan the three selection rules return the same plan: success and control steps
are identical in all fifteen paired seed--geometry combinations, for the diffusion baseline, MeanFlow,
flow matching and consistency training. With four candidates, generation time changes by at most six per
cent, because the four plans are one batched network evaluation, while projection time grows by a factor
of $3.2$ to $4.9$, because the programs are solved one after another on the CPU. Solving them in parallel
would bring MeanFlow at $\nfe=1$ from $18.1$ to about $11.7$\,ms per control step with all four
candidates kept; this is a prediction from two measured points.
\dataref{DA\_20260827 \S2, \S6, \S10.5}

Cumulative projection cost slows MeanFlow down on this task: $98.0$ control steps at $\nfe=2$ and $72.0$
at $\nfe=1$, against about $61$ with the other rules, at success rates of $0.927$ and $0.943$. The same
happens for consistency training and not for the diffusion baseline. Temporal consistency is the
selection rule for the flow-based models on this task.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:avoiding:conclusion}

The supported operating point on obstacle avoidance is a flow-based model at one or two network
evaluations with the per-step projection of DPCC. MeanFlow at one evaluation provides the strongest
multi-seed result: $0.993$ success with constraint satisfaction in $18.1$\,ms per control step. In the
single-seed comparison, consistency training at two evaluations is the least expensive configuration
that reaches $1.00$, but its advantage over MeanFlow is not statistically resolved. Endpoint projection
is not needed at either operating budget. At one evaluation it has no guiding step, and at two it has
only one; raising the budget so that it can guide sampling gives up the low-step advantage that decides
this benchmark. The alignment and quadrotor tasks below require larger budgets and therefore test the
projection method where it has several guiding steps.

MeanFlow improves with the budget: its median final distance falls from $0.2354$\,m at $\nfe=2$ to

\subsubsection{Consistency Training}\label{sec:res:aligning:consistency}

Consistency training is level with MeanFlow at the smallest budget and behind it at the largest, and it is
ahead of MeanFlow in neither. At $\nfe=2$, with the floor of $\alpha$ at $0.2$, the paired difference is
$-0.0102$\,m, five contexts each way, $p=1.0000$, and $+0.0240$\,m, $p=1.0000$, on the tightened
constraint set. At $\nfe=20$ it ends further from the target than MeanFlow by $0.2316$\,m on average, in
seven of ten contexts; the sign test does not reject ($p=0.3438$) and the permutation test does
($p=0.0234$). With the floor at $0.05$ the gap at $\nfe=20$ is $0.3426$\,m, in nine of ten contexts,
$p=0.0215$. At $\nfe=20$ consistency training is level with flow matching ($p=0.5078$) and with the
diffusion baseline ($p=1.0000$).

The reason is visible in the budget. MeanFlow improves from $\nfe=2$ to $\nfe=20$; consistency training does
not, moving from $0.3738$ to $0.3897$\,m with the floor at $0.2$ and from $0.3978$ to $0.4488$\,m with the
floor at $0.05$. Its training target is built from a stop-gradient copy of the network itself, so part of
that network's error is carried into the field and does not shrink as the step size does
(\autoref{sec:method:alphaflow}). Where more evaluations help, as here, that residual error decides the
comparison; where one evaluation already saturates the task, as on obstacle avoidance, it does not show.

configuration reaches the target, MeanFlow succeeds in seven and consistency training in two
(CLOSURE\_20260907 R.4).}

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:aligning:projection}

On MeanFlow at $\nfe=10$, endpoint projection matches the final distance of per-step projection and takes
$325$\,ms less per control step, in all nine contexts whose time differs ($p=0.0039$, random selection;
$-320$\,ms and $p=0.0039$ with cumulative projection cost; $-211$\,ms and $p=0.0391$ with temporal
consistency). At $\nfe=20$ the two methods take the same time ($p$ between $0.18$ and $1.00$). The
activation threshold is $0.4$ at $\nfe=10$ and $0.2$ at $\nfe=20$, which gives endpoint projection three
guiding steps at both budgets, above the degenerate case of \autoref{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}.

At $\nfe=20$ on tightened constraints, endpoint projection with random selection keeps every context free
of violations and moves the box in eight of ten, at $275.3$\,ms per control step; per-step projection with
the same rule is free of violations in nine contexts, with $2.7$ violating steps per episode, at
$265.9$\,ms. Executed without projection, the same plans violate a constraint on $31.6$ steps per episode.
\dataref{va\_results.py projection block; CLOSURE\_20260907 \S2, R.2}

The diffusion baseline with per-step projection was evaluated on the untightened constraint set only. There
it is free of violations in $0.10$ of the episodes, with $130$ violating steps per episode, leaves the box
unmoved in $21$ of $30$, and takes $2157.9$\,ms per control step.
\provisional{Without a tightened evaluation of the diffusion baseline at $\nfe=20$, no constraint comparison
between the baseline and the flow-based models is made on this task. Repeated evaluations of configurations
with projection vary by about $0.4$\,m in final distance, so a minimum over contexts describes one episode.}

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:aligning:conclusion}

The alignment results select MeanFlow as the generative model: at twenty evaluations it is closer to
the target than both flow matching and diffusion on the paired contexts, whereas consistency training
does not improve with the larger budget. On tightened constraints, the best observed combination is
MeanFlow with endpoint projection and random selection at twenty evaluations: it moves the box in eight
of ten contexts and keeps all ten free of violations. At ten evaluations endpoint projection also
matches the final distance of per-step projection while reducing the time per control step. This
conclusion is limited to one training seed and training-set contexts; the absent tightened-diffusion run
prevents a constraint-matched comparison with the baseline.

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:uav:projection}

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:uav:conclusion}

The preferred combination depends on the scene. On UAV-corridor, MeanFlow or consistency training with
per-step projection is the supported choice: both cross the finish line collision-free on all twelve
flights, whereas endpoint projection is faster but collision-free on at most two. On UAV-pillars, MeanFlow
is the strongest generative model across the eleven configurations, and MeanFlow with endpoint projection
and random selection is the best observed projected combination, with $0.90$ success with constraint
satisfaction and substantially lower projection time. UAV-s-curve has no supported best combination:
the controller determines whether an unprojected MeanFlow path can be flown, and no projected
configuration completes the task within the constraints. These conclusions use one training seed, so
the UAV-corridor and UAV-pillars choices are operating points for the reported experiments rather than
population-level rankings.

fraction of its time on D3IL-avoiding, ends level with it on D3IL-aligning, and completes UAV-corridor
where the projected baseline does not.

MeanFlow is ahead of both flow matching and the diffusion baseline in every environment, on the measure
each environment separates: control steps and time on D3IL-avoiding, final distance on D3IL-aligning,
collision-free completion on UAV-corridor, and success at the goal point on UAV-pillars. Flow matching is

with it on D3IL-aligning, and mixed on UAV-corridor. The full order MeanFlow $>$ flow matching $>$ diffusion
therefore holds on D3IL-avoiding and on UAV-pillars, and MeanFlow $>$ \{flow matching, diffusion\}
everywhere. Consistency training is not ahead of MeanFlow in any environment. Where the task separates them, its finite-difference target leaves part of the network's
own error in the field regardless of the step budget (\autoref{sec:method:alphaflow}).
