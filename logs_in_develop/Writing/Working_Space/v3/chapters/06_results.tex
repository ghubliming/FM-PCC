\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

The results follow the order of the study: D3IL-avoiding, the benchmark of \ac{DPCC}, first, then
the two environments built for this thesis, D3IL-aligning and the UAV scenes. \autoref{sec:res:summary} brings the three together and
answers, per environment, how the generative models and the projection methods compare. Each
environment is reported in those two parts and closes with the supported model--projection combination.
Each table states its backbone and its evaluation protocol.

% =============================================================================
\section{Obstacle Avoidance}\label{sec:res:state}
% =============================================================================

Every comparison in this section is made first at the protocol of \ac{DPCC} --- five training seeds and
two episodes per seed and geometry, ten episodes per geometry --- and then repeated with twenty episodes
per seed, one hundred per geometry, as stronger evidence (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:avoiding}). Results are
averaged per geometry and then across the three geometries. At every control step the planner generates
$B=4$ candidate plans and executes one of them, and every model is shown under each of the three rules
that make that choice (\autoref{sec:method:selection}): random selection ($r$), cumulative projection
cost ($c$) and temporal consistency ($t$). The models are then compared at their best rule.

The generative models come first and the projection methods second. Where the budget at which this
benchmark is run leaves endpoint projection no sampling step to act on, the projection comparison moves to
the environments that run at larger budgets (\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:projection}).

\subsection{Generative Models}\label{sec:res:avoiding:models}

Both sides of this comparison are generative models; what differs is the family and the number of network
evaluations each needs. The step budget $\nfe$ counts those evaluations in both families, but it is not
the same quantity. For the diffusion baseline it is the number of denoising steps of the reverse process,
fixed at $\nfe=20$ when the noise schedule was discretised during training, and the model is run at the
value it was trained with (\autoref{sec:method:diffusion}). For the flow-based models it is the number of
steps the ODE solver takes at inference, chosen per result without retraining
(\autoref{sec:method:engine}).

\subsubsection{At the Protocol of DPCC}\label{sec:res:avoiding:dpccprotocol}

At its own protocol the diffusion baseline reproduces the published result
(\autoref{tab:avoiding-dpcc-protocol}, \autoref{tab:target}): with cumulative projection cost it reaches a
success rate with constraint satisfaction of $1.000$ in $70.1$ control steps. Temporal consistency reaches
the same rate in $76.1$ steps, and random selection $0.967$. Instantaneous-velocity matching at the same budget of twenty
network evaluations reaches $1.000$ with both informed rules, in $63.2$ and $63.7$ control steps against the
baseline's $70.1$ and $76.1$, and at $477$ against $553$\,ms per control step.

Analytic average-velocity matching is the model this benchmark is run at one network evaluation, and at
DPCC's own protocol it reaches $0.967$ with temporal consistency, in $58.6$ control steps at $18.3$\,ms per
control step. Against the baseline's best rule that is one episode of thirty fewer reaching the goal within
the constraints, $11.5$ fewer control steps, and a thirtieth of the time per control step. A second
evaluation gives the same $0.967$ in $59.4$ steps, and the rule matters more than the budget: with
cumulative projection cost the same model falls to $0.933$ and $72.4$ or $97.2$ control steps.

\subsubsection{Step Budget}\label{sec:res:fewstep}

The flow-based models keep their success with constraint satisfaction as the step budget falls to a
single network evaluation, while the diffusion baseline loses it below the budget it was trained with
(\autoref{fig:k-ladder}). At ten episodes per geometry the baseline reaches $0.667$ at $\nfe=1$ and
$1.000$ at $\nfe=10$ and $\nfe=20$; the flow-based models stay between $0.97$ and $1.00$ from $\nfe=1$ to
$\nfe=20$. For the diffusion model the number of steps is fixed when the noise schedule is discretised
at training time; for the flow-based models it is chosen by the ODE solver at inference.

\subsubsection{Smoothness of the Plans}\label{sec:res:avoiding:smoothness}

Success, control steps and violating steps score the executed episode. Between the plan and that episode
sit two stages that both smooth what the generative model produced: the projection pulls a plan towards the
feasible set, and the resulting end-effector positions reach the robot through inverse kinematics and a
joint-space tracking law (\autoref{tab:platforms}, \autoref{sec:method:deployment}). The plans themselves
are the quantity these models differ in, and this section looks at them directly.

The stack between plan and motion has a wide tolerance, which \ac{DPCC} measures. Its authors vary the
sampling time assumed by the dynamics model inside the projection against the true sampling time and still
report success with constraint satisfaction of $0.99$ and $0.95$ at a factor of two in either direction,
and $0.86$ and $0.77$ at a factor of four \parencite[Table~2]{romer2025diffusion}. A model of the dynamics
wrong by a factor of four therefore still yields feasible motion in three quarters of episodes, a margin
wider than the distance between two generative models trained on the same data.

Smoothness is the property that margin hides, and it is one \ac{DPCC} values: replanning at every step from
a multimodal distribution can alternate between behaviour modes, which is what its selection rules are for,
and with them its authors obtain \enquote{a smoother behavior and shorter time to reach the goal}
\parencite[Sec.~5.4, Fig.~3]{romer2025diffusion}. It reaches the executed metrics only through the number of
control steps, and only once it is lost badly enough to lengthen the episode. On a platform whose controller
filters less, such as the quadrotor (\autoref{sec:res:uav:controller}), the executed motion follows the plan
more closely. Comparing the plans before projection therefore measures the models where they differ, and it
is where the flow-based objectives are strongest.

\subsubsection{Plans before Projection}\label{sec:res:avoiding:raw}

evaluation analytic average-velocity matching's successive plans form a narrow band around the executed path, each a bounded curve
towards the goal. The diffusion baseline at the same budget produces plans that jump across the
workspace from one control step to the next. Its executed path is nevertheless smooth, because the
tracking controller filters the plan --- which is the point: the smoothness of the executed path is the
controller's, not the model's. The analytic average-velocity matching panel at two evaluations shows that the second evaluation
does not remove this coherence, but it does not reduce the violation count in this episode.

\subsubsection{Steps at Which Projection Guides Sampling}\label{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}

Endpoint projection needs sampling steps on which it is active and which are not the final step: only
there does a later step exist for the network to react to, so only there does projection steer the
sample being generated rather than correct a finished one. Their number, the \emph{guiding step count},
is
\begin{equation*}
  n\sidx{guide} \;=\; \max\!\big(\nfe - \lfloor (1-\eta)\,\nfe \rfloor,\, 1\big) - 1
\end{equation*}
(\autoref{sec:method:degenerate}). With $n\sidx{guide}=0$ endpoint projection computes the same plan as
per-step projection, because at the final step the predicted endpoint is the current sample. The
evaluation code reports the count at run time: with the activation threshold at $1.0$ it ran with one
guiding step at $\nfe=2$, two at $\nfe=3$ and four at $\nfe=5$, and the number of solved programs grew as
$1:1.51:2.52$ against the predicted $1:1.5:2.5$. A budget of $\nfe=1$ has no guiding step at any
threshold. Configurations with $n\sidx{guide}=0$ are reported separately and not averaged with others.
\dataref{job 25444 evaluation log, 2026-09-06}

\subsubsection{Candidate Plans and Selection Rules}\label{sec:res:ablations}

With a single candidate plan the three selection rules return the same plan: success and control steps
are identical in all fifteen paired seed--geometry combinations, for the diffusion baseline, analytic average-velocity matching,
instantaneous-velocity matching and consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching. With four candidates, generation time changes by at most six per
cent, because the four plans are one batched network evaluation, while projection time grows by a factor
of $3.2$ to $4.9$, because the programs are solved one after another on the CPU. Solving them in parallel
would bring analytic average-velocity matching at $\nfe=1$ from $18.1$ to about $11.7$\,ms per control step with all four
candidates kept; this is a prediction from two measured points.
\dataref{DA\_20260827 \S2, \S6, \S10.5}

Cumulative projection cost slows analytic average-velocity matching down on this task: $98.0$ control steps at $\nfe=2$ and $72.0$
at $\nfe=1$, against about $61$ with the other rules, at success rates of $0.927$ and $0.943$. The same
happens for consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching and not for the diffusion baseline. Temporal consistency is the
selection rule for the flow-based models on this task.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:avoiding:conclusion}

The supported operating point on D3IL-avoiding is a flow-based model at one or two network
evaluations with the per-step projection of DPCC. Analytic average-velocity matching at one evaluation
provides the five-seed result: $0.993$ success with constraint satisfaction in $61.0$ control steps and
$18.1$\,ms per control step. The single-seed comparison of all four models does show the advantage
remembered for consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching (\autoref{tab:state-models}): at full
success it uses $59.68$ rather than $60.17$ control steps and $0.0286$ rather than $0.2209$\,s per step.
This observed lead is slight on path length and large on time because the compared budgets are two and
five evaluations; it rests on a difference of three episodes and has not been repeated over five
training seeds. The two results therefore support the analytic objective as the multi-seed operating
point and the consistency-interpolated objective as the lowest-cost observed single-seed configuration.
Endpoint projection
is not needed at either operating budget. At one evaluation it has no guiding step, and at two it has
only one; raising the budget so that it can guide sampling gives up the low-step advantage that decides
this benchmark. D3IL-aligning and the UAV scenes below require larger budgets and therefore test the
projection method where it has several guiding steps.

conclusion is limited to training-set contexts.

\subsubsection{UAV-pillars}\label{sec:res:uav:pillars}

UAV-pillars is the scene that separates the four generative models. The flow-based models are evaluated
at $\nfe=5$ under eleven configurations on the full constraint set --- the plan without projection;
per-step projection with each of the three selection rules on plain and on tightened constraints; and
endpoint projection with a single candidate and with each rule. The diffusion model is evaluated at its
budget of twenty in five of them. Passing the goal is not what is scarce here: every model crosses the
finish line on nearly every flight, and the diffusion baseline does so on all fifty of its flights. What
separates the models is passing it without touching a pillar, which is what
\autoref{tab:uav-pillars} reports.

\paragraph{Plans without projection.} Executed as the model produced them, instantaneous-velocity matching
passes the goal collision-free on all ten flights and analytic average-velocity matching on nine.
Consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching and the diffusion baseline manage none: the baseline
crosses the line on every flight while violating the pillar clearance on $132$ control steps per flight.
Nothing enforces the constraints in this row, so a clean flight here is a property of the plan alone.

\paragraph{With the constraints enforced.} Under projection the ordering is the same at the top
(\autoref{tab:uav-pillars}). Instantaneous-velocity matching reaches $0.80$ or more in eight of its eleven
configurations and $1.00$ in four, with a mean of $0.718$. Analytic average-velocity matching reaches
$0.80$ or more in five and $0.90$ at best, mean $0.627$; consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching
in three, mean $0.364$; and the diffusion baseline reaches $0.30$ at best, mean $0.08$ over the five
configurations it was run in. On this scene, then, instantaneous-velocity matching is the model whose plans
survive projection most often, and the two average-velocity models trade success for a shorter path:
at their best projected configuration they finish in $442$ and $453$ control steps against
instantaneous-velocity matching's $562$ (\autoref{tab:uav-pillars-best}). Neither ordering dominates the
other, and both hold against the baseline, which finishes no flight within the constraints in three of its
five configurations and $0.30$ at best.

\subsection{Projection Methods}\label{sec:res:uav:projection}

On UAV-corridor, per-step projection is the method. Endpoint projection, run at $\nfe=3$ and $\nfe=5$
with one and two guiding steps, is faster on the same model --- $47$ to $125$\,ms against $99$ to
$144$\,ms per control step for analytic average-velocity matching --- but passes the goal collision-free on
at most seven of twelve flights, where per-step projection does so on all twelve. The projection
configuration of this scene differs from that of UAV-pillars and UAV-s-curve
(\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:uav}), so the scenes are not pooled.

On UAV-pillars, endpoint projection is the method. It holds the best configuration of the scene ---
instantaneous-velocity matching, a single candidate, $1.00$ at $78$\,ms per control step --- and the best
configuration of analytic average-velocity matching as well, $0.90$ at $154$\,ms. Averaged over the
configurations of \autoref{tab:uav-pillars}, it spends $104$\,ms per control step in projection against
$1611$\,ms for per-step projection, and none of its $120$ flights loses physical safety --- no contact and
no loss of altitude --- against $43$ of the $180$ flights with per-step projection. It is not free of
constraint violations: $41$ of its flights violate the clearance at least once, against $89$ with per-step
projection.

does not rank them.

\subsection{Caveat: The Tracking Controller}\label{sec:res:uav:controller}

On the manipulator, the tracking controller absorbs what the plan leaves rough
(\autoref{sec:res:avoiding:smoothness}). The quadrotor offers no such margin: it is underactuated and
open-loop unstable (\autoref{sec:bg:quadrotor}), and a plan reaches the rotors only through the cascaded
controller that tracks it. The result of a flight is therefore a property of the planner and the controller
together, and UAV-s-curve, with its two sharp turns, is where the controller becomes the limit.

This is shown by keeping the plan and changing the controller (\autoref{tab:uav-controller}). Analytic
average-velocity matching at $\nfe=10$, executed without projection, passes the goal on none of ten flights
with the brake-to-rest controller and ends on average $2.81$\,m from the goal point of its route. With
MuJoCo MPC \parencite{howell2022predictive} as the tracking controller, the same plans end $0.299$, $0.298$
and $0.294$\,m from that point, and two of the three flights cross the finish line. The brake-to-rest
failures are not wall collisions: on all ten flights the vehicle stays at altitude until it loses attitude
control and inverts, between control steps $395$ and $421$, about $12$\,s into the flight. MuJoCo MPC flies
the same turn. The unprojected plan is flyable; the brake-to-rest controller cannot fly it.

Changing the controller is not sufficient once projection changes the plan. With per-step projection the
plan passes the goal on none of three flights under MuJoCo MPC and on none of ten under the brake-to-rest
controller, and no flight passes it within the constraints. The unprojected experiment isolates a limit of

controller together. UAV-s-curve therefore supports no ordering of the generative models and no preferred
projection method, and the ordering on the two other scenes is to be read for the controller with which it
was measured.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:res:uav:conclusion}

UAV-corridor shows that the flow-based planners fly under constraints: analytic and
consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching with per-step projection at $\nfe=3$ pass the goal
collision-free on every flight, at a sixth of the time of the diffusion baseline, which stays collision-free
but never reaches the finish line. UAV-pillars separates the four models, and it separates them on the
constraint rather than on the goal, which every model passes: instantaneous-velocity matching with endpoint
projection passes collision-free on all ten flights at $78$\,ms per control step, analytic average-velocity
matching reaches $0.90$ in a shorter path, consistency-interpolated average-velocity matching $0.90$ only at
$1.56$\,s per step, and the diffusion baseline $0.30$ at $4.4$\,s. UAV-s-curve has no supported combination;
its limit is the tracking controller.
