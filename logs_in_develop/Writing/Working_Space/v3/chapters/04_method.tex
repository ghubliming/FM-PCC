\section{Starting Point: Diffusion Predictive Control}\label{sec:method:dpcc}

A compact but complete description of the inherited system \parencite{romer2025diffusion}: temporal
U-Net backbone \parencite{janner2022planning}, diffusion engine, per-step projection onto a feasible
set defined by dynamics and geometry, a candidate fan with selection rules, and receding-horizon
execution. Presented at the level of detail needed to see exactly where the substitution is made.

\subsection*{The constrained denoising step}

The inherited algorithm is \eqref{eq:bg:ddpm:reverse} with a projection inserted between its
steps. Its justification is a guidance argument: introducing a binary feasibility variable
$O$ with likelihood
$p(O{=}1\mid\vect{x},k) \propto \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}d(\vect{x},\mathcal{Z}_{f})^{2}\big)$,
where $d$ is the Euclidean distance to the feasible set, a first-order expansion of
$\log p(O\mid\vect{x},k)$ about the learned mean --- the same expansion that underlies classifier
guidance \parencite{dhariwal2021diffusion} --- gives a guidance term that, for closed convex
$\mathcal{Z}_{f}$, is exactly the displacement to the projection. The conditioned reverse step
is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dpcc:thm}
  p_{\theta}\!\left(\vect{x}^{k-1}\mid\vect{x}^{k},k,O{=}1\right)
  \approx \Normal\!\left(\proj{\mathcal{Z}_{f}}\!\left(\vect{\mu}_{\theta}^{k}\right),\;
    \sigma_{k}^{2}\Id{n}\right)
\end{equation}
\parencite[Theorem~1]{romer2025diffusion}. Because \eqref{eq:method:dpcc:thm} projects the
\emph{mean} and then adds noise, the drawn sample can still leave $\mathcal{Z}_{f}$; the
algorithm that is actually run therefore reorders the two operations, projecting \emph{after}
the noise is added:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dpcc:step}
  \vect{x}^{k-1}
  = \proj{\mathcal{Z}_{f}}\!\left(
      \vect{\mu}_{\theta}\!\left(\vect{x}^{k},k,\vect{c}\right) + \sigma_{k}\vect{\epsilon}^{k}
    \right),
  \qquad \vect{\epsilon}^{k}\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\Id{n}),
\end{equation}
which gives deterministic constraint satisfaction at the steps where it is applied
\parencite[Eq.~16]{romer2025diffusion}. \eqref{eq:method:dpcc:step} is the single equation this
thesis replaces: \autoref{sec:method:engine} substitutes the engine that produces
$\vect{\mu}_{\theta}$, and \autoref{sec:method:hardflow} substitutes the operator applied to it.

\subsection*{The dynamics model: first-order Euler}

The rows \eqref{eq:bg:mpc:dyn} need a concrete $f$. The inherited system does not use an identified
model of the manipulator and its low-level controller; it uses a first-order (explicit Euler)
integrator over the commanded quantity, and pushes everything it omits into the mismatch term
$\vect{w}_{t}$ of \eqref{eq:method:plant}. For the two-dimensional manipulator benchmark, where the
state stacks a commanded and a measured planar position and the action is a planar increment, that
model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dpcc:euler}
  \vect{s}_{t+1}
  = \vect{s}_{t} + \begin{bmatrix}\vect{a}_{t}\trans & \vect{a}_{t}\trans\end{bmatrix}\trans t\sidx{s}
    + \vect{w}_{t},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ \emph{both} halves of the state are advanced by the \emph{same} action
\parencite[Sec.~6.1]{romer2025diffusion}. Three things follow, and all three are inherited by every
environment in this thesis.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The model is deliberately crude, and that is the point.} $f$ has no knowledge of the
    arm's kinematics, its impedance controller, or its integration error; $\gamma$ is estimated
    empirically from unconstrained rollouts and the tightening of \eqref{eq:method:dpcc:tighten}
    absorbs the rest. A projector built on a crude model plus an honest mismatch bound is a
    different design from one built on an accurate model, and it is the design this thesis inherits.
  \item \textbf{$t\sidx{s} = 1$, because the action is a displacement and not a velocity.} The
    network's output is a per-step Cartesian increment, so the Euler update is already in the right
    units and no physical timestep enters the constraint. Setting $t\sidx{s}$ to the simulator's
    integration step --- a natural-looking mistake --- would rescale the entire feasible set.
    \srcnote{\texttt{aux\_repo/dpcc/config/projection\_eval.yaml:14--16}, whose comment reads
    \emph{``since $a = [\Delta x,\Delta y]$ and not $[v_{x},v_{y}]$''}; the same value and the same
    reasoning are carried in \texttt{config/uav\_projection.yaml:125--127} and
    \texttt{config/visual\_aligning\_eval.yaml}.}
  \item \textbf{Anchoring both the commanded and the measured channel is what couples the plan to
    the plant.} If only the measured position were constrained, the projector could return a plan
    whose commanded channel is unreachable; if only the commanded one were, the plan would be
    admissible in a space the robot never occupies. \autoref{sec:method:deployment} shows the same
    two-channel structure in all three environments, and \autoref{sec:method:proj} gives the row form.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Constraint tightening}

The dynamics $f$ used inside \eqref{eq:bg:mpc:dyn} is an approximation, with mismatch bounded
by $\|\vect{w}_{t}\|_{2}\le\gamma$. Feasibility of the \emph{predicted} plan therefore does not
imply feasibility of the executed one. The inherited remedy tightens the predicted-state
constraints by the mismatch ball,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dpcc:tighten}
  \tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t+1} = \mathcal{S}_{t+1}\ominus\mathcal{B}_{\gamma},
\end{equation}
with $\ominus$ the Minkowski difference and $\mathcal{B}_{\gamma}$ the $\ell_{2}$ ball of radius
$\gamma$; sampling against $\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{f}$ then guarantees that all executed states
satisfy the original constraints \parencite[Theorem~2]{romer2025diffusion}. The bound $\gamma$ is
estimated empirically from unconstrained rollouts. \emph{Tightened geometry} in this thesis
means \eqref{eq:method:dpcc:tighten} applied with a larger $\gamma$ than the baseline's, and it
is an experimental factor, not a formatting suffix.

\subsection*{The candidate fan and its selection rules}

Rather than one plan, $B$ plans are drawn per timestep and one is executed. The inherited
selection rules are \emph{temporal consistency} --- take the candidate closest to the previous
timestep's executed plan on their overlapping segment --- and \emph{cumulative projection
cost} --- take the candidate that \eqref{eq:bg:mpc:proj} altered least, summed over the steps
at which it ran \parencite[Sec.~5.4]{romer2025diffusion}. Both are examined in
\autoref{sec:method:selection}; the third rule in this work's implementation is random selection,
which is the convention the two rules were proposed against.

The section ends with a table of provenance --- inherited, modified, new --- at file
granularity, which is the honest form of the ``we build on X'' sentence and feeds
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}. \srcnote{TARGET \S5.3}

\section{Problem Formalisation}\label{sec:method:formalisation}

\subsection*{The control problem}

The plant is a discrete-time system with state $\vect{s}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}$ and action
$\vect{a}_{t}\in\mathcal{A}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:plant}
  \vect{s}_{t+1} = f(\vect{s}_{t},\vect{a}_{t}) + \vect{w}_{t},
  \qquad \|\vect{w}_{t}\|_{2}\le\gamma,
\end{equation}
where $f$ is a known approximate model and $\vect{w}_{t}$ collects model mismatch. A goal
$g$ is reached when an indicator $\phi(\vect{s}_{t},g)$ equals one. Available is a dataset of
$N$ demonstrations produced by an unknown stochastic expert; the demonstrations are
\emph{multimodal}, meaning several distinct ways of reaching the goal are present, which is
why a generative model rather than a regressor is used. At test time the system must in
addition satisfy \emph{novel} constraints
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:constraints}
  \vect{s}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{S},
  \qquad \vect{a}_{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{A},
  \qquad \forall t,
\end{equation}
which are not assumed to hold in the demonstrations. This is the problem statement of
\parencite[Sec.~3]{romer2025diffusion}, restated here because every claim in \autoref{ch:results} is made
against it.

\subsection*{The plan variable}

At control step $t$ the model proposes a horizon of $H+1$ state--action pairs,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:plan}
  \vect{x}
  = \left(\vect{s}_{t:t+H|t},\,\vect{a}_{t:t+H|t}\right) \in \R^{n},
  \qquad n = (H+1)\,d,
\end{equation}
where $\vect{s}_{t+i|t}$ denotes the prediction for time $t+i$ made at time $t$, and $d$ is the
per-step transition width. The generative model is conditional on a context
$\vect{c} = (\vect{s}_{t},g)$, imposed by inpainting: the entries of $\vect{x}$ corresponding to
the current observation are overwritten with their known values after every sampler step, and
the same entries are zeroed in every training target so that no gradient is spent on them.
The feasible set is the intersection of the plan-level constraints with the dynamics rows,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:feasible}
  \mathcal{Z}_{f} = \left\{\vect{x} \;\middle|\;
    \vect{s}_{t'|t}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t'},\;
    \vect{a}_{t'|t}\in\mathcal{A}_{t'},\;
    \vect{s}_{t'+1|t} = f(\vect{s}_{t'|t},\vect{a}_{t'|t}),\;
    \forall t'\right\},
\end{equation}
and the closed loop applies the first action $\vect{a}_{t|t}$ of the selected plan and re-solves
at $t+1$.

\subsection*{Notation, fixed once}

This field's papers overload $t$, $u$ and $x$: control time, transport time, the velocity field
and the control input all compete for the same letters. \autoref{tab:notation} fixes one
convention for the whole thesis. Two decisions in it are worth stating explicitly. The plan is
$\vect{x}$, not $\tau$ as in \parencite{romer2025diffusion}, which frees $\ftime$ for transport time;
and $\ftime$ runs \emph{data-at-one}, so the diffusion index $k$ of \autoref{sec:bg:diffusion}
counts down to the data while the transport index $k$ of \autoref{sec:bg:fm} counts up to it. Every
equation reproduced from a source that uses the opposite direction has been mapped, and the
mapping is noted at the point of use.

Three further collisions arise once the control stack is written down, and the table resolves them
by decoration rather than by renaming, so that each subfield keeps its own idiom. The generative
velocity field is $\vfield$ and the drone's linear velocity is $\dot{\vect{p}}$ --- never $\vect{v}$.
The body moment is $\vect{\tau}\sidx{b}$, always bold and always subscripted; an undecorated $\tau$
is always transport time. And $\matr{J}(\vect{q})$ is the manipulator Jacobian while
$\matr{J}\sidx{b}$ is the drone's inertia tensor; the two never appear in the same equation.

\section{Generative Engine}\label{sec:method:engine}

The substitution itself. Three engines are instantiated behind one interface: naive flow
matching, the average-velocity few-step objective, and the bootstrapped-target variant.
The section gives each one's training target, states what changes in the sampler for each,
and --- the point of the section --- shows that \emph{nothing outside the target and the
integration loop changes}.

\subsection*{What the interface is}

The inherited system is organised around two calls: one that turns an iterate into a proposed
mean, and one that loops over $\nfe$ steps and applies the constraint mechanism. Every engine
in this thesis implements exactly those two calls, and the projection code, the candidate fan,
the conditioning, the normalisation and the closed loop are shared verbatim. Written against
\eqref{eq:method:dpcc:step}, the substitution is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:engine:iface}
  \vect{\mu}^{k}
  = \begin{cases}
      \dfrac{\beta_{k}\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\,\hat{\vect{x}}^{0}
        + \dfrac{(1-\bar{\alpha}_{k-1})\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\,\vect{x}^{k}
        & \text{diffusion (baseline),\; \eqref{eq:bg:ddpm:mean}}\\[10pt]
      \vect{x}_{\ftime_{k}} + \tfrac{1}{\nfe}\,
        \vfield_{\theta}\!\left(\vect{x}_{\ftime_{k}},\ftime_{k},\vect{c}\right)
        & \text{flow matching}\\[6pt]
      \vect{x}_{\ftime_{k}} + \tfrac{1}{\nfe}\,
        \afield_{\theta}\!\left(\vect{x}_{\ftime_{k}},\ftime_{k},h{=}\tfrac{1}{\nfe},\vect{c}\right)
        & \text{MeanFlow,}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
with $\ftime_{k} = k/\nfe$ and the reverse index of the first line replaced by the forward index
of the other two. The three lines are the whole engine delta.
\srcnote{\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3/models/diffusion.py:125--158} and
\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3\_meanflow/models/mf\_diffusion.py:189--300}, against
\texttt{aux\_repo/dpcc/diffuser/models/diffusion.py:119--203}.}

\subsection*{Engine 1 --- naive flow matching}

Training draws a data plan $\vect{x}_{1}$, a prior sample $\vect{x}_{0}$ and a transport time
$\ftime$; forms the interpolant and regresses the velocity, exactly
\eqref{eq:bg:fm:path}--\eqref{eq:bg:fm:loss}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:engine:fm}
  \vect{x}_{\ftime} = (1-\ftime)\vect{x}_{0} + \ftime\vect{x}_{1},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \E\left\|
     \vfield_{\theta}(\vect{x}_{\ftime},\ftime,\vect{c}) - (\vect{x}_{1}-\vect{x}_{0})
  \right\|_{2}^{2}.
\end{equation}
Sampling is \eqref{eq:bg:fm:euler}. Relative to the baseline this deletes the schedule buffers,
the noise-to-sample conversion \eqref{eq:bg:ddpm:x0} and the posterior mean
\eqref{eq:bg:ddpm:mean}, and it deletes the noise injected at every reverse step. The
conditioning mask, the loss reduction and the backbone are untouched.
\srcnote{\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3/models/diffusion.py:273--299}
(\texttt{q\_sample}, \texttt{p\_losses}).}

\subsection*{Engine 2 --- the average-velocity objective}

The network takes two times. This work anchors the query at the \emph{start} of the interval,
$(\vect{x}_{r},r,h)$ with $h=\ftime-r$, because that is the point the sampler queries; the
source \parencite[Eq.~(6)]{geng2025meanflow} anchors at the end and integrates in the opposite time
direction. Differentiating $(\ftime-r)\afield = \int_{r}^{\ftime}\vfield$ with respect to $r$ at
fixed $\ftime$, and following the trajectory so that $\dd\vect{x}_{r}/\dd r = \vfield$, gives the
start-anchored form of \eqref{eq:bg:mf:identity},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:engine:mfid}
  \afield(\vect{x}_{r},r,\ftime)
  = \vfield(\vect{x}_{r},r) + h\,\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\afield(\vect{x}_{r},r,\ftime),
  \qquad h = \ftime - r,
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{eq:bg:mf:identity} under the change of variables, with the sign flip carried by
$\dd h/\dd r = -1$. The training target and loss are then
\begin{align}
  \afield\sidx{tgt}
    &= \sg\!\left[\;\vect{v} + h\,
       \mathrm{JVP}\!\left(\afield_{\theta};\,
         (\vect{x}_{r},r,h),\,(\vect{v},+1,-1)\right)\right],
       \qquad \vect{v} = \vect{x}_{1}-\vect{x}_{0},
       \label{eq:method:engine:mftgt}\\
  \mathcal{L}(\theta)
    &= \E\Big[\,
        \varrho\big(\|\afield_{\theta}(\vect{x}_{r},r,h) - \afield\sidx{tgt}\|_{2}^{2}\big)
      + \varrho\big(\|\vfield_{\theta}(\vect{x}_{r},r,h) - \sg(\vect{v})\|_{2}^{2}\big)\Big],
       \label{eq:method:engine:mfloss}
\end{align}
where $\varrho(e) = e\,/\,\sg\big((e+c)^{p}\big)$ is the adaptive reweighting of
\autoref{sec:bg:fewstep} and the squared errors are summed over the plan before reweighting.
Four properties of \eqref{eq:method:engine:mftgt}--\eqref{eq:method:engine:mfloss} are choices,
not transcription, and each is load-bearing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The $\vect{x}$-tangent is the analytic velocity $\vect{x}_{1}-\vect{x}_{0}$, not a
    predicted one.} Substituting a network-predicted velocity there turns this engine into a
    different one, and the resulting ablation would no longer be controlled.
  \item \textbf{$(\ftime,r)$ are two independent draws} on the transport axis, the larger
    assigned to $\ftime$; drawing $r$ conditionally on $\ftime$ starves the large-$h$ queries,
    which is precisely where one- and two-step sampling lives.
  \item \textbf{A fraction of samples is drawn with $r = \ftime$}, i.e.\ $h=0$, at which
    \eqref{eq:method:engine:mftgt} reduces to $\afield\sidx{tgt} = \vect{v}$ and the objective
    becomes \eqref{eq:method:engine:fm}. These anchor the field.
  \item \textbf{The instantaneous head is a full second loss, not a stabiliser}, and it is
    \emph{discarded at sampling time} --- only $\afield_{\theta}$ is integrated. Mixing the two
    heads at sampling time produced step-to-step jitter.
\end{itemize}
\srcnote{\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3\_meanflow/models/mf\_diffusion.py:353--473}
(\texttt{\_sample\_tau\_pair}, \texttt{\_adaptive}, \texttt{\_p\_losses\_meanflow}); the sign
check against the source's opposite time direction is recorded in that function's docstring.}

Sampling takes $\nfe$ interval jumps of size $h=1/\nfe$, passing both the position and the
interval:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:engine:mfsample}
  \vect{x}_{\ftime_{k+1}} = \vect{x}_{\ftime_{k}}
    + \tfrac{1}{\nfe}\,\afield_{\theta}\!\left(\vect{x}_{\ftime_{k}},\;\ftime_{k},\;h{=}\tfrac{1}{\nfe},\;\vect{c}\right),
  \qquad \ftime_{k} = \tfrac{k}{\nfe},
\end{equation}
which at $\nfe = 1$ is exactly the one-step form \eqref{eq:bg:mf:onestep}. The transport time is
never frozen: the network was trained on $\afield_{\theta}(\cdot,\ftime,h)$ and querying it at a
fixed $\ftime$ turns it into a biased $h$-only function, out of the distribution it was fitted
on. Where an adaptive solver is used instead of the Euler scheme, the interval passed at each
sub-stage is the remaining distance to that macro step's declared endpoint, which keeps every
query inside the $\{\ftime,h\ge 0,\ \ftime+h\le 1\}$ domain the model was trained on.
\srcnote{\texttt{mf\_diffusion.py:189--300}; the guardrail and the sub-stage interval rule are
this work's own, from the Gen3v6 U3/U8 audits.}

\subsection*{Engine 3 --- the bootstrapped target}

Presented as an \emph{ablation and a negative result}, per TARGET~\S8 kill criterion~1: with the
architecture matched and its annealed scalar provably live, it ties the average-velocity engine
at low budget, loses at high budget, and is better nowhere across two environments. It is kept
in this section because it is a legitimate arm of the mechanism story, but the engine claim the
thesis defends is the average-velocity objective. \emph{Its mathematics is deliberately not
written in this draft}; \autoref{sec:res:fewstep} and \autoref{sec:disc:negative} carry the result.

One structural point about it is needed here regardless, because it invalidated a set of this
work's earlier runs: its interpolation scalar appears \emph{only in training}, and at one
setting the two targets coincide branch for branch. A checkpoint trained at that setting
\emph{is} the predecessor engine, whatever the directory is called. The same collapse argument
appears in \autoref{sec:bg:fewstep} for the $r=\ftime$ fraction; both are cases of a hyperparameter
whose extreme value silently changes which engine is being measured, and both are checked by
assertion rather than by convention.

\subsection*{Two facts that are not free choices}

\begin{remark}[The prior scale differs between arms]\label{rem:prior}
The inherited sampler does not start from the standard normal: it draws
$\vect{x}^{\nfe}\sim\Normal(\vect{0},\tfrac{1}{4}\Id{n})$ and injects half the posterior
standard deviation at each reverse step. The naive flow-matching engine inherits the same
$\tfrac{1}{2}$ prior scale at sampling time while its training draws $\vect{x}_{0}\sim
\Normal(\vect{0},\Id{n})$; the MeanFlow engine uses unit scale at both ends. The discrepancy is
recorded here rather than smoothed over, because a prior-scale mismatch between arms is exactly
the kind of confound the matched-architecture discipline exists to exclude --- and because it
has already caused one measurable defect in this work, when a constraint arm was ported between
generations together with the wrong scale.
\srcnote{\texttt{aux\_repo/dpcc/diffuser/models/diffusion.py:158,168};
\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3/models/diffusion.py:164};
\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3\_meanflow/models/mf\_diffusion.py:204}; the porting defect is
Gen3v6 fix\_4.}
\hole{State in \autoref{sec:setup:protocol} whether the reported flow-matching numbers were
re-run at matched prior scale, or carry the inherited $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Do not claim
prior-matching until that is checked.}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The Jacobian--vector product constrains the architecture]\label{rem:jvp}
\eqref{eq:method:engine:mftgt} differentiates through the network, so every component inside the
differentiated closure must be forward-mode friendly. This is not a performance detail --- it
determines how the visual encoder may be attached (\autoref{sec:method:backbone}).
\end{remark}

\section{Backbone and Conditioning}\label{sec:method:backbone}

Where the matched-architecture discipline is spelled out. One temporal U-Net at one
parameter count is used by every engine, including the baseline's. For the vision-conditioned
task, perception is a vendored encoder producing a fixed-width latent, and the generative
backbone never sees an image --- so the latent is a clean interface boundary and the
``same backbone'' claim is verifiable rather than asserted.

\subsection*{The trajectory backbone}

The backbone is a one-dimensional temporal U-Net in the lineage of
\parencite{janner2022planning}: the plan is treated as a length-$H$ signal with $d$ channels,
and convolution runs along \emph{time}, not along the state dimension. Channel widths follow
$\left(c_{0},\dots,c_{L}\right) = \left(d,\;w,\;2w,\;4w,\;8w\right)$ for a base width $w$, with
three stride-two down/up stages and skip concatenations. The horizon is zero-padded up to the next
multiple of eight before the network and cropped back afterwards, because three halvings require it;
$H = 8$ needs no padding, but the code path exists and is worth naming, since a padded frame that
reached the conditioning average would dilute it.

Every level is a pair of residual blocks. Writing $\vect{h}\in\R^{c\sidx{in}\times H}$ for a block's
input, $\mathcal{B}$ for a $\left(\text{Conv1d}\to\text{GroupNorm}\to\text{Mish}\right)$ unit and
$\vect{z}$ for the block's conditioning vector,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:backbone:block}
  \operatorname{Block}(\vect{h},\vect{z})
  = \mathcal{B}_{2}\Big(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\vect{h}) + \big(\matr{W}\sidx{emb}\,\varphi(\vect{z})\big)\vect{1}_{H}\trans\Big)
    + \matr{P}\vect{h},
\end{equation}
with $\varphi$ a pointwise nonlinearity, $\matr{P}$ a $1\times1$ channel-matching convolution, and
$\vect{1}_{H}\trans$ broadcasting the projected embedding along the horizon. The conditioning
therefore enters as a \emph{per-channel additive bias that is constant in time} --- a fact that
matters in a moment.

\subsection*{The conditioning vector}

For the state-based environments $\vect{z}$ is the transport-time embedding alone,
$\vect{z} = \phi_{\ftime}(\ftime)$, a sinusoidal positional encoding followed by a two-layer MLP;
conditioning on the current observation is done by inpainting
(\autoref{sec:method:formalisation}), not through $\vect{z}$.

For the vision-conditioned environment, two RGB views are encoded per frame by independent
ResNet-18 towers with group normalisation --- vendored from the benchmark unchanged, not
re-implemented --- each producing $64$ features, concatenated to a per-frame descriptor. The
descriptor is averaged over the $T\sidx{win}$-frame observation window \emph{before} the trajectory
is padded, giving one latent per plan,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:backbone:latent}
  \vect{e}
  = \frac{1}{T\sidx{win}}\sum_{i=1}^{T\sidx{win}}
    \Big[\,E\sidx{view1}(\matr{I}^{(1)}_{i})\;;\;E\sidx{view2}(\matr{I}^{(2)}_{i})\,\Big]
  \;\in\;\R^{128}.
\end{equation}
$\vect{e}$ is the \textbf{entire} interface between perception and generation: the U-Net's input
channels are the plan's $d$, never an image. That is what makes the architecture-matched claim
checkable rather than asserted --- the perception stack above \eqref{eq:method:backbone:latent} is
identical across every engine, and everything below it is encoder-agnostic.

Two ways of injecting $\vect{e}$ are implemented, and the thesis reports the first as the method and
the second as an ablation:

\begin{description}
  \item[Concatenated conditioning (the shipped default).] The latent is projected by its own MLP and
    \emph{concatenated} with the time embedding, so the block embedding of
    \eqref{eq:method:backbone:block} is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:method:backbone:concat}
      \vect{z} = \big[\,\phi_{\ftime}(\ftime)\;;\;\phi\sidx{vis}(\vect{e})\,\big] \in \R^{2w},
    \end{equation}
    and the visual signal reaches the trajectory only through the additive bias in
    \eqref{eq:method:backbone:block}.
  \item[Affine (true FiLM) conditioning (the ablation).] The latent instead produces a per-channel
    scale and shift applied after the second convolution,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:method:backbone:film}
      \operatorname{Block}\sidx{FiLM}(\vect{h},\vect{z},\vect{e})
      = \Big(\big(1+\vect{\gamma}(\vect{e})\big)\odot \tilde{\vect{h}} + \vect{\beta}(\vect{e})\Big)
        + \matr{P}\vect{h},
      \qquad
      \big[\vect{\gamma};\vect{\beta}\big] = \phi\sidx{film}(\vect{e}),
    \end{equation}
    with $\tilde{\vect{h}}$ the pre-residual output of \eqref{eq:method:backbone:block} and
    $\phi\sidx{film}$ zero-initialised, so training starts from the identity and the ablation is a
    strict superset of nothing. Here $\vect{e}$ does \emph{not} enter $\vect{z}$.
\end{description}

\begin{remark}[The default is not FiLM, and the code's name for it says otherwise]\label{rem:naming}
\eqref{eq:method:backbone:concat} is \emph{concatenated conditioning}: it produces one additive bias
per channel, i.e.\ \eqref{eq:method:backbone:film} with $\vect{\gamma}\equiv\vect{0}$. Feature-wise
linear modulation is by definition the affine map with a learned multiplicative term, so only
\eqref{eq:method:backbone:film} is FiLM. In this work's code both arms are selected by a flag named
\texttt{film\_mode} (\texttt{'v1'} and \texttt{'v2'}), which makes the default look like FiLM in every
configuration file, every checkpoint tag and every log line. \textbf{The thesis uses the mechanism
names, not the flag values}, and every table reports the arm by mechanism. This is one instance of a
general hazard the write-up guards against: an implementation identifier that has hardened into a
label can smuggle a claim into a paper.

\texttt{mix\_visual\_aligning/models/visual\_unet.py:64--97} selects the branch and comments them
\emph{``fake FiLM''} / \emph{``true FiLM''};
\texttt{unet1d\_temporal\_cond.py:53--82, 205--235} is the additive form,
\texttt{unet1d\_temporal\_film.py:38--92} the affine one. The writing-side rule and the full

\end{remark}

\subsection*{Two-time engines: the extra input and the JVP constraint}

The few-step engines need $\afield_{\theta}(\cdot,\ftime,h)$, so the backbone takes a second time
input. It is embedded by its own MLP and \emph{added} to the transport-time embedding, before the
visual latent is concatenated:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:backbone:twotime}
  \vect{z} = \big[\,\phi_{\ftime}(\ftime) + \phi_{h}(h)\;;\;\phi\sidx{vis}(\vect{e})\,\big].
\end{equation}
The order is not cosmetic --- $h$ modulates the time channel, not the visual one --- and it is the
only structural change the two-time family makes to the backbone.

This forces the one non-obvious construction of the visual pipeline. The MeanFlow target
\eqref{eq:method:engine:mftgt} differentiates the network with a forward-mode \ac{JVP} in
$(\vect{x}_{r},r,h)$. If the closure still held raw images, the two ResNet towers would be
differentiated too --- roughly doubling compute and activation memory for a derivative that is
\emph{identically zero}, since $\vect{e}$ depends on none of the three, and forward-mode
differentiation through the vendored encoder is not guaranteed to be implemented at all. The
encoder is therefore evaluated \emph{once, outside} the closure and $\vect{e}$ passed down as a
captured constant, so its tangent is zero by construction rather than by hope. The pre-encoding is
deliberately \emph{not} placed under a no-gradient scope: the encoder is trained end-to-end and
freezing it would change what is learned, whereas capturing the tensor only removes a tangent that
was zero anyway.
\srcnote{Methodology\_Sources/AUX\_visual\_aligning\_env.md \S3.1; the short-circuit and its
rationale are in \texttt{mix\_visual\_aligning/models/visual\_unet\_twotime.py:1--45}, the block
and conditioning arithmetic in \texttt{models/unet1d\_temporal\_cond.py:53--135, 205--235} and
\texttt{models/unet1d\_temporal\_film.py:38--92}.}

Transformer-style backbones are declared out of scope with a reason rather than by omission:
using them properly requires spatial features and cross-attention rather than a pooled
latent, which is a different architecture and would break the parameter match.

\section{Constraint Enforcement I: Per-Step Iterate Projection}\label{sec:method:proj}

Arm~B, inherited. The section states the program that is actually solved, the schedule on which
it is solved, and the one structural fact the mechanism argument later uses.

\subsection*{The program}

With the plan flattened to $\vect{x}\in\R^{n}$, \eqref{eq:bg:mpc:proj} is instantiated as a
quadratic objective with linear equality rows for the dynamics, linear inequality rows for the
bounds, and one quadratic inequality per horizon step per obstacle:
\begin{align}
  \proj{\mathcal{Z}_{f}}(\vect{x})
    = \arg\min_{\vect{z}\in\R^{n}}\;\; &
       \tfrac{1}{2}\,\vect{z}\trans\matr{Q}\vect{z} - \vect{x}\trans\matr{Q}\vect{z}
       \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\vect{z}-\vect{x}\right\|_{\matr{Q}}^{2} + \text{const}
       \label{eq:method:proj:obj}\\
  \text{s.t.}\quad &
       \matr{A}\vect{z} = \vect{b}
       &&\text{(dynamics rows, \eqref{eq:bg:mpc:dyn})}
       \label{eq:method:proj:eq}\\
    & \matr{C}\vect{z} \le \vect{d}
       &&\text{(state / action bounds, halfspaces)}
       \label{eq:method:proj:ineq}\\
    & \vect{z}_{i}\trans\matr{P}_{j}\vect{z}_{i} + \vect{\ell}_{j}\trans\vect{z}_{i}
       \le \nu_{j}
       &&\text{(obstacle $j$ at horizon step $i$),}
       \label{eq:method:proj:obs}
\end{align}
with $\matr{Q}$ diagonal --- the identity unless a subset of dimensions is weighted --- and
$\vect{z}_{i}$ the $i$-th horizon block of $\vect{z}$; $(\matr{P}_{j},\vect{\ell}_{j},\nu_{j})$
are the quadratic, linear and constant coefficients of obstacle $j$. The rows \eqref{eq:method:proj:obs} are
what makes $\mathcal{Z}_{f}$ non-convex, so \eqref{eq:method:proj:obj}--\eqref{eq:method:proj:obs}
is a \ac{NLP} and is solved by SLSQP \parencite{kraft1988software,virtanen2020scipy}, warm
started at $\vect{x}$ itself, with box bounds inherited from the normalisation. The first
dynamics block is overwritten with the measured current state, so the projection cannot move the
point the plan is anchored at. The returned projection cost is
$c_{\mathcal{Z}_{f}}(\vect{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|\proj{\mathcal{Z}_{f}}(\vect{x})-\vect{x}\|_{\matr{Q}}^{2}$,
which is the quantity the cumulative-cost selection rule of \autoref{sec:method:selection} sums.
Each candidate in the fan is a separate program.
\srcnote{\texttt{aux\_repo/dpcc/diffuser/sampling/projection.py:70--155} and its Gen-local copies;
the constraint families are \texttt{SafetyConstraints}, \texttt{DynamicConstraints},
\texttt{ObstacleConstraints} in the same file.}

\subsection*{The dynamics rows, written out}

The Euler model \eqref{eq:method:dpcc:euler} enters \eqref{eq:method:proj:eq} as one equality row per
integrated channel per horizon step. Writing $z_{i,d}$ for dimension $d$ of horizon block $i$, a
channel pair $(d\sidx{pos},d\sidx{act})$ contributes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:proj:deriv}
  z_{i,d\sidx{pos}} + t\sidx{s}\,z_{i,d\sidx{act}} - z_{i+1,d\sidx{pos}} = 0,
  \qquad i = 0,\dots,H-1,
\end{equation}
plus one row pinning $z_{0,d\sidx{pos}}$ to the measured current state --- the only row whose
right-hand side changes at every control step. Two details of \eqref{eq:method:proj:deriv} are easy
to get wrong and are therefore stated. First, the projector operates in \emph{normalised}
coordinates, so each row is rescaled by the per-dimension data range: with
$\Delta_{d} = z_{\textrm{max},d} - z_{\textrm{min},d}$ and
$\Sigma_{d} = z_{\textrm{max},d} + z_{\textrm{min},d}$, the implemented row is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:proj:derivnorm}
  \Delta_{d\sidx{pos}}\,z_{i,d\sidx{pos}}
  + t\sidx{s}\,\Delta_{d\sidx{act}}\,z_{i,d\sidx{act}}
  - \Delta_{d\sidx{pos}}\,z_{i+1,d\sidx{pos}}
  = -\,t\sidx{s}\,\Sigma_{d\sidx{act}} ,
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{eq:method:proj:deriv} conjugated by the normaliser; forgetting the affine offset
$\Sigma$ silently biases every dynamics row. Second, the row count is the number of
\emph{channel pairs}, and that number is a modelling decision, not a constant: it is four for the
two-dimensional manipulator and six for both three-dimensional environments, because each of them
anchors a commanded and a measured position in three axes
(\autoref{tab:embodiments}).
\srcnote{\texttt{aux\_repo/dpcc/diffuser/sampling/projection.py:344--402}
(\texttt{DynamicConstraints.build\_matrices}); the pair lists are in
\texttt{mix\_visual\_aligning\_test/eval\_mix\_visual\_aligning.py:272--279} and
\texttt{mix\_uav\_test/eval\_mix\_uav.py:1265--1266}.}

\subsection*{The activation schedule}

The projection is not applied at every step. A threshold $\eta\in[0,1]$ selects the portion of
the transport near the data end at which it runs. In the inherited descending-index sampler that
is the condition $k \le \eta\nfe$; in this work's ascending-index transport samplers it is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:proj:gate}
  k \;\ge\; \left\lfloor (1-\eta)\,\nfe \right\rfloor
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad k = \nfe-1 ,
\end{equation}
the second clause forcing the terminal step so that a plan is never returned unprojected. The
two forms agree up to the rounding of $\eta\nfe$, and the number of activated steps is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:proj:nact}
  n\sidx{act}(\nfe,\eta) = \max\!\left\{\nfe - \left\lfloor(1-\eta)\nfe\right\rfloor,\;1\right\}.
\end{equation}
$\eta = 0$ therefore does not mean ``no projection'': it means the single terminal projection,
i.e.\ project-after-sampling. This is the post-processing baseline of \parencite[Sec.~6.1]{romer2025diffusion},
and writing it as a special case of \eqref{eq:method:proj:gate} rather than as a separate code
path is what keeps the two comparable.
\srcnote{\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3/models/diffusion.py:174--183} carries the derivation of the
guarded form and the record of what it changes relative to the earlier unguarded expression.}

\subsection*{What is projected}

Arm~B projects the \emph{current iterate}. Under \eqref{eq:method:proj:gate} with a large $\eta$
that iterate is still far from the data end of the transport, so
\eqref{eq:method:proj:obj}--\eqref{eq:method:proj:obs} is posed on an object that is not yet a
plan: the geometric rows \eqref{eq:method:proj:obs} are being asked about positions that the
sampler has not finished producing. This is the fact the mechanism argument of
\autoref{sec:disc:interpretation} uses, and it is the single structural difference between this arm
and the next one.

\section{Constraint Enforcement II: In-ODE Endpoint Projection}\label{sec:method:hardflow}

Arm~C: \emph{in-ODE endpoint projection}, the mechanism introduced by
\parencite{li2025hardflow} (their \texttt{hardflow\_new} mode), re-derived here on this work's own
constraint set. The name is the mechanism rather than the source's, for two reasons that
\autoref{sec:method:hardflow} makes good on below: what runs here solves no objective, so calling it
an optimisation would over-describe it; and the solver it happens to use is a backend flag, so
naming the arm after the solver would assert a contribution that is not there. The same feasible set
\eqref{eq:method:feasible} and the same solver as \autoref{sec:method:proj}, but the optimisation is
posed on the \emph{predicted clean endpoint} implied by the current iterate and velocity ---
$\hat{\vect{x}}_{1} = \vect{x}_{\ftime} + (1-\ftime)\,\vfield_{\theta}(\vect{x}_{\ftime},\ftime,\vect{c})$
for the transport family, the analogue of \eqref{eq:bg:ddpm:x0} for the baseline --- and the
result is pulled back into the transport before the next step. Written in the optimal-control
form of \parencite[Problem~1]{li2025hardflow}, the sampler is steered by a control $\vect{u}_{\ftime}$
added to the learned field, $\dot{\vect{x}}_{\ftime} = \vfield_{\theta}(\vect{x}_{\ftime}) + \vect{u}_{\ftime}$,
penalised in $\|\vect{u}_{\ftime}\|_{2}^{2}$ and subject to the terminal requirement
$h(\vect{x}_{1})\le 0$. What is implemented here is that problem's \emph{reversed} receding-horizon
instance \parencite[Problem~5]{li2025hardflow}: an Euler reference step, then an optimisation whose
decision variable is the \emph{predicted endpoint} rather than the iterate --- which is what keeps
the constraint $h$ undistorted by the network --- followed by the inverse map back into the
transport. Two departures from the source are deliberate and are what make the arm comparison
controlled: the program is built from the same constraint list arm~B consumes rather than from the
source's own hard-coded geometry, and the optional terminal cost $C(\cdot)$ is dropped, so both arms
solve \emph{the same feasible-set problem} and differ only in where it is posed. With $C$ dropped
the remaining objective is a pure quadratic proximity term, whose minimiser is independent of its
weight; what actually damps the early steps is the linear factor in the pull-back, not the
weighting.
\srcnote{\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3\_meanflow/sampling/hardflow\_projection.py:1--40}, which records
the port decisions and the mode chosen (\texttt{hardflow\_new}, the only one that calls the network
as a black box outside the solver).} The section makes the structural
comparison to arm~B explicit --- identical constraints, identical solver, different
linearisation point --- because that is what licenses attributing any cost difference to the
algorithm.

The section also accounts honestly for what arm~C spends: it evaluates the network an extra
time per guided step to form the endpoint estimate, so the two arms are \emph{not}
evaluation-matched even when their step budgets are equal. This is stated here so that no
results table has to carry the caveat.

\subsection*{What differs from the published configuration}

Arm~C is the published algorithm run inside \emph{this} thesis's apparatus, not the published
\emph{experiment}. The two are easy to conflate, because the source evaluates on the same
benchmark and cites the same predecessor \parencite{romer2025diffusion} for its constraint set. They
are not the same setup, and the differences are large enough that no number reported here may be

Three of these rows change what the arm \emph{is}, and each is a deliberate choice made so that the
comparison inside this thesis is controlled --- at the price of comparability outside it.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Plan length and replanning ($16/8$ against $8/1$).} The published experiment plans
    sixteen steps and executes eight of them before replanning; this work plans eight and executes
    one, which is the inherited receding-horizon convention of \parencite{romer2025diffusion} and is
    what every other arm in this thesis does. The consequence is not cosmetic in either direction.
    A longer plan gives the endpoint optimisation more room --- the constraint geometry is imposed
    over sixteen future states rather than eight --- so the arm is being asked an easier question
    there. But executing one action instead of eight means the whole sampler-plus-projector stack
    runs \emph{eight times more often per environment step} here, which inflates every wall-clock
    figure relative to the published ones and is the reason a per-replan timing and a per-step
    timing must never be tabulated together. Matching the engines to each other was judged more
    important than matching this thesis to the source; the alternative would have been to run every
    engine at $16/8$ and abandon the inherited baseline.
  \item \textbf{The dynamics rows.} The source fits a linear model
    $\vect{s}_{i+1} = \matr{A}\vect{s}_{i}+\matr{B}\vect{a}_{i}+\vect{c}$ by least squares on the
    demonstration data. This work keeps the inherited first-order Euler model
    \eqref{eq:method:dpcc:euler} instead, because arm~B is defined on it: giving the two arms
    different dynamics rows would have made the arm comparison --- the thing RQ3 asks --- a
    comparison of feasible sets rather than of algorithms.
  \item \textbf{The solver.} SLSQP throughout, rather than the source's interior-point solver, for
    the same reason: it is arm~B's solver. It is a backend, not a method: the activation gate that
    decides whether the algorithm runs at all is solver-agnostic, and putting the solver in the arm's
    name would assert that the solver is the contribution. It is not.
  \item \textbf{The terminal cost, and what its removal costs.} The source's formulation admits an
    optional terminal objective $C(\cdot)$ --- on this benchmark, the squared distance from the last
    predicted state to the target --- which it uses to \emph{improve sample quality}, not to enforce
    constraints. This work drops it, because arm~B has no such term: the inherited projection
    \eqref{eq:bg:mpc:proj} is pure proximity, and an arm~C carrying a goal-seeking objective would
    beat it on task metrics for a reason that has nothing to do with where the program is posed.
    Dropping $C$ is therefore what makes the arm comparison a comparison.

    Two consequences follow, and both are stated here rather than discovered in
    \autoref{ch:results}. First, with $C$ removed the objective is a pure quadratic proximity term,
    whose minimiser does not depend on its weight --- so arm~C's per-step program is \emph{exactly}
    $\proj{\mathcal{Z}_{f}}(\hat{\vect{x}}_{1})$, the same operator as arm~B applied at a different
    point. The claim ``identical constraints, identical solver, different linearisation point'' is
    then literally true rather than approximately true, and what distinguishes arm~C is the lookahead
    and the damped pull-back, not any optimisation of an objective.
    Second, and less comfortably: the source's headline result on this benchmark is that its method
    reaches the target in \emph{fewer} steps than unguided sampling, and that result is produced by
    $C$. The shape of its own table makes the attribution hard to avoid --- every projection-only
    baseline there \emph{lengthens} the path relative to unguided sampling, and only the arm carrying
    $C$ shortens it. \textbf{Arm~C as run here has no mechanism that shortens paths.} If it shows no
    task-performance advantage over arm~B, that is the expected consequence of a deliberate
    simplification, and it must not be reported as evidence against the published method.
\end{itemize}

The one row that does agree is the activation convention, and it is the load-bearing one. The
published configuration activates on the second half of a ten-step budget, which by
\eqref{eq:method:degen:ngen} gives $n\sidx{act} = 5$ and $n\sidx{gen} = 4$ --- comfortably inside the
admissible regime of \autoref{sec:method:degenerate}. The degeneracy this thesis characterises is
therefore \emph{not} a defect of the published experiment; it is a property of the convention that
appears once the step budget is pushed below what that experiment used, which is exactly what a
budget sweep does. Saying this plainly is the difference between a contribution and an accusation.

\section{When the Guided Sampler Is Not Guided}\label{sec:method:degenerate}

\textbf{A short section carrying one of the thesis's contributions.} The activation convention
\eqref{eq:method:proj:gate} that gates arm~C admits a regime in which the only active step is the
terminal one. At the terminal time the lookahead vanishes, the pull-back becomes a full snap
rather than a damped correction, and no subsequent step exists for the network to react to. What
executes is projection applied to a finished sample --- arm~B's algorithm with a different
solver.

\subsection*{The boundary}

Of the $n\sidx{act}$ steps counted by \eqref{eq:method:proj:nact}, one is the terminal step,
which by the argument above performs no guidance. Define the \emph{genuine step count}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:degen:ngen}
  n\sidx{gen}(\nfe,\eta)
  \;=\; n\sidx{act}(\nfe,\eta) - 1
  \;=\; \max\!\left\{\nfe - \left\lfloor(1-\eta)\nfe\right\rfloor,\;1\right\} - 1 .
\end{equation}
The binding quantity is $n\sidx{gen}$, \emph{not} $\nfe$: two configurations with the same step
budget and different thresholds can sit on opposite sides of the boundary. Three regimes follow,
and the thesis tags every reported row with which one it is in:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \toprule
  $n\sidx{gen}$ & regime & what the row may support \\
  \midrule
  $0$        & \textbf{degenerate} & nothing. No in-ODE projection runs at all. \\
  $1$        & \textbf{thin}       & a single nudge; no attributable signal. \\
  $\ge 2$    & \textbf{admissible} & a claim about the arm. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A second quantity is reported alongside it: the \emph{first lookahead}, the distance
$1-\ftime_{k_{0}+1}$ remaining at the first genuine step, where $k_{0} = \nfe - n\sidx{act}$. It
measures how much transport the endpoint estimate is actually extrapolating over, and it is what
distinguishes an admissible row that carries real signal from one that is technically admissible
but nearly terminal.
\srcnote{\texttt{flow\_matcher\_v3\_meanflow/sampling/hardflow\_projection.py:584--646}
(\texttt{hardflow\_step\_budget}, \texttt{hardflow\_regime}); the tiering is enforced in the
evaluation gates, not left to the reader.}

\subsection*{The reporting rule, applied to this work's own results}

Rows with $n\sidx{gen} = 0$ are tagged and excluded from every claim; rows with
$n\sidx{gen} = 1$ are marked as carrying no attributable signal; neither is ever averaged into a
cell. Two consequences are stated here rather than buried. First, the rule invalidates several of
this work's own earlier measurements --- notably every low-budget row of the endpoint-projection arm at the
shipped threshold, which was reported before the boundary was derived. Those rows are named in
\autoref{sec:res:constraints:degenerate} and withdrawn there. Second, the same arithmetic applies to
any published comparison that uses this activation convention at a small step budget, which is
why the condition is presented as a contribution rather than as an implementation note: it is a
computable property of $(\nfe,\eta)$ that decides whether a comparison measures anything at all.

\section{Candidate Selection}\label{sec:method:selection}

The projector's fan-and-select machinery, described and then examined. The three rules are random choice, temporal
consistency and cumulative projection cost (\autoref{sec:method:dpcc}); written out,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:sel:rules}
  i\sidx{rand}(t) \sim \Unif\{1,\dots,B\},
  \quad
  i\sidx{tc}(t) = \arg\min_{j}\left\|\vect{x}^{j}_{t:t+H-1|t} - \vect{x}^{i(t-1)}_{t:t+H-1|t-1}\right\|_{2},
  \quad
  i\sidx{cc}(t) = \arg\min_{j}\sum_{k} c_{\mathcal{Z}_{f}}\!\left(\tilde{\vect{x}}^{k,j}\right),
\end{equation}
with $\tilde{\vect{x}}$ the pre-projection iterate. The section then establishes analytically
what the ablation later confirms empirically: at $B=1$ all three of
\eqref{eq:method:sel:rules} return the same index, so they are the \emph{same computation} and
evaluating all three is pure waste. This is stated as a proof obligation discharged here, so that the
results chapter only has to handle the fan-greater-than-one case.

\section{Closed-Loop Deployment}\label{sec:method:deployment}

How a plan becomes a command, for all three environments, in one section --- because the parallel
is the substance of RQ4. In every case the generative model replans at each control step, emits a
\emph{position increment}, and never emits an actuator command; everything below the increment is
classical control --- a differential \ac{IK} loop feeding a joint-space PD law on the two
manipulator environments, and a cascaded \ac{PID} controller, or optionally a sampling-based
\ac{MJPC} tracker, on the quadrotor.

\subsection*{The shared skeleton}

Write $\Delta\vect{p}_{t}$ for the first action of the selected plan at control step $t$. All three
environments run the same two-line recursion,
\begin{align}
  \vect{p}_{\textrm{des},t+1} &= \vect{p}_{\textrm{des},t} + \Delta\vect{p}_{t},
    &&\vect{p}_{\textrm{des},0} = \vect{p}_{0},
    \label{eq:method:dep:setpoint}\\
  \vect{o}_{t} &= \left(\vect{p}_{\textrm{des},t},\;\vect{p}_{t},\;[\,\dot{\vect{p}}_{t}\,]\right),
    \label{eq:method:dep:obs}
\end{align}
where $\vect{o}_{t}$ is the observation the model conditions on at the next step and the bracketed
velocity block is present only for the aerial platform. Two structural properties follow, and they
are the reason this section exists rather than being a footnote in the setup chapter.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\eqref{eq:method:dep:setpoint} is a free-running integrator driven by the model's own
    past outputs.} $\vect{p}\sidx{des}$ is not read back from the plant. Plan error therefore
    accumulates rather than being corrected, which is the structural reason the aerial task is the
    fragile one and the reason a divergence guard is needed at all.
  \item \textbf{\eqref{eq:method:dep:obs} feeds the commanded setpoint back alongside the measured
    state}, so the model conditions on its own history. The gap
    $\vect{p}_{\textrm{des},t} - \vect{p}_{t}$ is exactly the tracking error the low-level controller is
    working on, and it is visible to the planner --- which is why the two channels are both
    constrained in \eqref{eq:method:proj:deriv}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Manipulator: the increment becomes a joint torque}

The environment is commanded with an absolute Cartesian pose. The evaluation loop forms it from the
increment and the \emph{commanded} channel of the observation --- not from the measured one, which
is what makes \eqref{eq:method:dep:setpoint} the free-running recursion it is --- and holds the
end-effector orientation and, on the planar benchmark, the height fixed.
\srcnote{\texttt{aux\_repo/dpcc/scripts/eval.py:236--238}: \texttt{next\_pos\_des = action + obs[:2]}.}

Below that setpoint sits the differential \ac{IK} loop. Let $\vect{q}\in\R^{7}$ be the joint
positions, $\matr{J}(\vect{q})$ the end-effector Jacobian, and let the task-space error be a
proportional term on position and orientation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:taskerr}
  \vect{\xi}(\vect{q})
  = \begin{bmatrix}
      k\sidx{p}\odot\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\vect{p}\sidx{des}-\vect{p}(\vect{q}),\,\pm\epsilon\sidx{p}\right)\\[2pt]
      k\sidx{q}\odot\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\vect{e}\sidx{rot}(\vect{q}),\,\pm\epsilon\sidx{r}\right)
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $\vect{p}(\vect{q})$ from forward kinematics and $\vect{e}\sidx{rot}$ the quaternion error to the
commanded orientation. The joint increment is the weighted, damped least-squares solution with a
null-space term that pulls the arm towards a rest posture,
\begin{align}
  \dot{\vect{q}}\sidx{null} &= k\sidx{null}\left(\vect{q}\sidx{rest}-\vect{q}\right),
    \label{eq:method:dep:null}\\
  \dot{\vect{q}}
    &= \matr{W}\matr{J}\trans
       \left(\matr{J}\matr{W}\matr{J}\trans + \lambda\Id{6}\right)^{-1}
       \left(\vect{\xi} - \matr{J}\dot{\vect{q}}\sidx{null}\right)
     + \dot{\vect{q}}\sidx{null},
    \label{eq:method:dep:dls}
\end{align}
after which the singular values of $\matr{J}\matr{W}\matr{J}\trans + \lambda\Id{6}$ are clipped into
$[\sigma\sidx{min},\sigma\sidx{max}]$ before the solve, $\|\dot{\vect{q}}\|$ is capped, and
$\vect{q} \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}(\vect{q}+\eta\sidx{ik}\dot{\vect{q}},\,\vect{q}\sidx{min},\vect{q}\sidx{max})$
is iterated $n\sidx{ik}$ times per control step. The resulting joint target, its finite-difference
velocity and acceleration are handed to a joint-space PD law, and \emph{that} is what produces the
torques the simulator integrates.

The parameters are inherited from the benchmark and are listed once because the loop is otherwise
not reproducible: $k\sidx{p} = (200,200,800)$, $k\sidx{q} = (30,30,30)$, $k\sidx{null} = 40$,
$\lambda = 10^{-12}$, $\matr{W} = \Id{7}$, $[\sigma\sidx{min},\sigma\sidx{max}] = [10^{-2},10^{2}]$,
$n\sidx{ik} = 3$, $\eta\sidx{ik} = 10^{-3}$, clips $\epsilon\sidx{p} = 0.01\,\textrm{m}$ and
$\epsilon\sidx{r} = 0.1$, and joint PD gains
$(120,120,120,120,50,30,10)$ / $(10,10,10,10,6,5,3)$.
\srcnote{\texttt{d3il/environments/d3il/d3il\_sim/controllers/IKControllers.py:134--323}
(\texttt{CartPosQuatImpedenceController}) and
\texttt{.../controllers/Config/mujoco\_controller\_config.gin:6--37}.}

Two consequences matter for the thesis. \eqref{eq:method:dep:dls} is a \emph{local} solve run three
times per step, so the arm does not exactly reach $\vect{p}\sidx{des}$ within one step --- the residual
is precisely the $\vect{w}_{t}$ that \eqref{eq:method:dpcc:euler} pushes into the mismatch bound and
\eqref{eq:method:dpcc:tighten} then absorbs. And the null-space term makes the map from setpoint to
joint configuration history-dependent, so two identical plans executed from different postures do not
produce identical joint trajectories, even though they produce the same Cartesian trace.

\subsection*{Visual aligning: the same chain, one dimension up}

The spatial aligning environment uses the identical controller stack; what changes is the plan and
the observation. The action is a three-dimensional increment $\Delta\vect{p}\in\R^{3}$, the state
half of the observation is $(\vect{p}\sidx{des},\vect{p})\in\R^{6}$ --- commanded and measured
end-effector position --- and the plan width is therefore $d = 9$ per horizon step, laid out as
$(\vect{a},\vect{p}\sidx{des},\vect{p})$. The six dynamics rows of
\eqref{eq:method:proj:deriv} are the pairs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:varows}
  \left(d\sidx{pos},d\sidx{act}\right) \in
  \{(3,0),(4,1),(5,2)\}\;\cup\;\{(6,0),(7,1),(8,2)\},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the commanded triple and the measured triple both integrate the same action, which is
\eqref{eq:method:dpcc:euler} written in three dimensions.
\srcnote{\texttt{mix\_visual\_aligning\_test/eval\_mix\_visual\_aligning.py:272--279}.}

Perception sits \emph{above} the plan, not inside the loop: two RGB views are encoded to a
$128$-dimensional latent that conditions the backbone by concatenation with the time embedding
\eqref{eq:method:backbone:concat} --- \emph{not} by FiLM, whatever the configuration flag is called
(Remark~\ref{rem:naming}) --- and the generative backbone never sees an image
(\autoref{sec:method:backbone}). The control chain below the setpoint is therefore
byte-identical to the state-based benchmark's, which is what licenses reading a change in results as
a change of \emph{modality} rather than of apparatus.
\srcnote{Methodology\_Sources/AUX\_visual\_aligning\_env.md \S2.}

\subsection*{Aerial: the increment becomes four rotor thrusts}

The aerial chain is the same skeleton with two more layers underneath, and it is multi-rate: the
plan is queried at the rate the expert data was recorded, and the physics and the low-level
controller run faster, with the setpoint held constant in between,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:multirate}
  n\sidx{dec} = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{1}{\Delta t\sidx{phys}\,f\sidx{plan}}\right),
  \qquad
  f\sidx{plan} = 33\,\textrm{Hz},\quad \Delta t\sidx{phys} = 0.01\,\textrm{s}
  \;\Rightarrow\; n\sidx{dec} = 3 .
\end{equation}
\srcnote{\texttt{mix\_uav\_test/eval\_mix\_uav.py:1454--1455};
$\Delta t\sidx{phys}$ from \texttt{quadrotor\_modified.xml:4}.}

The vehicle is the rigid body of \parencite[Eqs.~(2)--(5)]{lee2010geometric}: with mass $m$, body
inertia $\matr{J}\sidx{b}$, attitude $\matr{R}\in SO(3)$ and body rates $\vect{\omega}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:plant}
  m\ddot{\vect{p}} = m g\,\unitvec{3} - T\,\matr{R}\unitvec{3},
  \qquad
  \dot{\matr{R}} = \matr{R}\hat{\vect{\omega}},
  \qquad
  \matr{J}\sidx{b}\dot{\vect{\omega}} + \vect{\omega}\times\matr{J}\sidx{b}\vect{\omega} = \vect{\tau}\sidx{b},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{(\cdot)}$ is the hat map. The controller implemented here is the \emph{position-controlled
flight mode} of that paper, instantiated on this model; each of the four steps below names the
equation of \parencite{lee2010geometric} it realises, and the two places where the implementation
departs from it are stated rather than left to be discovered.

\paragraph{Outer loop --- position to thrust vector.} With $\vect{e}\sidx{p} = \vect{p}-\vect{p}\sidx{des}$
and $\vect{e}\sidx{v} = \dot{\vect{p}}-\dot{\vect{p}}\sidx{des}$ --- the tracking errors of
\parencite[Eqs.~(17)--(18)]{lee2010geometric} --- a diagonal PD with acceleration feed-forward gives
the required world-frame force
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:outer}
  \vect{a}\sidx{cmd} = -\matr{K}\sidx{p}\vect{e}\sidx{p} - \matr{K}\sidx{d}\vect{e}\sidx{v} + \ddot{\vect{p}}\sidx{des},
  \qquad
  \vect{F} = m\left(\vect{a}\sidx{cmd} + g\,\unitvec{3}\right),
\end{equation}
which is the bracketed term of \parencite[Eq.~(23)]{lee2010geometric}. \textbf{Deviation 1 --- frame
convention.} The source states \eqref{eq:method:dep:plant} with $\unitvec{3}$ pointing \emph{down}
and the thrust acting along $-\vect{b}_{3}$, so its position and velocity terms carry the opposite
sign to those written here; this implementation is $z$-up with thrust along $+\vect{b}_{3}$. The law
is the same law, and the scalar gains are not transferable between the two statements without that
flip. \textbf{Deviation 2 --- diagonal gains.} $\matr{K}\sidx{p}$ and $\matr{K}\sidx{d}$ are diagonal
matrices here, where \parencite{lee2010geometric} proves stability for the scalar gains $k_{x},k_{v}$;
the per-axis split (a stiffer vertical channel) is an engineering choice and is \emph{not} covered by
the paper's stability statement.

\paragraph{Attitude extraction.} The rotor disk must point along $\vect{F}$, and the remaining degree
of freedom is fixed by the commanded yaw $\psi\sidx{des}$. This is
\parencite[Eqs.~(22)--(23)]{lee2010geometric}, with the free axis $\vect{b}_{1c}$ --- there only
required to be orthogonal to $\vect{b}_{3c}$ --- pinned here by projecting the yaw direction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:att}
  \vect{b}_{3} = \frac{\vect{F}}{\|\vect{F}\|},\quad
  \vect{b}_{2} = \frac{\vect{b}_{3}\times\vect{x}\sidx{c}}{\|\vect{b}_{3}\times\vect{x}\sidx{c}\|},\quad
  \vect{b}_{1} = \vect{b}_{2}\times\vect{b}_{3},\quad
  \matr{R}\sidx{des} = \begin{bmatrix}\vect{b}_{1} & \vect{b}_{2} & \vect{b}_{3}\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $\vect{x}\sidx{c} = (\cos\psi\sidx{des},\sin\psi\sidx{des},0)\trans$.

\paragraph{Inner loop --- geometric attitude control.} The attitude error is the coordinate-free
error on $SO(3)$ of \parencite[Eq.~(21)]{lee2010geometric}, and the moment is its
\parencite[Eq.~(20)]{lee2010geometric}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:inner}
  \vect{e}\sidx{R} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\matr{R}\sidx{des}\trans\matr{R} - \matr{R}\trans\matr{R}\sidx{des}\right)^{\vee},
  \qquad
  \vect{\tau}\sidx{b} = -\matr{K}\sidx{R}\vect{e}\sidx{R} - \matr{K}_{\omega}\vect{e}_{\omega}
      + \vect{\omega}\times\matr{J}\sidx{b}\vect{\omega},
\end{equation}
where $(\cdot)^{\vee}$ is the inverse hat map. \textbf{Deviation 3 --- the attitude feed-forward is
dropped.} The source defines
$\vect{e}_{\omega} = \vect{\omega} - \matr{R}\trans\matr{R}\sidx{des}\vect{\omega}\sidx{des}$ and adds
$-\matr{J}\sidx{b}(\hat{\vect{\omega}}\matr{R}\trans\matr{R}\sidx{des}\vect{\omega}\sidx{des}
- \matr{R}\trans\matr{R}\sidx{des}\dot{\vect{\omega}}\sidx{des})$ to
\eqref{eq:method:dep:inner}. This implementation sets $\vect{\omega}\sidx{des} \equiv \vect{0}$, so
$\vect{e}_{\omega} = \vect{\omega}$ and the whole feed-forward term vanishes. That is legitimate only
while the commanded attitude turns slowly --- and $\matr{R}\sidx{des}$ is driven by
\eqref{eq:method:dep:att} from a setpoint the \emph{generative model} produces, so its rate is not
under the controller's control. The almost-global exponential stability of
\parencite[Prop.~1]{lee2010geometric} therefore does not transfer verbatim to what is run here, and
the thesis does not claim it.

\paragraph{Allocation.} The collective thrust is the world force projected on the current body-$z$
axis, floored so the vehicle cannot free-fall during recovery, and the wrench is distributed over the
four rotors by a fixed geometry matrix:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:alloc}
  T = \max\!\left(\vect{F}\trans\vect{b}_{3},\;T\sidx{floor}\right),
  \qquad
  \matr{M}\vect{u} = \begin{bmatrix}T\\ \vect{\tau}\sidx{b}\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \matr{M}_{:,i} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & r_{i,y} & -r_{i,x} & \kappa_{i}\end{bmatrix}\trans,
\end{equation}
with $\vect{r}_{i}$ the $i$-th rotor site position and $\kappa_{i}$ its yaw-torque gear ratio, both
read from the model file rather than hard-coded. \eqref{eq:method:dep:alloc} is
\parencite[Eq.~(1)]{lee2010geometric} --- there written for four rotors at distance $d$ on a square,
$[\,f\;\vect{\tau}\sidx{b}\,]\trans = \matr{M}\vect{f}\sidx{rotor}$ with
$\matr{M}$ built from $\pm d$ and the thrust-to-torque constant --- generalised to arbitrary rotor
placements by reading $\vect{r}_{i}$ and $\kappa_{i}$ off the vehicle model. It is invertible under
the same non-degeneracy condition. When $\vect{u} = \matr{M}^{-1}[T;\vect{\tau}\sidx{b}]$
leaves the actuator box, the command is rescaled \emph{thrust-first}: the mean is held and the
torque component is shrunk by the largest factor that keeps every rotor in range, but never below
one half, so attitude authority is never more than halved. Parameters of record:
$\matr{K}\sidx{p} = \operatorname{diag}(4,4,8)$, $\matr{K}\sidx{d} = \operatorname{diag}(3,3,4)$,
$\matr{K}\sidx{R} = \operatorname{diag}(70,70,4)$, $\matr{K}_{\omega} = \operatorname{diag}(2.5,2.5,1)$,
$u\sidx{hover} = mg/4$, $u\sidx{max} = \max(2u\sidx{hover},6)$, $u\sidx{min} = 0$,
$T\sidx{floor} = 0.1\,mg$, $g = 9.81\,\textrm{m}/\textrm{s}^{2}$; $m$ and $\matr{J}\sidx{b}$ are
read from the vehicle model.
\srcnote{\texttt{uav\_env\_test/flight\_controller.py:33--155} (\texttt{CascadedPID}).}

\paragraph{The velocity setpoint is synthesised, not planned.} \eqref{eq:method:dep:outer} needs
$\dot{\vect{p}}\sidx{des}$, which the plan does not supply --- it supplies displacements. Three
policies are implemented and the choice is a recorded experimental factor, not an implementation
detail:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:vdes}
  \dot{\vect{p}}\sidx{des} =
  \begin{cases}
    \Delta\vect{p}_{t}\,f\sidx{plan} & \text{default --- the increment read as a mean velocity,}\\
    \vect{0} & \text{stop-and-go --- brake to rest at every plan step,}\\
    \dfrac{\Delta\vect{p}_{t}}{\|\Delta\vect{p}_{t}\|}\,\bar{v} & \text{constant-speed, with }
      \bar{v} = f\sidx{plan}\,\overline{\|\Delta\vect{p}\|}\text{ from the dataset.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The third is timing-free by construction and calibrates itself to whatever data it is given; its
magnitude is the mean of the first policy, so the three differ in \emph{profile}, not in average
speed. \srcnote{\texttt{mix\_uav\_test/eval\_mix\_uav.py:1551--1563, 1822--1832}.}

\paragraph{The alternative low-level controller.} A sampling-based predictive controller is
implemented behind the same interface, so it can be swapped for the cascade without touching the
plan layer. It is the \emph{Predictive Sampling} planner of \parencite{howell2022predictive}, used
as published; only the task is ours. Three pieces have to be named for the substitution to be
readable.

\emph{The objective.} The planner minimises the finite-horizon sum
$J = \sum_{i} c(\vect{x}_{i},\vect{u}_{i})$ over a rollout of the true simulator dynamics
\parencite[Eq.~(3)]{howell2022predictive}, with the running cost a weighted sum of twice-differentiable
norms of \emph{residuals} --- quantities that are small when the task is solved
\parencite[Eq.~(4)]{howell2022predictive}. The task defined here has two residual terms, both under a
quadratic norm and at risk-neutral setting ($R = 0$ in
\parencite[Eq.~(5)]{howell2022predictive}, so the risk transform is the identity):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:method:dep:mjpc}
  J = \sum_{i=1}^{H\sidx{mjpc}}
      \Big(\underbrace{\left\|\vect{p}_{i}-\vect{p}\sidx{des}\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{r_{1},\;w_{1}=1}
      + \underbrace{w_{v}\left\|\dot{\vect{p}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{r_{2},\;w_{2}=w_{v}}\Big),
\end{equation}
with $\vect{p}\sidx{des}$ passed in as the instruction. The velocity residual is not decoration: it
plays the role of the cascade's derivative gain, and without it the planner reaches the setpoint at
full thrust and overshoots --- minimum cumulative position error being achieved by arriving as fast
as possible.

\emph{The plan and its parameterisation.} The plan is a time-indexed spline over control knots
$\vect{\theta}$ rather than a raw action sequence \parencite[\S3.3]{howell2022predictive}; the
zero-order-hold interpolant is used here, with one knot per horizon step, so the spline degenerates
to the action sequence itself and the parameterisation buys nothing but interface uniformity.

\emph{The search.} Each call performs a fixed number of iterations of Algorithm~4 of
\parencite{howell2022predictive}: draw $N-1$ perturbations
$\vect{\theta}_{i} \sim \Normal(\vect{\theta},\sigma^{2})$, roll out all $N$ candidates --- the
incumbent included --- from the measured state, and keep
$\vect{\theta} \leftarrow \arg\min_{i} J(\vect{\theta}^{(i)})$. It is derivative-free and zero-order;
its authors present it as an elementary baseline. Between control steps the plan is shifted forward
and re-sampled, which is the warm-start of
\parencite[Alg.~1]{howell2022predictive} and the reason a few iterations per step suffice.

\emph{One deliberate departure.} Contacts are disabled in the planner's copy of the model. The
tracker therefore plans against the vehicle's free-flight dynamics only, and obstacle avoidance
remains entirely the projector's job. This is not a shortcut: it keeps the constraint mechanism a
single identifiable component, so that swapping the tracker cannot silently change how constraints
are enforced --- which is what makes the swap a controlled comparison rather than a confound.
Defaults: $N = 16$, $H\sidx{mjpc} = 0.3\,\textrm{s}$ of physics steps, five improvement iterations
per call, $\sigma = 0.3$, $w_{v} = 0.1$.
\srcnote{\texttt{mix\_uav\_test/mjpc\_tracker.py:70--155}, which wraps
\texttt{mujoco\_mpc.mjx.predictive\_sampling} unmodified.}
