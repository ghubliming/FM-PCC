\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary}\label{sec:conc:summary}

This thesis replaced the stochastic diffusion engine of a constrained predictive controller with
deterministic ODE transport, holding the backbone, the parameter count, the projector, the data and
the evaluation harness fixed, and asked what that substitution buys under a strict definition of
improvement.

The substitution turns the sampling budget from a property of the trained model into a parameter of
the integrator. That is the whole result, and everything else follows from it. On the benchmark the
baseline reports on, the MeanFlow engine attains equal-or-better success and constraint satisfaction
with fewer control steps at a single network evaluation per plan, against the baseline's twenty ---
Pareto dominance in the strong sense, architecture-matched. Decomposed, the engine contributes about
a $1.4\times$ compute advantage at equal budget; the budget contributes the order of magnitude. On a
harder, vision-conditioned task the same engine beats the baseline decisively on plan quality, and
that comparison also shows that it is the few-step \emph{objective} rather than transport alone that
separates: plain flow matching ties the baseline there. On a quadrotor embodiment built for this
work, the stack transfers to an underactuated, open-loop-unstable plant and flies it through static
obstacle fields under a cascaded geometric controller.

Alongside the performance results the thesis contributes three things that are not performance
results. A \textbf{correctness condition} for evaluating in-loop guided samplers: below a computable
number of genuine steps such a sampler is sample-then-project, and a comparison run there measures
nothing --- derived, then confirmed by the instrumentation at run time. A \textbf{regime taxonomy}
and the argument that reporting it is the cheapest defence against a general threat to validity, on
the evidence of a scene in this work's own results that is feasible, well-instrumented, and unable to
support the comparison run on it. And a \textbf{negative result with a derived mechanism}: an
additional engine that does not improve on its predecessor in any of four environments, kept and
explained rather than dropped.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}\label{sec:conc:rqs}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 --- Engine.] \textbf{Yes, and the improvement orders the family only partially.} Under an
    identical backbone, projector, dataset and harness, replacing diffusion with deterministic
    transport improves the quality--cost frontier: the MeanFlow engine Pareto-dominates the pinned
    baseline on the state-based benchmark and beats it decisively on plan quality on the
    vision-conditioned one. The family is ordered as \emph{MeanFlow ahead of the rest}, with plain
    flow matching and the bootstrapped target clustered behind it; the planned finer ordering
    between those two does not hold, and \autoref{sec:disc:negative} gives the measurement and the
    mechanism. Plain transport alone buys about $1.4\times$ at equal budget and ties the baseline on
    quality --- a cost result, not a quality one.

  \item[RQ2 --- Budget.] \textbf{Down to a single network evaluation, and the mechanism is that the
    budget changes hands.} The transport engines hold their quality from twenty evaluations to one;
    the baseline scores $0.667$ at one evaluation against $1.000$ at ten, at its own published
    protocol. For diffusion the budget is fixed when the noise schedule is discretised at training
    time; for transport it is a choice made by the integrator at inference, and the average-velocity
    objective is what makes one Euler step of the learned field a usable plan. \guard{The
    low-budget baseline cells are ten episodes per cell; the direction is far outside that
    resolution, the exact value is not, and a clean sweep at this work's protocol is outstanding.}

  \item[RQ3 --- Constraints.] \textbf{Regime-dependent, not superior --- and the question is only
    well-posed above a computable floor.} The second half of the question turns out to be the more
    important one: the comparison between the two arms is meaningless where the number of genuine
    steps reaches zero, because the endpoint arm then \emph{is} the iterate arm. Above that floor,
    endpoint projection is the cheaper arm in all three entries, at parity on safety: a cost
    crossover at a budget of three on the state-based benchmark, a $325$\,ms-per-step latency win at
    a budget of ten on the vision-conditioned entry, and an $8$--$21\times$ projection-cost advantage
    with zero unsafe rollouts in 210 on the aerial entry. It is not \emph{superior} --- no
    safety advantage survives its significance test --- and this thesis stops sweeping for one.

  \item[RQ4 --- Transfer.] \textbf{Yes across embodiment; yes across a harder and
    differently-observed task, but not, on this evidence, as a state-to-vision transfer.} The
    engine result reproduces on the vision-conditioned task and on the aerial embodiment, and the
    constraint-arm result reproduces in all three entries at three different strengths, so the
    answers to RQ1--RQ3 are not properties of one benchmark. Two qualifications are load-bearing.
    The vision-conditioned entry changes the control problem \emph{and} the observation modality at
    once, so it cannot separate the two increments, and the state-based leg that would separate them
    was never run. The aerial entry's \emph{ranking} is one seed of one scene; its embodiment
    argument, regime taxonomy and projection results do not depend on that ranking and stand
    without it.
\end{description}

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:conc:future}

\subsection*{What would settle what is currently unsettled}

Four items, in order of evidence gained per unit of compute, would move claims in this thesis from
qualified to banked. Raising the vision-conditioned entry to fifty enumerated contexts multiplies its
paired sample fivefold with no retraining and brings the protocol close to the benchmark's own.
Two to three additional seeds on the aerial pillars scene bank the aerial ranking. Evaluating all
engines at the baseline's published two-trial protocol on one binary completes the two-tier protocol
argument, at about a tenth of the cost of the tier already paid for. And building the
three-dimensional \emph{state} leg of the aligning task --- one configuration, four trained engines,
one evaluation --- is what would separate harder control from harder perception and make the
state-to-vision transfer claimable at all.

\subsection*{Directions beyond this thesis}

\hole{Outline only. Two directions are drafted in

Two directions do follow directly from results in this thesis and can be stated now. First, the
budget and the activation threshold should be chosen \emph{together}: \autoref{sec:disc:interpretation}
argues they are one decision, and this work maps that trade-off at two points rather than across it.
Mapping it properly would turn a regime argument into a design rule. Second, the candidate fan should
be parallelised rather than shrunk --- the fan is free on the generator and linear on a projector
that is currently solved serially on the CPU, so the same latency is available without discarding
candidates. That is a code change with a predicted, measurable effect, and
\autoref{sec:res:ablations} states both the prediction and its uncertainty.
