\chapter{Experimental Setup}\label{ch:setup}

The evaluation has two phases. First, the generative models and projection methods are established on
the obstacle-avoidance benchmark of \ac{DPCC}, with its setup taken over unchanged so that the diffusion
baseline is met on its own benchmark. Second, they are tested further in two environments built for
this thesis: a vision-conditioned alignment task and a quadrotor benchmark with three scenes.
Throughout this chapter and the results, these environments are called \emph{D3IL-avoiding},
\emph{D3IL-aligning}, \emph{UAV-corridor}, \emph{UAV-pillars}, and \emph{UAV-s-curve}; the descriptive
subsection titles and these prefixed names refer to the same tasks. \autoref{sec:method:envs} describes how each environment is constructed; this
chapter gives, for each of the three, the task and constraint values, the data, the metrics it is scored
on, the baseline it is compared with, and the training and evaluation protocol.

\section{Simulated Platforms}\label{sec:setup:platforms}
% =============================================================================

Every environment is simulated by MuJoCo, the rigid-body physics engine used throughout this thesis
(\autoref{sec:bg:quadrotor}) \parencite{todorov2012mujoco}. The two
manipulation tasks run the seven-joint Panda manipulator in MuJoCo, with the model files, task
definitions and demonstrations of D3IL \parencite{jia2024towards}. The quadrotor benchmark runs the Skydio X2 model from MuJoCo Menagerie
\parencite{zakka2022menagerie} with the quadrotor task patch distributed by MuJoCo MPC
\parencite{howell2022predictive}. MuJoCo is the simulator in every case; D3IL and MuJoCo MPC supply the
task files and data that run in it. \autoref{fig:platforms} shows both models; the X2 dimensions used
in projection are drawn in \autoref{fig:platform-x2-dimensions} in \autoref{app:repro}.
\autoref{tab:platforms} gives the properties that enter the experiments.

How a plan reaches each of them differs. On the manipulator the planner commands an end-effector
increment; inverse kinematics turns each Cartesian setpoint into joint commands. On the quadrotor the
planner updates a commanded position increment once every three $0.01$\,s MuJoCo physics steps.
Between planner updates, the cascaded geometric controller of \autoref{sec:method:geometric}
computes rotor thrusts at every physics step.

\section{Tasks and Environments}\label{sec:setup:tasks}
% =============================================================================

The three environments differ in what the planner observes, how many dimensions its plan has, and which
constraint forms the projection must satisfy. Each is given below with its task, its constraint set and the
values that enter the experiments.

\subsection{Obstacle Avoidance}\label{sec:setup:tasks:avoiding}

The obstacle-avoidance task is the benchmark of \ac{DPCC}, simulated in MuJoCo from the task files and
demonstrations of D3IL: the same environment, Panda manipulator, demonstrations, constraint geometries, projection configuration and evaluation
protocol \parencite{jia2024towards,romer2025diffusion} (\autoref{sec:method:env:avoiding}). Holding
that complete benchmark fixed provides the controlled validation of replacing DPCC's diffusion model
with the three flow-based objectives. A manipulator moves its
end effector across a field of six obstacles to a goal line (\autoref{fig:env-avoiding}).

At test time
the plan is constrained by halfspaces that the demonstrations never respect, in three geometries --- \emph{top-left-hard}, \emph{top-right-hard} and \emph{both-hard}, defined in \autoref{tab:avoiding-geometries} and drawn in \autoref{fig:constraints-avoiding-sets} --- together with the circular keep-out regions
of \autoref{sec:method:env:avoiding}. The geometries are those of the \ac{DPCC} configuration \parencite[Sec.~6.1]{romer2025diffusion}, and all three share the workspace $x\in[0.2,0.8]$, $y\in[-0.3,0.4]$, the six obstacles of radius $0.025$ and the goal line at $y=0.35$. Every
constraint also has a tightened version, shrunk by the margin of $0.025$ that \ac{DPCC} introduces and
applies to this benchmark \parencite[Sec.~5.3]{romer2025diffusion} (\autoref{sec:method:dpcc}). The same
holds for every constraint set of this thesis, and one convention is fixed here for every result that
follows: \textbf{every result of \autoref{ch:results} is on the tightened set.} It is the set \ac{DPCC}
evaluates on, so it is the set on which the baseline is compared at its own protocol; it is the set on
which a projected plan satisfies the constraint at all --- on the quadrotor corridor the untightened
per-step projector satisfied it on no flight while its tightened twin did on every one --- and the plain
set, being the tightened one without its margin, adds nothing a reader could rank on. Untightened
evaluations are therefore not reported. An unprojected plan is the same plan on either set, and its
violations are scored against the tightened boundary.
\srcnote{\texttt{config/projection\_eval.yaml}: \texttt{halfspace\_constraints['avoiding-d3il']},
\texttt{enlarge\_constraints: \{'avoiding': 0.025\}}; parity with \texttt{aux\_repo/dpcc} checked in
v2, \autoref{sec:method:env:avoiding}.}

Each geometry is two things (\autoref{fig:constraints-avoiding-sets}). The \emph{halfspace} is a straight
line across the field with one side declared out of bounds for the planned end-effector position: the plan
must stay below the line, so the region above it --- the shaded corner --- is excluded, and the constraint
is a single linear row per plan step. The \emph{keep-out disk} is a circle of radius $0.08$\,m that the
planned position must stay outside of, placed over one of the posts so that it covers the post and a margin
of free table around it; it is the quadratic row of the set, and it is what makes the free lane through the
field a corridor between the halfspace on one side and the disk on the other. \emph{both-hard} has two
halfspaces, one from each side, and its disk on the first post. Neither element is a physical object: the
posts are the only obstacles the arm can touch, and a plan that crosses a halfspace or enters a disk violates
the constraint without any contact. The tightened version of each set moves the line and enlarges the disk by
$0.025$\,m, the dashed boundaries of the figure.

\subsection{Vision-Conditioned Alignment}\label{sec:setup:tasks:aligning}

The alignment task of D3IL \parencite{jia2024towards} asks the manipulator to push a box into a target
pose on a table. The task runs in MuJoCo; its task files, demonstrations and camera set-up are D3IL's. Around them this thesis
built a constrained-control environment (\autoref{sec:method:env:aligning}): a three-dimensional plan
with its dynamics rows, a constraint set on the planned end-effector position, and the coupling of the
visual encoder to the generative network. The policy observes two RGB camera
streams, a front view and an in-hand view, instead of the state (\autoref{fig:aligning-cameras}). Each
episode starts from an initial box pose and a target pose, together called a context. The tightened
constraints are shrunk by $0.03$\,m.
\srcnote{\texttt{config/visual\_aligning\_eval.yaml}: \texttt{workspace\_bounds},
\texttt{enlarge\_constraints: 0.03}.}

Where the box is can only be seen. The vector half of the observation is
$(\vect{p}\sidx{des},\vect{p})\in\R^{6}$, the commanded and the measured end-effector position
(\autoref{sec:method:env:aligning}); it carries no box pose, no target pose and no object state of any
kind. The two RGB streams are the sole source of both, so the policy must locate the box in the images
before it can move toward it --- which is why a $0.10$\,m box in a $0.20\times0.15$\,m draw region is a
harder problem than its planar geometry suggests.
\srcnote{\texttt{fm\_visual\_aligning/datasets/sequence.py}: \texttt{ParityAligningDataset.OBS\_DIM = 6},
the 9D visual trajectory used for training (\texttt{diffuser\_visual\_aligning\_test/
train\_visual\_aligning\_dpcc.py:168--170}); the 23D \texttt{StateOnlyAligningDataset} that does carry
the box and target pose is not used for any result in this thesis. Draw ranges:
\texttt{gym\_aligning/envs/aligning.py:62--67} (\texttt{box\_space}, \texttt{target\_space}).}

Obstacle avoidance is a planar task that imitation policies already solve: the best policies reported
by D3IL reach a success rate of $0.95$ on it \parencite[Table~3]{jia2024towards}, so it tests constraint
satisfaction on a plan that is easy to produce. This environment raises three things at once. The plan is
three-dimensional rather than planar; the policy sees the scene through two cameras and a visual encoder
rather than through a state vector; and the task itself is not solved --- the best image-based policy
reported by D3IL finishes roughly a third of its episodes (\autoref{sec:setup:baselines:aligning},
\autoref{tab:va-vs-d3il}). It therefore asks whether the \ac{DPCC} construction still holds where the plan
is harder to produce, and it puts the projection where only some episodes finish at all.

The active geometry is \emph{combined-5} (\autoref{tab:aligning-constraints},
\autoref{fig:aligning-contexts}): a halfspace that cuts off the far corner of the table, a circular
keep-out region between the box and its target --- the same two geometric forms D3IL-avoiding uses, on a
task whose plan is three-dimensional. Neither is respected by the demonstrations, so satisfying them is
left to the projection.
\srcnote{\texttt{config/visual\_aligning\_eval.yaml}: \texttt{active\_geo\_variants: [combined\_5]},

The context is drawn at random, and this is the difference from obstacle avoidance that matters most for
what the policy has to do. In obstacle avoidance every episode begins at one fixed end-effector position
and the six posts never move, so a policy can learn a route. Here the box is placed anywhere in
$x\in[0.4,0.6]$, $y\in[-0.25,-0.10]$ at any yaw in $\pm 90^{\circ}$, and its target anywhere in
$x\in[0.4,0.6]$, $y\in[0.20,0.35]$ at any yaw in $\pm 90^{\circ}$; the demonstrations were recorded from
such draws and the evaluation replays an enumerated set of them (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:aligning}).
Two episodes therefore ask for two different pushes. \autoref{fig:aligning-contexts} draws the ten
contexts the evaluation replays, which are the ten every result in \autoref{sec:res:visual} is averaged
over. They are ten different pushes: their lengths range from $0.41$ to $0.52$\,m and average
$0.4530$\,m, the starting distance \autoref{sec:res:visual} reports, so a policy that had learned one
route would satisfy none of them. One context in that figure is drawn with the real box footprints at
their recorded yaw, which every draw carries, because the marks alone understate the object --- a
$0.10$\,m box has to be pushed around a $0.06$\,m keep-out region.

\subsection{Quadrotor Benchmark}\label{sec:setup:tasks:uav}

The quadrotor benchmark was built for this thesis around the Skydio X2 model of MuJoCo Menagerie
\parencite{zakka2022menagerie} (\autoref{sec:method:env:uav}). The vehicle is underactuated and open-loop
unstable (\autoref{sec:bg:quadrotor}), and its plan is tracked by the controller of
\autoref{sec:method:deployment}. Three scenes are defined
(\autoref{fig:env-uav}, \autoref{tab:uav-scenes}):

\begin{description}
  \item[UAV-pillars] --- the cross-embodiment test. The obstacle field of D3IL-avoiding
    (\autoref{sec:setup:tasks:avoiding}) is scaled by $36$, so that the rod's radius becomes the vehicle's
    rotor reach, and its six obstacles are extruded into pillars; the three test-time constraint
    geometries of that benchmark are mapped into the arena at the same scale. The planner is the one
    trained on the avoiding demonstrations and is not retrained: the quadrotor executes the plans it
    produces, so the scene asks whether a plan the projector has repaired on the table is flyable by a
    vehicle, with a plant's tracking error between the plan and the path. It is a scene on which the
    constraint is new to the data, which is what gives the projector something to do;
    \autoref{sec:res:uav:pillars} reads it on those terms.
  \item[UAV-corridor] --- the three-dimensional test. Two parallel walls leave a clear width of
    $1.90$\,m, and the scene carries two test-time constraints, evaluated separately, as the geometries
    of D3IL-avoiding are. The first, \emph{tilt}, is a halfspace boundary that slopes by $14^\circ$
    across the corridor in the horizontal plane and is leaned over by $60^\circ$ from the vertical about
    the launch altitude, so that one plane pushes the vehicle sideways and downward at once: at the
    launch altitude a sideways move alone does not clear it on any lane. The second, \emph{hump}, is a
    roof in the vertical plane that rises from the floor at $x=-1.5$\,m to $1.10$\,m at $x=0$ and falls
    back to the floor at $x=1.5$\,m, which the vehicle must climb over; the ceiling of the workspace box
    is raised for it. The models are trained on the demonstrations of a narrower, level corridor and
    never see either boundary, so both are test-time constraints in the same sense as the halfspaces of
    D3IL-avoiding.
  \item[UAV-s-curve] --- the tracking-controller caveat. Two offset corridor segments, joined by a
    diagonal passage, require two sharp changes of direction, and the scene is reported for what those
    turns do to the controller (\autoref{sec:res:uav:controller}), not as a third benchmark.
\end{description}

\subsection{Quadrotor Benchmark}\label{sec:setup:data:uav}

Two of the three scenes have quadrotor demonstrations, and one has none. On UAV-pillars the planner is
the one trained on the demonstrations of D3IL-avoiding (\autoref{sec:setup:data:avoiding}) --- human
teleoperation of a manipulator, recorded on the table --- and it is not retrained: the scene is that
obstacle field scaled into an arena, and the quadrotor executes the plans the planner produces there
(\autoref{sec:setup:tasks:uav}). What the scene therefore tests is the execution of a projected plan by a
different plant, and not a policy trained to fly; a quadrotor deployed outside simulation would need
demonstrations of its own, which this thesis does not have.
\guard{On UAV-pillars the demonstrations are the manipulator's. Nothing on that scene is evidence about
learning to fly from flight data; it is evidence about whether a plan repaired by the projector survives
execution by a vehicle with tracking error.}

The demonstrations of UAV-corridor and UAV-s-curve were generated for this thesis as described in
\autoref{sec:method:env:uav}. That section gives the waypoint-and-arc construction, cosine speed profile,
tracking equations, rejection rule and recorded observation--action pair; \autoref{tab:uav-demos} records
only their numerical and scene-specific parameters. The corridor with the sloping halfspace and the roof
reuses the demonstrations and trained models of the level corridor, because both boundaries are test-time
constraints.

These demonstrations are not human. There is no public teleoperated quadrotor dataset for these scenes,
so the data this thesis trains on was \emph{generated} for it: a reference path is laid out analytically
per passage class (\autoref{tab:uav-demos}), and every episode is then \emph{flown in MuJoCo} by the same
cascaded geometric controller the evaluation uses (\autoref{sec:method:deployment}), under the same
physics and at the same time step. What is recorded is the measured state of that flight, not the
reference. An episode is kept only if the flight it produced stays airborne and within its contact
budget (\autoref{tab:uav-demos}). Each
demonstration is therefore a trajectory the plant has been shown to track, and \emph{expert} means that
in this thesis and nothing more: a competent generated flight, not a human one.

Where these demonstrations differ from the two manipulation datasets is that they were generated against
the obstacles they fly among, and \autoref{fig:expert-uav} shows what follows from it. On UAV-s-curve the
single route satisfies the constraint set the projection later enforces, tightening included: the
generator threads a passage the scene already contains, so nothing in the data has to be corrected. The
projection is then asked to repair a plan that was never broken, which is why that scene can expose a
limit of the tracking controller but cannot order the projection methods.

UAV-corridor is built the other way, and it is the way the obstacle-avoidance benchmark is built. The three
lanes clear both walls, but every one of them crosses the tilt --- at the launch altitude, where the leaned
plane cuts the corridor along the same line as before it was leaned, by $0.24$, $0.36$ and $0.47$\,m for the
left, centre and right lane --- and every one of them passes under the hump, whose roof at its peak stands
$1.49$\,m above the floor once inflated while the demonstrations fly between $0.90$ and $1.30$\,m. Both
boundaries are introduced at test time and did not exist when the demonstrations were flown. The scene in
which the constraint is new to the data is exactly the scene in which the projection has something to do.


UAV-pillars is the second kind by construction, and more sharply than the corridor: its constraint sets are
the three test-time geometries of D3IL-avoiding, which $0$, $1$ and $2$ of the $96$ demonstrations satisfy
(\autoref{sec:setup:tasks:avoiding}), so on that scene the projector has the same repair to do as on the
table, and the vehicle adds only what a plant adds --- tracking error between the plan and the path.
\autoref{sec:res:uav:pillars} reads it on those terms.

That figure draws the references the generator laid out rather than the flights that tracked them, which
is the conservative choice: a constraint crossed by the reference is crossed by construction rather than
by tracking error.

One thing has to be read correctly in that figure and in every quadrotor result that follows. A violation
is a property of the \emph{path}, not of the aircraft: what is tested is the line of vehicle centres
against the constraint surfaces, and those surfaces already carry the $0.31$\,m rotor reach
(\autoref{tab:eval}), so the vehicle's own extent is inside the boundary rather than measured against it.
A route turns red when its centre line crosses an inflated surface, and nothing is ever scored by sweeping
the rotor disks through an obstacle. The scenes are built to be flyable on those terms --- the free
channel that remains after inflation is a real one --- so a red route means a plan that had somewhere else
to go, not a vehicle too large for the gap.

\section{Metrics}\label{sec:setup:metrics}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Obstacle Avoidance}\label{sec:setup:metrics:avoiding}

The metrics are those of \ac{DPCC} \parencite[Sec.~6.1]{romer2025diffusion}: the number of control
steps to reach the goal (steps per episode), the success rate of reaching the goal (fraction of episodes),
the success rate of reaching the goal while satisfying the constraints at every step (fraction of
episodes), and the number of control steps on which a constraint is violated (steps per episode).

The first of those is a headline number in both works this chapter measures against, and not a
quantity introduced here: \ac{DPCC} reports the control steps an episode takes beside its success
rates \parencite[Sec.~6.1]{romer2025diffusion}, and the endpoint-projection work reports, on this same
D3IL task, the average number of steps the manipulator takes to reach the target on its constraint-satisfying
episodes as one of its three columns \parencite[Sec.~VII-A]{li2025hardflow}. A plan that arrives in
fewer control steps is a plan that completes the task sooner, which is why it is reported next to
success rather than folded into it.

To these this thesis adds the time to compute one action (milliseconds, or seconds where a
configuration is slow enough to warrant it), the quantity \ac{DPCC} states for its own
method \parencite[Sec.~6]{romer2025diffusion}. It is measured around the planner at every control step
and covers everything the planner does to produce the action that is executed: generating the $B$
candidate plans, projecting them onto the constraint set, and selecting one. The same measurement is
used in all three environments, and
\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:compute} gives the machine every configuration was measured on.

\subsection{Vision-Conditioned Alignment}\label{sec:setup:metrics:aligning}

Each episode is scored by
\begin{itemize}
  \item the final distance between the box and its target in the table plane (metres), summarised over the
    contexts by its median and its minimum, and the share of the initial distance that was closed,
    $(d\sidx{init}-d\sidx{final})/d\sidx{init}$ in per cent, against an initial distance averaging
    $0.4530$\,m: $100\,\%$ is a box brought onto its target and $0\,\%$ one that was not moved towards it
    at all;
  \item whether the box was moved at all (a count of contexts);
  \item the number of control steps on which a constraint is violated (steps per episode);
  \item the time to compute one action (milliseconds), as in \autoref{sec:setup:metrics:avoiding}.
\end{itemize}
When generative models are compared, the distance is measured on plans executed without projection, so
that it reflects the generative model alone; when projection methods are compared, it is measured with
projection.
\srcnote{\texttt{per\_rollout\_detail.csv}: \texttt{context\_final\_xy\_dist}, \texttt{frozen},
\texttt{n\_violations}, \texttt{avg\_time\_ms}. The recorded box angle is not used: the evaluation writes
\texttt{context\_final\_box\_angle\_deg} under an inconsistent convention across runs, so no orientation
metric is defined here or reported in \autoref{ch:results}.}

\subsection{Quadrotor Benchmark}\label{sec:setup:metrics:uav}

Each flight is scored by
\begin{itemize}
  \item success: the vehicle crosses the finish line of its scene. This is the success
    criterion of D3IL-avoiding transferred to the air --- there an episode succeeds when the end effector
    crosses the goal line (\autoref{sec:setup:metrics:avoiding}), here when the vehicle crosses the line
    that closes its route. Every quadrotor table and figure of \autoref{ch:results} calls a flight that
    does so a \emph{success} and prints the count of them;
  \item whether it is collision-free: no control step on which the flown path violates a constraint. The
    test is on the path, not on the airframe: the surfaces it is tested against already carry the
    $0.31$\,m rotor reach (\autoref{tab:eval});
  \item success with constraint satisfaction: success on a collision-free flight, as on
    D3IL-avoiding;
  \item on UAV-pillars, the metrics of D3IL-avoiding (\autoref{sec:setup:metrics:avoiding}) scored on the
    flown path --- success at the mapped goal line, success with constraint satisfaction, violating
    control steps, control steps --- so that a plan's outcome on the table and its outcome in the air are
    read in the same units, together with whether the vehicle made contact with a pillar;
  \item the number of control steps (steps per flight), and the final distance to the goal point of the
    route (metres), which describes where a flight that does not succeed ends;
  \item the time to compute one action (milliseconds, \autoref{sec:setup:metrics:avoiding}), split into
    generation and projection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Aggregation and Variability}\label{sec:setup:metrics:spread}

Every metric above is measured once per episode and reported as an average over many of them, so a bare
average would hide how much it moves. \autoref{ch:results} therefore prints \emph{mean $\pm$ spread}
wherever a spread exists, and what the spread is over depends on what the result is a claim about.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multi-seed results} --- every D3IL-avoiding table --- are reported as the mean over the
    five training seeds and the \emph{sample standard deviation across those seeds}. A claim there is a
    claim about a model and not about one training run, so the variation that matters is how much the
    result moves when the model is trained again. It is also the larger of the two spreads available and
    therefore the conservative one. Each seed is first averaged over the constraint geometries it covers,
    then the mean and the standard deviation are taken over the seeds
    (\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:avoiding}).
  \item \textbf{Single-seed results} --- the one-seed D3IL-avoiding tables, D3IL-aligning and the
    quadrotor scenes --- carry no seed dimension, so the spread is the sample standard deviation
    \emph{across the episodes, contexts or flights} of the cell, which is stated with each table. Where a
    cell averages several constraint geometries, the within-geometry standard deviations are pooled.
  \item \textbf{Counts are printed as counts.} A result of the form \enquote{$10/10$ flights} or
    \enquote{$20/20$ episodes} is not an average and gets no $\pm$; neither does a median, which is
    reported with the number of contexts it is taken over.
\end{itemize}

\section{Baselines}\label{sec:setup:baselines}
% =============================================================================

Three generative models are compared with the diffusion model of \ac{DPCC} (\emph{Diffusion} in tables):
\emph{instantaneous-velocity matching} (\emph{FM}) \parencite{lipman2023flow}, which learns the
instantaneous velocity of the probability path; \emph{analytic average-velocity matching}
(\emph{MeanFM}) \parencite{geng2025meanflow}, which learns the average velocity over an interval through
an analytic Jacobian--vector-product target; and \emph{consistency-interpolated average-velocity
matching} (\emph{CI-MeanFM}) \parencite{zhang2025alphaflow}, whose consistency step ratio $\alpha$
interpolates the target between the instantaneous velocity at $\alpha=1$ and a stop-gradient prediction
towards the analytic average-velocity objective as $\alpha\to0$. In this thesis $\alpha$ is annealed from
$1$ and held at a final value
$\alpha_{\mathrm{end}}$, which each result states. Two projection methods are compared: the per-step projection of
\ac{DPCC} and endpoint projection \parencite{li2025hardflow}. All generative models share the temporal
U-Net backbone of the baseline, with $4.0$\,M parameters, unless a table states otherwise. Throughout
all figures and tables, the four models are labelled \emph{Diffusion}, \emph{FM}, \emph{MeanFM}, and
\emph{CI-MeanFM}, respectively; running prose retains the full mechanism names.

\subsection{Obstacle Avoidance}\label{sec:setup:baselines:avoiding}

The baseline is \ac{DPCC} in the configuration its authors report: the diffusion model at a step budget
of $\nfe=20$, per-step projection on tightened constraints, and selection by
cumulative projection cost over $B=4$ candidate plans \parencite[Sec.~6.1]{romer2025diffusion}.
\autoref{tab:target} places the published result next to its reproduction in this thesis. The baseline is
compared on the tightened constraint set, and so is every other configuration of this thesis
(\autoref{sec:setup:tasks:avoiding}): tightening is the margin \ac{DPCC} itself introduces so that a plan
projected onto the shrunk set stays feasible under the tracking and discretisation error of execution
\parencite[Sec.~5.3]{romer2025diffusion}, it is the setting under which \ac{DPCC} reports its results, and it
is the setting that allows the safer execution --- a plan that clears the tightened boundary clears the
real one with a margin to spare. Comparing on the plain set would give up that margin and, on this
benchmark, the constraint satisfaction with it.

\subsection{Vision-Conditioned Alignment}\label{sec:setup:baselines:aligning}

No published result exists for the configuration evaluated here, so there is no external baseline to
reproduce, as there is on D3IL-avoiding. The task itself is D3IL's \parencite{jia2024towards}, which
evaluates imitation-learning policies on it from images: two $96\times96$ RGB views, front and in-hand,
each passed through its own ResNet-18 convolutional trunk with spatial-softmax pooling.

What D3IL scores as \emph{success} on this task is a threshold on the final pose of the box, and both
halves of it must hold at once: the box centre within $0.018$\,m of the target centre \emph{and} the box
orientation within $0.048\pi \approx 8.6^{\circ}$ of the target orientation, the second of which is what
makes the coloured sides line up. An episode that meets both counts as $1$ and every other episode as $0$;
the published figure is the share of episodes counted $1$.

That metric is not used here. The planner of this thesis is a different network with a different
training target and output --- a plan of positions that is projected and tracked, not an action applied
directly (\autoref{tab:va-vs-d3il}) --- so the published success rate does not transfer to it. The
alignment task is scored by the final distance of the box to its target
(\autoref{sec:setup:metrics:aligning}), and the D3IL numbers that follow describe how hard the task is.

From images the strongest policy is BESO, a
score-based policy with a history of observations, at $0.359\pm0.031$; next are DDPM-GPT at
$0.316\pm0.026$ and DDPM-ACT, which adds action chunking, at $0.278\pm0.071$. From the state the same task
is solved far more often: VAE-ACT reaches $0.891\pm0.022$ and BESO $0.861\pm0.016$. On obstacle avoidance
from the state BESO reaches $0.950\pm0.015$ \parencite[Table~3]{jia2024towards}. Seen from images, this is a
task that the policies of D3IL finish in roughly a third of episodes, where obstacle avoidance from the
state (D3IL-avoiding) is close to solved.

Those policies are small, and most of what they carry is perception. The \emph{head} --- everything behind
the image encoder --- is a four-layer transformer at an embedding width of $72$ for BESO and DDPM-GPT. For
DDPM-ACT and VAE-ACT it is an encoder--decoder pair at a width of $64$, two layers in the encoder and four
in the decoder; VAE-ACT carries a third two-layer stack at the same width, the variational branch that
produces its latent \parencite[Table~9]{jia2024towards}.

Counted out at those shapes, with the four-fold feed-forward expansion the implementation uses, a
width-$72$ block is $63.0$\,k parameters and the four-layer stack $0.25$\,M. A width-$64$ encoder block is
$49.9$\,k and a decoder block $62.3$\,k --- the difference is the three cross-attention projections --- so
DDPM-ACT's two stacks come to $0.35$\,M and VAE-ACT's three stacks, with its embeddings and output heads,
to $0.47$\,M. None of the four heads reaches half a million parameters.

What carries the weight of an image-conditioned policy there is the encoder, and it is not close: each of
the two camera streams has its own ResNet-18 trunk of roughly $11$\,M parameters, so the perception is
about $22$\,M against a policy of under half a million --- some fifty times the head it feeds. This thesis
takes that encoder over unchanged (\autoref{sec:method:backbone}) and places the $4.0$\,M temporal U-Net
of the state-based benchmark behind it, so the two set-ups differ in the planner and not in how the
images are seen.

\subsection{Quadrotor Benchmark}\label{sec:setup:baselines:uav}

The quadrotor benchmark is new, and no learned planner has been evaluated on it before. Its closest
precedent is the quadrotor task distributed with MuJoCo MPC \parencite{howell2022predictive}, which flies
the same Skydio X2 model \parencite{zakka2022menagerie}. That task is described here as it is
distributed, because the benchmark of this thesis starts from its vehicle model and departs from it in
everything else.

\paragraph{The original task.} The quadrotor task distributed with MuJoCo MPC
\parencite{howell2022predictive} is the following. The scene is a racing course of eight square gates, each a frame of four
capsules with a $1.5$\,m opening, placed at different headings and heights around a closed loop. The
vehicle starts on the floor. A goal marker leads it around the course: it sits at one of twelve fixed
positions, between $0.75$ and $2.25$\,m above the floor, placed so that flying from one to the next passes
through a gate, and it advances to the next position whenever the vehicle comes within $0.5$\,m of it,
returning to the first after the last. The gates do not appear in the objective; passing through them is
a consequence of where the goal positions are placed.

The controller is Predictive Sampling, the sampling-based predictive controller of MuJoCo MPC, and the
simulator itself is its model. At each planning instant it perturbs its current control sequence into 32
candidate sequences of the four rotor thrusts, each parameterised by five spline points, rolls every
candidate forward in MuJoCo over a horizon of $0.5$\,s at a time step of $0.01$\,s, and keeps the
cheapest. The cost of a rollout sums four quadratic terms over the horizon: the distance of the vehicle
from the current goal position, weighted $25$; its linear velocity and its angular velocity, each
weighted $1.25$; and the deviation of each rotor thrust from the hover thrust, weighted $10^{-3}$. The
first control of the kept sequence is applied to the rotors, and planning repeats. Nothing is learned,
no demonstrations are used, and no success rate is defined: the task is an interactive demonstration of
the controller.

\paragraph{This benchmark.} The benchmark of this thesis is built on the principle D3IL-avoiding and
\ac{DPCC} are built on \parencite{jia2024towards,romer2025diffusion}: a policy is trained on
demonstrations of a task, constraints the demonstrations do not satisfy are introduced at test time, and
each run is scored both for finishing the task and for satisfying those constraints at every step. It
keeps the vehicle model and the simulator of the original task and
changes the three things that make it a test of constrained generative planning. The plan comes
from a network trained on demonstrations instead of from sampling against the true dynamics; the
environment is a set of walls, pillars and a test-time sloping halfspace that the plan must not violate, instead of
gates that a goal sequence leads through; and the plan is a sequence of positions tracked by the
cascaded geometric controller (\autoref{sec:method:deployment}), instead of rotor thrusts applied
directly. Each flight is scored for success and for constraint satisfaction (\autoref{sec:setup:metrics:uav}).

\section{Training and Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:setup:protocol}
% =============================================================================

Every model is trained once per training seed, and the same checkpoint is then evaluated under every step
budget, projection method and selection rule; only the evaluation parameters vary between results. This
section gives the machine everything ran on, then the training parameters, then the evaluation ones.

\subsection{Compute Environment}\label{sec:setup:protocol:compute}

All training and evaluation reported in this thesis ran on a single shared GPU node of the
institute's cluster, scheduled with SLURM. The node carries two 16-core AMD EPYC~7282 processors
(32~physical cores, 64~hardware threads) and eight NVIDIA RTX~A5000 accelerators with 24~GB of
device memory each. \textbf{Every job was allocated exactly one GPU}: no result in this thesis comes
from a distributed or multi-GPU run. The full hardware and software inventory, including package
versions, is given in \autoref{app:repro}, \autoref{tab:compute}.

The measurements of \autoref{sec:setup:metrics} were taken on this machine; how its properties bear
on reading them is given in \autoref{app:repro}.

The optimiser settings in that table are not uniform across the models, and the reason is the training
target. The four objectives fit different quantities --- a noise residual, an instantaneous velocity, and
an average velocity over an interval --- and a gradient of one is not a gradient of another: the scale of
the loss, and how much it varies between two draws of the transport time, differ by construction. Each
model was therefore given the batch size, step size and weight averaging under which its own loss curve
converged, rather than a single setting imposed on all four. On D3IL-avoiding this makes the two
average-velocity models the ones trained with four times the batch and five times the learning rate of
instantaneous-velocity matching and the diffusion baseline, and the only ones evaluated with the
exponential moving average of their weights; the logit-normal transport-time sampler in their column is
the same kind of choice, made for the same reason.

What is held fixed is what the comparison rests on. All four models share the backbone, its parameter
count, the dataset, the plan horizon and the projection they are read through, and each is evaluated at
the step budgets stated with its results. What varies is how each objective was brought to convergence,
which is a property of the training run and not of the comparison.

Several of those parameters enter how a result should be read. The projector acts on planar position in D3IL-avoiding and spatial position in
D3IL-aligning and the UAV scenes; individual geometric predicates may use only some of those spatial
coordinates.

The \emph{step budget} counts network evaluations per plan, but it is not the same quantity in the two
families: for the flow-based models it is the number of ODE solver steps, chosen at inference, and for
the diffusion baseline the number of denoising steps, fixed at 20 when the model is trained. The
\emph{selection rule} is one of the three of \autoref{sec:method:selection} --- random, cumulative
projection cost, or temporal consistency --- and the \emph{projection method} one of the three of
\autoref{sec:method:proj}; both vary per result and neither is ever averaged over. \emph{Low-level
control} means inverse kinematics followed by a joint-space PD controller on the manipulator, and on the
quadrotor the cascaded geometric tracker of \autoref{sec:method:deployment}, which MuJoCo MPC replaces in
\autoref{sec:res:uav:controller} and nowhere else.

An \emph{episode} is one run of the task from its initial state to its end: the environment is reset, the
planner replans at every control step and executes the first action of its plan, and the run ends when the
goal is reached --- the goal line crossed, the box delivered, the finish line of the scene flown through
--- or when the \emph{episode limit} is reached. That limit is a hard cap on how long a single run may
go on, and nothing else. A planner that has lost the task would otherwise replan forever, and every control step of the
quadrotor scenes costs a projection solve, so a run that cannot finish is stopped and scored unsuccessful.
The three limits differ because the tasks are of different lengths: the quadrotor limits are set at $1.2$
times the longest demonstration of each scene, which is why the three scenes have three different values.

The \emph{activation threshold} is not held at one value either. It selects the last $\eta\nfe$ sampling
steps, so the cost of a projected plan is the cost of one solve times that count, and at the budgets
D3IL-aligning is run at the default of $0.5$ would mean solving ten programs for every one of the four
candidate plans at every one of up to $400$ control steps. That is not affordable at the scale of this
evaluation, and it is also not necessary: what endpoint projection needs is not a fixed \emph{share} of
the sampling steps but a fixed \emph{number} of steps left to guide. The threshold is therefore lowered as
the budget rises, so that the two alignment budgets are compared at the same number of guiding steps
rather than at the same share. The one movement in the other direction --- $\eta=1.0$ on D3IL-avoiding ---
is the same argument at the opposite end: at the budget floor half of the sampling steps is one step, and
one step is the terminal one, so the threshold is raised until the method has anything to act on at all.
\autoref{sec:setup:protocol:guiding} works that arithmetic through.

The \emph{tightening} margin is $0.025$\,m in all three environments, the value \ac{DPCC} introduces. It
shrinks the feasible set, so that a plan which satisfies a constraint numerically still has room left to be
tracked, and it is the one quantity the tightened variants of a constraint set change.

\emph{Body inflation} is a second offset and a different quantity, and only the quadrotor carries it. A
constraint is written for a point, and the manipulator's end effector is treated as one; a quadrotor is
not a point, so every surface of the quadrotor scenes is moved outward before the projection ever sees it
--- by the vehicle's rotor reach of $0.31$\,m. The configuration also defines a planning pad of $0.02$\,m
on top of that reach, but every scene evaluated here switches the pad off for the planner, so the planner
and the collision scorer are offset by the same $0.31$\,m. The $0.31$\,m is the
largest per-axis distance from the centre of mass to the rim of a rotor disk at zero yaw: the four rotors
sit at $(\pm 0.14, \pm 0.18)$\,m and each disk has a radius of $0.13$\,m, so the reach is
$0.18+0.13=0.31$\,m across the vehicle and $0.14+0.13=0.27$\,m along it, and the larger of the two is
taken. The two offsets are kept apart in the configuration and are kept apart here, because they answer
different questions: $0.025$ is margin, $0.31$ is geometry.

\subsubsection{Vision-Conditioned Alignment}\label{sec:setup:protocol:aligning}

\subsection{Guiding Steps of Endpoint Projection}\label{sec:setup:protocol:guiding}

Two of the evaluation parameters above interact, and their product decides whether a configuration can
carry a statement about endpoint projection at all. One worked example is enough to read every projected
result in \autoref{ch:results}.

Endpoint projection runs on the sampling steps the activation threshold selects, which are the last
$\lceil \eta\nfe \rceil$ of the $\nfe$ steps. Of those, the terminal step is not a guiding one: there the
predicted endpoint \emph{is} the finished sample, no later step is left for the network to react to, and
what runs is a projection applied to a finished plan --- which is what per-step projection does at that
step too. The steps that remain are the guiding ones:
%
\begin{equation*}
  n\sidx{guide}(\nfe,\eta) \;=\; \lceil \eta\nfe \rceil - 1 ,
\end{equation*}
%
as described in \autoref{sec:method:degenerate}.

Read it on the two settings that matter most in \autoref{ch:results}. On D3IL-aligning at
$\nfe=20$ with $\eta=0.2$, the projection runs on $\lceil 4 \rceil = 4$ sampling steps and three of them
are guiding ones, so the reported endpoint-projection rows there are the method doing its own work. On
D3IL-avoiding at the budget the benchmark is actually run at, $\nfe=2$ with the default $\eta=0.5$, the
projection runs on $\lceil 1 \rceil = 1$ sampling step and that step is the terminal one --- so
$n\sidx{guide}=0$, endpoint and per-step projection compute the same plan, and a difference between them
there would be noise rather than a property of either method. The same arithmetic gives zero at $\nfe=1$
for every threshold below $1.0$.

Three rules follow from that, and they hold for every result in \autoref{ch:results}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A configuration with $n\sidx{guide}=0$ is reported separately and never averaged with
    configurations that have guiding steps.
  \item The count is not assumed. The evaluation records how many programs the projection solved per
    plan, and that number is compared against $\lceil \eta\nfe \rceil$ before a result is reported.
  \item The threshold is stated with every projected result that departs from the default of
    \autoref{tab:eval}.
\end{itemize}

Which configurations the first rule excludes, and how closely the recorded program counts follow the
prediction, is reported with the results (\autoref{sec:res:constraints:degenerate}).
