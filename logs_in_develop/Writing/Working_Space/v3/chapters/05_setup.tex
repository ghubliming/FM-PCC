\chapter{Experimental Setup}\label{ch:setup}

This chapter fixes what is measured, on what, against what, and under which protocol. Three
commitments in it are load-bearing for every table in \autoref{ch:results} and are stated here once
rather than repeated: the baseline is pinned to a single configuration and never renegotiated per
comparison (\autoref{sec:setup:baselines}); improvement means Pareto dominance at equal success and
equal constraint satisfaction, not an improvement on one axis (\autoref{sec:setup:metrics}); and
every reported cell names the protocol that produced it, because the three entries do not share one
(\autoref{sec:setup:protocol}).

\section{Tasks and Environments}\label{sec:setup:tasks}

Three entries are evaluated. They are not three benchmarks chosen for coverage; they are one
benchmark and two deliberate increments on top of it, and the chapter is organised by what each
increment adds rather than by environment.

\begin{description}
  \item[Entry~1 --- the state-based manipulation benchmark.] The \emph{avoiding} task of the D3IL
    suite \parencite{jia2024towards}, which is also the benchmark the \ac{DPCC} baseline reports on
    \parencite{romer2025diffusion}. A planar manipulator must reach a goal line while avoiding a
    field of obstacles, with the plan constrained by halfspaces the training data never contained.
    The policy observes state. This entry is the foundation: it is the only one with multi-seed
    data, and it is where the baseline is met on its own ground.
  \item[Entry~2 --- the vision-conditioned manipulation task.] The \emph{aligning} task of the same
    suite, in which a box must be pushed into a target pose, with the policy observing two RGB
    camera streams instead of a state vector. Relative to Entry~1 this adds \emph{two} increments at
    once: three-dimensional control, and perception in place of state.
  \item[Entry~3 --- the aerial task.] A quadrotor embodiment built for this work around the Skydio
    X2 model taken verbatim from \texttt{mujoco\_menagerie}, wrapped in static obstacle scenes.
    The physics are third-party and unmodified; the scenes, the control stack and the demonstration
    data are ours.
    \srcnote{Writing/Auxiliary/Methodology\_Sources/AUX\_uav\_model\_and\_scenes.md}
\end{description}

\subsection*{Why the third entry is a different problem, not a harder one}

The two manipulation entries and the aerial entry differ in a way that a table of task names hides,
and it is the reason \textbf{RQ4} treats embodiment as a separate axis from modality. The
manipulator is \emph{fully actuated} --- one actuator per degree of freedom --- and
\emph{quasi-statically stable}: it holds its configuration under gravity compensation with no
control input. A plan for it needs only to be \emph{kinematically} feasible, that is, a reachable
sequence of setpoints, and one control loop at planner rate suffices.

The quadrotor is \emph{underactuated}: four rotor inputs serve six pose degrees of freedom, and
thrust acts along the body $z$-axis only, so the vehicle must tilt in order to translate. Hover is
an \emph{open-loop unstable} equilibrium requiring continuous closed-loop stabilisation. A plan for
it must be \emph{dynamically} feasible --- second-order, thrust- and attitude-limited --- and the
loop is cascaded and multi-rate, at 33\,Hz for planning against 100\,Hz for physics
(\autoref{sec:method:deployment}). The transfer claimed by Entry~3 is therefore from a fully
actuated, statically stable, kinematically controlled plant to an underactuated, open-loop-unstable,
second-order one. That is the content of the entry, independently of which engine wins on it.

\subsection*{Constraint geometries}

On Entry~1 the feasible set is instantiated as three halfspace geometries of increasing difficulty
--- \emph{top-left-hard}, \emph{top-right-hard} and \emph{both-hard} --- and every result on this
entry is reported per geometry and then aggregated across the three, never pooled over cells. On
Entry~3 the scenes form a difficulty ladder: an unconstrained \emph{empty} scene used as the
raw-generator denominator, a \emph{corridor} of two parallel walls with a 0.9\,m clear channel, an
\emph{s-curve} of two offset corridor segments, and \emph{pillars}, six cylinders of radius
0.12\,m arranged in three columns.
\srcnote{AUX\_uav\_model\_and\_scenes.md \S2; AUX\_constraint\_geometry.md}

Adding scene geometry does not perturb the vehicle dynamics: a tracking task re-run inside the empty
scene reproduced its reference root-mean-square error bit-for-bit, because MuJoCo walls are passive
rigid bodies with contact but no aerodynamic effect. This is what licenses attributing every
cross-scene difference to geometry rather than to physics.

\subsection*{The regime taxonomy}

A single vocabulary is needed for something that recurs across all three entries and that a naive
reading of a results table gets wrong. A scene, geometry or task is called

\begin{description}
  \item[all-pass] when every arm --- including the unconstrained one --- satisfies the constraint
    set. The constraint half of the primary metric then carries no information, the metric collapses
    to plain success, and \textbf{no constrained-control conclusion may be drawn}, however clean the
    numbers look.
  \item[discriminating] when the constraint set separates the arms: some configurations violate it
    and some do not. This is the only regime in which the primary metric is a ranking instrument.
  \item[all-fail] when no arm satisfies the constraint set. The primary metric is floored, ranking
    by it is meaningless, and comparison must fall back to the staged funnel of
    \autoref{sec:setup:metrics}.
\end{description}

The taxonomy is not a post-hoc excuse for weak cells. It is diagnostic, it is measured rather than
asserted --- an all-pass scene is one where the violation counter reads zero on the
\emph{unprojected} arm --- and it is used in three chapters. \autoref{ch:results} states the regime
of every entry before reporting it; \autoref{sec:disc:threats} argues that failing to do so is a
general threat to the validity of single-benchmark engine comparisons, on the evidence of this
work's own results.

\subsection*{Two caveats that belong to the tasks rather than to any result}

\emph{The success criterion on Entry~2 is strict in a dimension no arm controls.} Binary success
there requires a position error within 1.8\,cm \emph{and} a rotation error within
$8.64^\circ$, and box rotation is uncontrolled on every arm evaluated, the baseline included. The
consequence is structural: Entry~2 sits in the all-fail regime, and it does so as a property of the
criterion and the constraint set rather than of any engine's weights.
\srcnote{AUX\_visual\_aligning\_env.md \S6}

\emph{One scene of Entry~3 is constraint-trivial.} On the corridor scene the violation counter reads
zero and the collision-free-completion rate reads one on \emph{every} arm, projected and unprojected
alike, while the same columns report violations in the hundreds on the pillars scene. The metric is
therefore sensitive and the scene really is all-pass. It follows that the corridor scene cannot
exercise the constrained-control problem this thesis is about, and it is not used to rank engines.
\dataref{Gen15 CLOSURE\_20260910 \S1.1}

\section{Datasets}\label{sec:setup:data}

Entries~1 and 2 use the D3IL demonstration sets distributed with the benchmark
\parencite{jia2024towards}, unmodified, so that the comparison against the baseline is a comparison
of engines and not of data. Entry~3 has no public demonstration set and its expert data were
generated for this work; the generation procedure, its acceptance criteria and its coverage are
documented in the apparatus notes.
\srcnote{Writing/Auxiliary/Methodology\_Sources/AUX\_uav\_expert\_data.md}

\hole{State the demonstration counts and the train/validation split for all three entries, read off
the dataset files rather than from a dev log. This is a book-keeping hole, not a blocked one.}

Two properties of the data matter for how \autoref{ch:results} reads. First, the constraint sets on
Entry~1 are \emph{novel at deployment}: the halfspaces are not present in the demonstrations, which
is what makes constraint enforcement a task for the projector rather than something the policy could
have learned. Second, every engine in a given comparison is trained on the same data with the same
horizon and the same action weighting, so a difference between two rows of a table is a difference
in objective or in constraint arm and in nothing else.

\subsection*{The internal comparators}

Beyond the pinned baseline, three comparisons inside the transport family are reported, and each
answers a different question. \emph{Flow matching} \parencite{lipman2023flow} is the engine that
replaces diffusion with deterministic transport and nothing else; it is the control that separates
``transport helps'' from ``the few-step objective helps''. \emph{MeanFlow}
\parencite{geng2025meanflow} adds the average-velocity objective and is the flagship.
\emph{The bootstrapped target} \parencite{zhang2025alphaflow} interpolates between the two, and is
reported as an ablation --- its result is negative, and \autoref{sec:disc:negative} gives it with
its mechanism rather than omitting it.

\textbf{Every table carries the backbone and the parameter count}, and rows on a non-matching
backbone are labelled as confounded rather than presented as a like-for-like result. The
architecture-matched form of every claim in this thesis is the 4.0\,M temporal U-Net against the
4.0\,M temporal U-Net of the baseline; transformer-backbone rows are secondary evidence.

\section{Metrics}\label{sec:setup:metrics}

\subsection*{The primary metric}

The primary quantity is \emph{success and constraint satisfaction}: the fraction of episodes in
which the task goal is reached \emph{and} no constraint violation is executed. It is a conjunction
on purpose. Reporting task success alone rewards a planner that drives through an obstacle, and
reporting violations alone rewards one that refuses to move --- a failure mode this work actually
observes, and labels wherever it occurs.

\subsection*{The cost axes, and what they are not}

Two cost axes are reported and they are not interchangeable. \emph{Control steps per episode}
counts steps of the closed loop and measures plan quality: a shorter path to the goal is a better
plan. \emph{Wall clock per control step} measures compute. Their product, seconds per episode, is
quoted where end-to-end cost is the point.

\guard{Wall-clock figures in this thesis are indicative, not a hardware benchmark.} The reasons are
properties of the machine and are stated with it at the end of this section: the evaluation node is
shared, and the evaluation loop is plausibly CPU-limited rather than accelerator-limited. Where a
compute ratio is quoted, both sides of it were measured on the same node under the same conditions,
which is what makes the ratio meaningful even though the absolute figure is not portable.

\textbf{One quantity is explicitly not a criterion.} The aerial entry logs a per-step budget in
milliseconds derived from its 33\,Hz plan rate. That figure is a data-rate artefact compounded with
cluster latency; it is \emph{never} read in this thesis as a real-time pass/fail criterion, and no
claim anywhere depends on it.

\subsection*{What ``better'' means}

\begin{definition}[Improvement]\label{def:improvement}
Configuration $A$ \emph{improves on} configuration $B$ when $A$ attains success and constraint
satisfaction at least equal to $B$'s and is strictly better on at least one cost axis while being no
worse on the other. Where that does not hold, the pair is a \emph{trade-off} and is reported as one.
\end{definition}

This is Pareto dominance with the quality axis as a gate rather than as a third objective, and it is
strict by design: it makes ``fewer control steps'' worthless if bought with a lower success rate,
which is exactly the trade the low-budget regime tempts one into. The word ``best'' is used only
where \autoref{def:improvement} is satisfied; elsewhere the text says ``non-dominated'' or
``trade-off''.

In figures, the same definition appears as a comparability band: a configuration competes on cost
only if its success-and-constraint score is within 0.05 of the best on the panel, and the frontier
is the non-dominated staircase over those eligible points. Points outside the band are drawn hollow
and are not on any frontier.

\subsection*{The funnel, for the all-fail regime}

In an all-fail regime \autoref{def:improvement} cannot discriminate, because its gate is floored for
every arm. Ranking there uses a three-stage funnel, applied in order and stopping at the first stage
that separates: \emph{(i)} distance to the goal, evaluated on the unguided arm, which measures the
generator without the projector confounding it; \emph{(ii)} constraint violations; \emph{(iii)}
wall clock. Stage~(i) is a plan-quality ranking and is labelled as one; it is not a success claim
and is never written as one.

\subsection*{Statistics, and the resolution of each entry}

The three entries afford different statistics, and conflating them would be the easiest way to
overstate a result.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Entry~1} is the only multi-seed entry. Comparisons are across 5 seeds; the
    across-seed standard error on the primary metric is about 0.07, so a difference below roughly
    0.10 in success and constraint satisfaction is not a real difference and is reported as a tie.
  \item \textbf{Entry~2} enumerates a fixed set of initial contexts rather than sampling random
    initialisations, and resets to each context explicitly. Every arm therefore sees the
    \emph{same} contexts, which makes every comparison \emph{paired}, and licenses exact paired sign
    tests and sign-flip permutation tests over the matched pairs. This is a design decision and a
    strength, not a substitute for seeds: random initialisation would have cost most of that power.
    At the corpus of record's ten contexts, the smallest attainable two-sided sign-test $p$ is
    0.002 and a 7--3 split is $p = 0.34$.
  \item \textbf{Entry~3} is seeds $\times$ rollouts, one scene at a time. The corpus of record is a
    single seed at ten rollouts, so a difference of 0.1 in mean success and constraint satisfaction
    is \emph{one rollout}. Every ordering sentence in \autoref{sec:res:uav} carries that fact.
\end{itemize}

A measured reproducibility floor qualifies Entry~2's minima specifically: repeated evaluation of
projected arms varies by about 0.4\,m in final distance, which is the scale of the quantity being
compared. Minima there are therefore a capability ceiling of a single rollout, not an estimate.

\section{Training and Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:setup:protocol}

\subsection*{Two protocol tiers on Entry~1, and why the difference is itself a result}

Entry~1 is reported at two protocols. \textbf{Tier~1} is the baseline's own published evaluation, 5
seeds $\times$ 2 trials, ten episodes per cell. \textbf{Tier~2} is this work's, 5 seeds $\times$ 20
trials, one hundred episodes per cell. Every claim in this thesis rests on Tier~2; Tier~1 exists so
that the question ``did you evaluate the baseline the way its authors did?'' is answered before it
is asked.

The shift between them was measured, and it is a methodological finding rather than a formality.

Two things follow. First, a score of 1.00 at ten episodes means ``at least 0.90, at a resolution of

published protocol's resolution is coarser than most of the differences the literature reports on
this benchmark. That belongs in a setup chapter rather than a discussion, because it changes what
any table on this benchmark --- including ours --- is capable of showing.

\provisional{The Tier-1 rows for the transport engines are owed. They exist only in pre-2026-08-19
batches produced by an older evaluation binary under mixed configurations, and they cannot be
recovered by subsampling Tier~2, because the corpus stores per-cell aggregates with the trial
dimension already collapsed. A clean Tier~1 is one evaluation at two trials per seed across all
engines on a single binary --- roughly one tenth the wall clock of the tier already paid for.
Until it lands, \autoref{tab:tiers} rests on baseline rows only, and the low-budget baseline cells
in \autoref{sec:res:fewstep} are quoted as a mechanism rather than as a powered comparison.}

\subsection*{Entry~2: contexts, not trials}

Entry~2 has no trial count by construction. Its evaluation enumerates $n_{\text{contexts}}$ initial
box poses and resets to each explicitly, with $n_{\text{trajectories}}$ rollouts per context; more
evaluation there means more contexts, not more trials.
\srcnote{config/visual\_aligning\_eval.yaml:44--45; config/aligning-d3il-visual.py:166}
The corpus of record is one seed at ten contexts with one trajectory each. The benchmark's own
aligning protocol uses sixty contexts, and raising ours to fifty is the highest
power-per-GPU-hour item outstanding on this entry: it multiplies the paired sample fivefold, needs
no retraining, and lands close to the benchmark's own figure.

\subsection*{Entry~3: seeds $\times$ rollouts, one scene at a time}

The corpus of record is seed~6 at ten rollouts per cell, on the corrected scene geometry. Scenes are
evaluated separately and never pooled: they occupy different regimes, and a pooled aerial number
would average an all-pass scene, a discriminating one and an all-fail one into a quantity that
describes none of them.

\subsection*{Aggregation, stated once}

Results on Entry~1 are aggregated \textbf{per geometry first, then across the three geometries},
never as a flat mean over $(\text{seed} \times \text{geometry})$ cells. The two differ whenever a
geometry carries a different number of seeds --- which is exactly the case for the pinned baseline
--- and only the former is comparable across rows with unequal coverage. The unit of comparison is
the cell $(\text{engine} \times \text{constraint arm} \times \text{geometry} \times \text{split})$;
\textbf{results are never aggregated across constraint arms}, and a model-versus-model comparison
uses each model's own best arm, always named.

\subsection*{A residual question about the sampling prior}

\autoref{sec:method:engine} records a discrepancy inherited from the baseline's sampler: it starts
from a prior of half the unit scale and injects half the posterior standard deviation, and the naive
flow-matching engine inherits that halved prior at sampling time while having been trained at unit
scale. The MeanFlow engine uses unit scale at both ends.
\provisional{The flow-matching numbers reported in \autoref{ch:results} were produced by the
shipped sampler, that is, with the inherited prior, and were \emph{not} re-run at matched scale.
The direction of the resulting bias is not established, so no result in this thesis is attributed to
it; what can be said is that it applies to the flow-matching rows only, and that the flagship
MeanFlow rows --- which carry the thesis's principal claim --- are unaffected. Resolving it is one
evaluation of the flow-matching checkpoints at unit prior.}

\subsection*{Compute environment}

All training and evaluation reported in this thesis ran on a single shared GPU node of the
institute's cluster, scheduled with SLURM. The node carries two 16-core AMD EPYC~7282 processors
(32~physical cores, 64~hardware threads) and eight NVIDIA RTX~A5000 accelerators with 24~GB of
device memory each. \textbf{Every job was allocated exactly one GPU}: no result in this thesis comes
from a distributed or multi-GPU run. The full hardware and software inventory, including package
versions, is given in \autoref{app:repro}, \autoref{tab:compute}.
\srcnote{Slurm\_Codes/sbatch/templates/2026\_04\_30\_job\_template.sh; every log header in Slurm\_Codes/logs/}

Two properties of this machine bear directly on how the wall-clock measurements in
\autoref{ch:results} may be read, and both argue for reading them as \emph{indicative} rather than as
a hardware benchmark.

\emph{The node is shared.} It serves the institute's student and staff GPU queues simultaneously,
and a snapshot taken while preparing this section found six of the eight accelerators and 42 of the
64 threads already committed to other users' jobs. What this does \emph{not} affect is the
accelerator itself: the scheduler neither oversubscribes GPUs nor preempts running jobs, so a job
holds its device exclusively for its lifetime. Contention is therefore confined to the CPU, the
memory system and I/O.

\emph{The CPU-to-GPU ratio is low, and this work is CPU-bound.} Thirty-two physical cores serve
eight accelerators, so a fully occupied node offers roughly four physical cores per GPU. Both halves
of the evaluation loop --- the MuJoCo rollout and the per-step projection, which is solved by SLSQP
on the CPU (\autoref{sec:method:proj}) --- run on those cores rather than on the accelerator.
Evaluation throughput on this machine is thus plausibly limited by the CPU side and not by the
RTX~A5000. This holds independently of contention, that is, it would hold on an idle node as well;
it is the more fundamental of the two caveats.

Both observations are recorded here rather than in \autoref{sec:disc:threats} because they concern
the measurement apparatus rather than the validity of a claim. Their consequence for the timing
figures specifically is taken up in \autoref{sec:res:summary}.
