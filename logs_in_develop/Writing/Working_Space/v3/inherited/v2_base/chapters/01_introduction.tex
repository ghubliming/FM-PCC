\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:intro:motivation}

A robot that plans with a generative model buys expressiveness with computation. Diffusion
planners \parencite{janner2022planning,chi2023diffusion} represent multi-modal demonstration data
\parencite{jia2024towards} well and, when paired with a constrained projection step
\parencite{romer2025diffusion}, can respect dynamics and safety at deployment. The price is the sampling
loop: producing one plan costs tens to hundreds of network evaluations, and in a
receding-horizon controller that cost is paid again at every timestep.

The section argues that this cost is not incidental but \emph{structural}, and that the
structure is what makes it addressable. In a diffusion model the number of steps is a
property of the trained noise schedule \parencite{ho2020denoising}; reducing it after training
degrades the sample, and recovering quality needs a different sampler
\parencite{song2021denoising}. In a deterministic transport model \parencite{lipman2023flow} the same
quantity is a property of the \ac{ODE} solver, and is chosen at inference. The motivation of this thesis is therefore not ``make the planner
faster'' but ``move the step budget from training time to inference time, and then find out
what it costs.''

A second motivation follows from the first. Once the budget is cheap, the constraint
machinery --- not the generative model --- becomes the dominant cost, and the question of
\emph{how} constraints are enforced stops being a footnote.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:intro:problem}

The object of study is a closed-loop controller in which, at each timestep, a conditional
generative model proposes a trajectory over a fixed horizon, a constraint mechanism makes
that trajectory admissible, and the first action is executed. The thesis holds this
architecture fixed and varies exactly two things: the generative engine, and the
constraint mechanism.

Stating it this way exposes the methodological demand the rest of the thesis has to meet.
A claim that one engine beats another is only meaningful if the backbone, parameter count,
data, normalisation, horizon, seeds, projector and harness are all held constant. A claim
that one constraint arm beats another is only meaningful if both arms see the same feasible
set, the same solver, the same activation threshold and the same candidate budget. Most of
the engineering behind this thesis exists to make those two sentences true, and the section
says so plainly rather than leaving it to the reader to infer.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:intro:rqs}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 --- Engine.] Under an identical backbone, projector, dataset and harness, does
    replacing the diffusion engine with deterministic ODE transport improve the
    quality--cost frontier of constrained predictive control, and does the improvement
    order the engine family?
  \item[RQ2 --- Budget.] How far can the \ac{NFE} be reduced before
    plan quality degrades, and what mechanism explains why the flow family can follow that
    ladder down while diffusion cannot?
  \item[RQ3 --- Constraints.] Given one fixed feasible set, which constraint-enforcement
    arm --- projection of the current \emph{iterate}, or projection of the predicted clean
    \emph{endpoint} inside the sampler --- should be used, and under what condition is the
    comparison even well-posed?
  \item[RQ4 --- Transfer.] Do the answers to RQ1--RQ3 survive a change of observation
    modality (state $\rightarrow$ vision) and a change of embodiment (manipulator
    $\rightarrow$ aerial), or are they properties of one benchmark?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro:contributions}

The section lists six contributions and is explicit that two of them are negative or
methodological rather than performance claims.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An architecture-matched engine substitution.} Deterministic transport
    engines \parencite{lipman2023flow,geng2025meanflow} are placed inside the \ac{DPCC} control stack
    \parencite{romer2025diffusion} with the backbone, parameter count and constraint machinery held
    fixed, so that the engine is the only moving part. The
    resulting comparison is reported under Pareto dominance rather than a single metric.
  \item \textbf{A correctness condition for evaluating in-loop guided samplers.} A guided
    sampler that is only active near the end of its own integration performs no guidance at
    all when the step budget is small: the sole active step is terminal, and what executes is
    sample-then-project. The condition is a computable function of the step budget and the
    activation threshold, and it disqualifies a class of comparisons that otherwise look
    valid. This thesis derives it, instruments it, and enforces it in every table.
  \item \textbf{A measured regime split between constraint arms, with a mechanism.} The two
    arms are shown to swap places as a function of the same quantity, and the swap is
    explained by \emph{which point} each arm hands to an identical projection operator, not by
    solver differences.
  \item \textbf{Removal of the candidate-selection machinery.} The projector's selection
    rules are shown not to earn their computation, and the system ships with the redundancy
    removed rather than retained ``just in case''.
  \item \textbf{Two constructed benchmark environments} --- a vision-conditioned
    manipulation task and an aerial task with four scenes, expert data, constraint geometry
    and a low-level controller --- together with their construction documented as
    methodology.
  \item \textbf{Retained negative results.} An additional engine variant that does not
    improve on its predecessor is reported as a clean negative rather than omitted, and a
    defect in this work's own benchmark geometry, found by its own instrumentation, is
    reported with the correction and its limits.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Terminology}\label{sec:intro:scope}

A short section, placed before the background because the rest of the thesis is unreadable
without it. It fixes the vocabulary that recurs in every chapter:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Step budget $K$ / NFE.} The number of generative network evaluations spent
    producing one plan. For the transport family this is a solver setting; for diffusion it
    is fixed by training. \emph{$K$ is never the planner's candidate fan} --- a distinct
    quantity, also swept, and a persistent source of confusion.
  \item \textbf{Arms.} Arm~A is the \emph{unguided} plan (no constraint enforcement). Arm~B is
    \emph{iterate projection}: the same projection applied to the sampler's current iterate. Arm~C
    is \emph{endpoint projection}: the same projection applied inside the sampler to the predicted
    clean endpoint, with a damped pull-back. The two names differ by one adjective because the two
    algorithms differ by one thing --- the point handed to an identical operator.
  \item \textbf{Genuine steps.} The number of sampler steps at which arm~C performs
    non-terminal guided work; formalised in Chapter~\autoref{ch:method}. Zero genuine steps
    means arm~C is not arm~C.
  \item \textbf{Tightened geometry.} A stricter constraint margin applied to an otherwise
    identical feasible set; an experimental factor, not a formatting suffix.
  \item \textbf{``Better''.} Pareto dominance --- equal success \emph{and} equal constraint
    satisfaction, with strictly fewer evaluations \emph{and} lower wall-clock. Anything else
    is named a trade-off.
\end{itemize}
